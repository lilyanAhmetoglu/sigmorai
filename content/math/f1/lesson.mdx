## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Compute the length of a vector and the dot product of two vectors by hand.
- Compute cosine similarity and use it to rank text chunks for a question.
- Say when a plain dot product gives the same ranking as cosine similarity.

## The problem

You build a search over your team's notes. Every note is cut into chunks, and every chunk becomes a list of numbers. A question also becomes a list of numbers. The search must pick the chunk that is closest to the question.

But what does "closest" mean for lists of numbers? One simple score puts a long chunk with big numbers at the top, even when it is not about the question. This lesson gives you the three tools behind a good ranking: the vector, the dot product and cosine similarity. You only need adding, multiplying and one square root.

## The idea

### A vector is a list of numbers

A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers. `(3, 4)` is a vector with 2 numbers. Each number sits at one position. A position is also called a **dimension**.

An **embedding** is a vector made from a text. The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings) say: "An embedding is a vector (list) of floating point numbers." (Floating point numbers are numbers with decimals, like 0.37.) Real embeddings are long:

- The OpenAI docs: "By default, the length of the embedding vector is 1536 for text-embedding-3-small".
- The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings) list Voyage AI models such as `voyage-4` with "1024 (default)" as the embedding dimension.

The math is the same for 1024 numbers as for 3, so this lesson uses short vectors you can check by hand.

We write `a_i` for the number at position i of vector `a`. For `a = (3, 4)`, `a_1 = 3` and `a_2 = 4`.

### Length

The **length** of a vector `a` is written `||a||` (two bars on each side). Square every number, add the squares, then take the square root `√`. (A square root undoes a square: `√25 = 5`, because `5 × 5 = 25`.)

Example: `||(3, 4)|| = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5`.

### The dot product

The **dot product** multiplies the two numbers at each position, then adds up the results. Both vectors must have the same number of positions.

`a·b = Σ a_i b_i`: the sum of a-i times b-i, over every position i.

The sign `Σ` (the Greek letter sigma) means: add up all of these.

Example with `a = (1, 2, 3)` and `b = (4, 5, 6)`:

- position 1: `1 × 4 = 4`
- position 2: `2 × 5 = 10`
- position 3: `3 × 6 = 18`
- dot product: `4 + 10 + 18 = 32`

The result is one number, not a vector. Negative numbers keep their sign. Example: `(3, −1)·(2, 4) = 3×2 + (−1)×4 = 6 − 4 = 2`. A negative product pulls the total down.

The dot product is large when both vectors have big numbers in the same positions. So it also grows when a vector just gets longer.

### Cosine similarity

**Cosine similarity** divides the dot product by the two lengths:

`cos(a, b) = a·b / (||a|| ||b||)`

("cos" is short for cosine. It comes from the angle between the two arrows.) Dividing by the lengths takes length out of the score, so only the **direction** is left. For any two vectors that are not all zeros, the result is between −1 and 1:

- `1`: the same direction.
- `0`: at a right angle. Example: `(1, 0)` and `(0, 1)`.
- `−1`: opposite directions. Example: `(1, 0)` and `(−1, 0)`.

If one vector is the other one times a positive number, they point the same way. Their cosine is 1, however long they are. Example with `(1, 2)` and `(2, 4)`:

- dot product: `1×2 + 2×4 = 10`
- lengths: `√5` and `√20`, and `√5 × √20 = √100 = 10`
- cosine: `10 / 10 = 1`

### How search uses the score

The OpenAI docs: "The distance between two vectors measures their relatedness." On the choice of score, they say: "We recommend cosine similarity."

A search ranks chunks in three steps:

```text
1. embed      question  → vector q
2. score      cos(q, chunk) for every chunk vector
3. return     the chunks with the highest scores
```

### Length 1 makes it cheaper

A vector with length 1 is called **normalized**. To normalize a vector, divide every number by its length. `(3, 4)` has length 5, so it becomes `(0.6, 0.8)`. Check: `√(0.36 + 0.64) = √1 = 1`.

If both vectors have length 1, the bottom of the cosine formula is `1 × 1 = 1`. So cosine similarity is just the dot product. Two vendors say this about their own embeddings:

- The OpenAI docs: "OpenAI embeddings are normalized to length 1". So cosine similarity "can be computed slightly faster using just a dot product".
- The Claude docs, for Voyage AI: "Cosine similarity is equivalent to dot-product similarity, while the latter can be computed more quickly." Their search example ranks documents with `np.dot(doc_embds, query_embd)`.

This only holds while the length is exactly 1. The Claude docs say that to shorten stored vectors, you "truncate them by keeping the leading subset of dimensions, then normalize them again".

A small comparison: cosine similarity is like comparing two compass needles. It asks where they point, not how big they are.

## Worked example

Our example: a question and three note chunks, as 3-number vectors.

```text
question q = (1, 2, 2)
chunk A    = (2, 1, 2)
chunk B    = (6, 2, 3)
chunk C    = (0, 3, 4)
```

The task: which chunk should the search return first?

### Step 1: the lengths

- `||q|| = √(1 + 4 + 4) = √9 = 3`
- `||A|| = √(4 + 1 + 4) = √9 = 3`
- `||B|| = √(36 + 4 + 9) = √49 = 7`
- `||C|| = √(0 + 9 + 16) = √25 = 5`

### Step 2: the dot products

- `q·A = 1×2 + 2×1 + 2×2 = 2 + 2 + 4 = 8`
- `q·B = 1×6 + 2×2 + 2×3 = 6 + 4 + 6 = 16`
- `q·C = 1×0 + 2×3 + 2×4 = 0 + 6 + 8 = 14`

Before: if you rank by the dot product alone, the order is **B (16), C (14), A (8)**. B comes first.

### Step 3: the cosines

| Chunk | Dot product | Lengths      | Cosine            |
| ----- | ----------- | ------------ | ----------------- |
| A     | 8           | `3 × 3 = 9`  | `8 / 9 ≈ 0.889`   |
| B     | 16          | `3 × 7 = 21` | `16 / 21 ≈ 0.762` |
| C     | 14          | `3 × 5 = 15` | `14 / 15 ≈ 0.933` |

After: by cosine, the order is **C (0.933), A (0.889), B (0.762)**. C comes first.

### Step 4: the hard case

**The hard case is chunk B.** It has the biggest dot product, but the lowest cosine. B is the longest vector (length 7). Its big numbers push the dot product up, but its direction is the furthest from q. The dot product rewarded size. Cosine removed size, and B dropped from first to last.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Fair to every chunk.** A long chunk can't win only because its numbers are big.
- **Easy to read.** Every score sits between −1 and 1, so you can compare scores across questions.

## Common mistakes

1. **Adding the numbers to get the length.** `3 + 4 = 7` is not `||(3, 4)||`. Fix: square each number, add the squares, then take one square root: 5.
2. **Mixing vectors of different sizes.** A 1024-number vector and a 1536-number vector have no position-by-position match. Fix: compare only vectors with the same number of positions, made by the same embedding model.
3. **Cutting stored vectors shorter and keeping the dot product.** Our example: `(0.6, 0, 0.8)` has length 1, but its first two numbers `(0.6, 0)` have length 0.6. Fix: normalize again after you cut, as the Claude docs say.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Our example: a question `q = (0, 3, 4)` and two chunks, `P = (2, 3, 6)` and `R = (2, 1, 2)`.

1. Find the length of each vector.
2. Find both dot products and both cosines.
3. Do the dot-product ranking and the cosine ranking agree here?

### Answer

1. `||q|| = √(0 + 9 + 16) = 5`. `||P|| = √(4 + 9 + 36) = √49 = 7`. `||R|| = √(4 + 1 + 4) = 3`.
2. Dot products: `q·P = 0 + 9 + 24 = 33` and `q·R = 0 + 3 + 8 = 11`. Cosines: `33 / (5 × 7) = 33 / 35 ≈ 0.943` and `11 / (5 × 3) = 11 / 15 ≈ 0.733`.
3. Yes. P comes first in both. P is longer than R, but it also points closer to q. The two rankings only split when a longer vector points further away, as chunk B did in the worked example. Cosine is the safe choice, because you don't know in advance which case you have.

## Recap

- A vector is a list of numbers. Its length is `√(sum of the squares)`, and an embedding is a long vector.
- The dot product multiplies the numbers at each position and adds them up. It grows with size, so a long vector can win unfairly.
- Cosine similarity divides by both lengths, so only direction counts. For length-1 vectors it equals the dot product, which is faster to compute.
