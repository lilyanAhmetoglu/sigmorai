## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Read a model's next-token output as a probability distribution, and turn logprobs back into probabilities.
- Combine probabilities: add them for _this or that_, multiply them for _this, then that_.
- Say which tokens greedy decoding, `topK` and `topP` let the model pick.

## The problem

Your app asks a model to label each support ticket with one word, like ` refund` or ` bug`. The API can also return logprobs: a number for each likely token. You want a simple rule: accept the label alone when the model is sure, and send the ticket to a person when it is not.

The numbers look strange. They are negative, and the same word shows up twice with a capital letter. To write a safe rule, you need to read these numbers as probabilities. This lesson shows how.

## The idea

### Probability is a number from 0 to 1

A **probability** says how likely an **event** is. An event is something that can happen, like _roll a 6_. 0 means it never happens. 1 means it always happens. 0.25 means about 1 time in 4.

Example: a fair 6-sided die. Each **outcome** (one possible result) is `1 / 6 ≈ 0.167`.

### A distribution covers every outcome

A **distribution** gives each possible outcome a probability. All of them add up to exactly 1, because one outcome must happen. For the die: `6 × 1/6 = 1`.

So the chance that an event does **not** happen is 1 minus its chance. The die does not show a 6: `1 − 1/6 = 5/6 ≈ 0.833`. The same rule finds a missing part of a distribution: subtract the parts you know from 1.

### Or: add

When two outcomes can't both happen, the chance of _one or the other_ is their sum. The die shows a 1 or a 2: `1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 ≈ 0.333`.

### Next-token prediction

A **token** is a small piece of text, like ` blue` or `.`. The model's **vocabulary** is the list of every token it knows. The [Claude glossary](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary) says that language models "are pretrained to predict the next word, given the previous context of text in the document."

At each step, the model's output is a distribution over its whole vocabulary. Every token gets a probability, even very small ones. Our made-up example for the text `The sky is`:

| Next token  | Probability |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| ` blue`     | 0.60        |
| ` clear`    | 0.20        |
| ` dark`     | 0.15        |
| every other | 0.05        |

The rows add up to `0.60 + 0.20 + 0.15 + 0.05 = 1`.

### Conditional probability

These numbers depend on the text before. That is a **conditional probability**, written with a bar `|` that you read as "given":

`P(x_t | x_<t)`: the probability of token x at step t, given all the tokens before step t.

`x_t` is the token at step t. `x_<t` means every token before step t. Change the text before, and the distribution changes.

### Then: multiply

The chance of two tokens in a row is the chance of the first, times the chance of the second given the first. Our example: ` blue` has 0.60, and `.` then has 0.50. The pair has `0.60 × 0.50 = 0.30`.

### Logprobs

Long texts multiply many small numbers. So APIs often return **logprobs** instead. The [OpenAI cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/using_logprobs) says "a logprob is log(p)", where p is the probability of a token "based on the previous tokens in the context".

`ln` (also written `log` here) is the natural logarithm: the `ln` button on a calculator. `e ≈ 2.718` is a fixed number, and `e^x` means e to the power (exponent) x. `e^x` undoes `ln`: `e^(ln p) = p`. So `e^(−0.105) ≈ 0.90`.

Three facts from the OpenAI cookbook:

- "Logprob can be any negative number or 0.0."
- "0.0 corresponds to 100% probability."
- "Higher log probabilities suggest a higher likelihood of the token in that context."

Logs turn multiplying into adding. For the pair above: `ln 0.60 ≈ −0.511` and `ln 0.50 ≈ −0.693`. The sum is `−1.204`, and `e^(−1.204) ≈ 0.30`, the same answer. The cookbook says you sum the logprobs, and "Exponentiating that sum gives the joint probability." (Joint probability is the chance of all the tokens together.)

### Picking a token

The model gives a distribution. Something still has to pick one token from it.

- **Greedy decoding:** always take the most probable token.
- **Sampling:** pick at random, with the probabilities as weights. Over 100 picks from the sky table, ` blue` comes up about 60 times.

The [Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/prompting-strategies) describe two filters that run before the pick:

- `topK`: "A topK of 1 means the selected token is the most probable among all the tokens in the model's vocabulary (also called greedy decoding)". A `topK` of 3 keeps the 3 most probable tokens.
- `topP`: "Tokens are selected from the most to least probable until the sum of their probabilities equals the topP value."

Our `topP` example: four tokens with 0.5, 0.3, 0.1 and 0.1, and `topP` 0.8. Take the biggest first: 0.5. Add the next: `0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8`. The sum has reached 0.8, so the last two tokens are out. The pick is then made between the first two.

A third setting, temperature, changes how random the pick is between the tokens that stay.

A small comparison: a distribution is a pie. Every token has a slice, and the slices always fill the whole pie.

## Worked example

Our example: the ticket labeller. The API returns logprobs for the first token of the label. Our rule: accept the label alone if its probability is at least 0.9. Otherwise a person reviews the ticket.

### Step 1: an easy ticket

```text
token      logprob
 refund    -0.051
```

Turn the logprob into a probability: `e^(−0.051) ≈ 0.950`. 0.950 is at least 0.9, so the app accepts ` refund`.

### Step 2: the hard case

```text
token      logprob
 refund    -0.511
 Refund    -1.139
 bug       -2.996
```

Turn each one back:

- ` refund`: `e^(−0.511) ≈ 0.600`
- ` Refund`: `e^(−1.139) ≈ 0.320`
- ` bug`: `e^(−2.996) ≈ 0.050`

Before: the top token is ` refund` at 0.600. That is under 0.9, so the first version of the rule sends this ticket to a person.

**This is the hard case.** ` refund` and ` Refund` are two different tokens, but they are the same label for our app. The model can only produce one of them at this step, so they can't both happen. So the two chances add: `0.600 + 0.320 = 0.920`.

After: the label "refund" has probability 0.920. That is at least 0.9, so the app accepts it.

### Step 3: the fixed rule

```text
1. turn every logprob into a probability with e^x
2. group tokens that mean the same label (case, leading space)
3. add the probabilities inside each group
4. accept the best group if its total is at least 0.9
```

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Fewer wasted reviews.** The model was sure about the label. It only spread its probability over two spellings.
- **Honest numbers.** Every check uses real probabilities, not raw logprobs.
- **Same rule for every label.** Step 2 of the rule also catches other spellings, like a missing leading space.

## Common mistakes

1. **Reading a logprob as a percent.** `−0.105` is not minus 10%. Fix: take `e^x` first. `e^(−0.105) ≈ 0.90`.
2. **Thinking a bigger minus number is more likely.** `−2.303` looks big, but it is further from 0. Fix: closer to 0 means more likely. `e^(−2.303) ≈ 0.10`.
3. **Splitting one answer across spellings.** ` Yes` and ` yes` each look unsure on their own. Fix: group tokens that mean the same thing, then add.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Our example: the text so far is `The meeting is on`. Here is the model's table for the next token.

| Next token  | Probability |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| ` Monday`   | 0.50        |
| ` Friday`   | 0.30        |
| ` the`      | 0.15        |
| every other | 0.05        |

1. You sample 200 times. About how many times do you get ` Monday`, ` Friday` and ` the`?
2. Which token does greedy decoding pick?
3. With `topK` set to 2, which tokens can still be picked?

### Answer

1. Multiply each probability by 200. ` Monday`: `0.50 × 200 = 100`. ` Friday`: `0.30 × 200 = 60`. ` the`: `0.15 × 200 = 30`. These are averages, so a real run will be close, not exact.
2. ` Monday`, because it has the biggest probability. Greedy decoding picks it every time.
3. ` Monday` and ` Friday`. `topK` keeps only the 2 most probable tokens, so ` the` and all the rest are out.

## Recap

- A distribution gives each outcome a probability, and they add up to 1. A model gives one over its whole vocabulary at every step.
- _Or_ adds (when both can't happen). _Then_ multiplies. Logprobs turn that multiplying into adding, and `e^x` turns a logprob back into a probability.
- Greedy decoding takes the top token. `topK` keeps a fixed number of tokens, and `topP` keeps tokens until their sum reaches its value.
