A language model writes one token at a time, and each step is a choice made with probabilities. This floor covers the probability you need to read that process: events, distributions, conditional probability, and sampling.

## Probability is a number from 0 to 1

A probability says how likely an event is. 0 means it never happens. 1 means it always happens. 0.25 means it happens 1 time in 4 on average.

Example: a fair 6-sided die. The event "roll a 6" has probability `1 ⁄ 6 ≈ 0.167`. The event "roll an even number" has 3 good outcomes (2, 4, 6), so its probability is `3 ⁄ 6 = 0.5`.

## A distribution covers every outcome

A distribution gives a probability to every possible outcome. The probabilities of all outcomes add up to exactly 1, because one of them must happen.

For the die: six outcomes, each `1 ⁄ 6`, and `6 × 1 ⁄ 6 = 1`.

If you know all outcomes but one, you can find the last one: subtract the others from 1.

## Next-token prediction

The Claude glossary describes pretraining this way: language models "are pretrained to predict the next word, given the previous context of text in the document."

The model's output at each step is a distribution over its vocabulary: one probability for every token it knows. Here is a made-up example for the text `The sky is`:

| Next token  | Probability |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| ` blue`     | 0.60        |
| ` clear`    | 0.20        |
| ` dark`     | 0.15        |
| every other | 0.05        |

The four rows add up to `0.60 + 0.20 + 0.15 + 0.05 = 1`. Real numbers depend on the model and the text before.

## Conditional probability

These probabilities depend on the text that came before. That is a conditional probability, written with a bar:

`P(xₜ | x₍ₜ₋₁₎ … x₁)`: the probability of token x at step t, given all the tokens before step t.

Change the text before, and the distribution changes. After `The sky at midnight is`, the token ` dark` should get a much bigger share than after `The sky is`.

The OpenAI cookbook defines the same idea. A logprob is `log(p)`, where p is the "probability of a token occurring at a specific position based on the previous tokens in the context."

## Many steps: multiply

To get the probability of two tokens in a row, multiply the probability of the first by the probability of the second, given the first.

Example: ` blue` has 0.60. Say `.` then has 0.50, given `The sky is blue`. The pair has probability `0.60 × 0.50 = 0.30`.

Long texts multiply many small numbers. The OpenAI API can return log probabilities (logprobs). With logs, multiplying becomes adding.

`ln` is the natural logarithm: the ln button on a calculator. `e^x` undoes it: `e^(ln p) = p`. So you can take the ln of each step, add, and turn the sum back into a probability:

- `ln 0.60 ≈ −0.511`
- `ln 0.50 ≈ −0.693`
- sum: `−1.204`, and `e^(−1.204) ≈ 0.30`, the same answer

The OpenAI cookbook says the same: you add the logprobs of the tokens, and "Exponentiating that sum gives the joint probability." Exponentiating means taking e to the power of the sum. It also notes that a higher logprob means a more likely token, and a logprob of `0.0` corresponds to 100% probability. Every logprob is 0 or below, because no probability is above 1.

## Sampling: picking from the distribution

The model gives a distribution. Something still has to pick one token from it. Two simple ways:

- **Greedy:** always take the most likely token. In the table, that is always ` blue`.
- **Sampling:** pick at random, with the probabilities as weights. Over 100 samples, ` blue` comes up about 60 times and ` clear` about 20 times.

The Gemini docs name the greedy case: a `topK` of 1 means the next token is the most probable one in the vocabulary, "also called greedy decoding".

Some settings remove unlikely tokens before the pick. The Gemini docs explain `topP` with an example: "if tokens A, B, and C have a probability of 0.3, 0.2, and 0.1 and the `topP` value is 0.5, then the model will select either A or B as the next token by using the temperature and exclude C as a candidate." A and B already add up to 0.5, so C is cut.

## Temperature, in one picture

Temperature changes how random the pick is. The Claude glossary: "Lower temperatures result in more conservative and deterministic outputs that stick to the most probable phrasing and answers." Deterministic means the same output every time. Higher temperatures lead to "more creative and diverse outputs".

Think of it as sharpening or flattening the table. Low temperature makes ` blue` win almost every time. High temperature gives ` clear` and ` dark` more chances. Floor 5 shows the exact formula.

Two warnings from the docs:

- **Claude:** "Even with temperature set to 0, the results will not be fully deterministic and identical inputs may produce different outputs across API calls."
- **Gemini:** for Gemini 3.x models, Google strongly recommends keeping these parameters at their default values, because changing them "can cause unexpected behavior, such as looping or degraded performance".

On paper, greedy decoding always picks the same token. A served API can still vary for other reasons, so don't count on identical outputs.

## Key points

- A probability is a number from 0 to 1. A distribution gives one to every outcome, and they add up to 1.
- At each step, the model gives a distribution over the next token, conditioned on the tokens before.
- The probability of a sequence is the product of its steps. Logprobs turn that product into a sum.
- Greedy decoding always takes the top token. Sampling picks with the probabilities as weights.
- Temperature controls how random the pick is. Check your vendor's docs before you change it.
