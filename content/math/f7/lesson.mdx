## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Compute the cross-entropy loss for one token, and the mean loss for a whole text.
- Explain why the full formula `−Σ y log ŷ` keeps only one term in next-token prediction.
- Turn a mean loss into a perplexity, and read what it says.

## The problem

Training lowers a **loss**: one number for how wrong the model is. Lower is better. But a language model has a special job. At every step it gives a probability to every token, and then the real next token is shown.

So which number tells a good guess from a bad one? You also see loss numbers in training reports and in model comparisons. This lesson builds the loss that language models use, cross-entropy, from probabilities and logs, with numbers you can check by hand.

## The idea

### What the loss must measure

The [Claude glossary](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary) says language models "are pretrained to predict the next word, given the previous context of text in the document." So the loss asks one question: how much probability did the model give to the token that really came next?

### Logs, in two lines

`ln` is the natural logarithm, the `ln` button on a calculator. For a probability p between 0 and 1, `ln p` is 0 or below: `ln 1 = 0`, and the smaller p is, the more negative `ln p` gets. `e ≈ 2.718` is a fixed number (`≈` means "about"), and `e^x` (e to the power x) undoes `ln`.

### The loss for one token

The **cross-entropy** loss for one prediction is minus the ln of p, where p is the probability the model put on the correct token:

`loss = −ln p(right token)`

The minus sign turns the negative log into a positive loss. Our made-up example for the text `The sky is`: ` blue` 0.60, ` clear` 0.20, ` dark` 0.15.

| The real next token was | p    | Loss `−ln p` |
| ----------------------- | ---- | ------------ |
| ` blue`                 | 0.60 | 0.511        |
| ` clear`                | 0.20 | 1.609        |
| ` dark`                 | 0.15 | 1.897        |

The smaller p is, the bigger the loss. Two edges:

- `p = 1` gives `−ln 1 = 0`. A sure, right answer costs nothing.
- A small p costs a lot. `p = 0.01` gives `−ln 0.01 ≈ 4.605`. As p gets close to 0, the loss grows without limit.

### The full formula

The general form adds up a term for every possible answer:

`−Σ y log ŷ`: cross-entropy is minus the sum, over every class, of the true probability y times the log of the predicted probability y-hat.

- `Σ` (sigma) means: add up one term per class.
- A **class** is one possible answer. Here, one token in the vocabulary.
- `ŷ` (y-hat) is the probability the model predicted for that token.
- `y` is the truth: 1 for the token that really came next, 0 for every other token.
- `log` here is the same natural log, `ln`.

Any term with `y = 0` is `0 × log ŷ = 0`, so it drops out. Only the real token's term is left. If the real token was ` clear`:

`−(0 × ln 0.60 + 1 × ln 0.20 + 0 × ln 0.15 + …) = −ln 0.20 ≈ 1.609`

That is the number in the table. The full formula and the one-token rule give the same result.

### A whole text: take the mean

A text has many tokens, so you take the mean (the average) of the losses. Our example: the model gave the right tokens of a 3-token text the probabilities 0.6, 0.5 and 0.9.

- losses: `−ln 0.6 ≈ 0.511`, `−ln 0.5 ≈ 0.693`, `−ln 0.9 ≈ 0.105`
- sum: `1.309`
- mean: `1.309 / 3 ≈ 0.436`

A low mean loss means the model gave the real text a high probability. So, in math terms, predicting the next word well and having a low mean cross-entropy are the same goal.

### Perplexity

The [OpenAI cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/using_logprobs) gives a second score: "Perplexity can be calculated by exponentiating the negative of the average of the logprobs." A **logprob** is `ln p`. So the negative of their average is the mean cross-entropy, and perplexity is `e^(mean loss)`.

For the 3-token example: `e^0.436 ≈ 1.547`. The cookbook adds: "Generally, a higher perplexity indicates a more uncertain result, and a lower perplexity indicates a more confident result."

One way to read it: if a model spreads its probability evenly over k tokens, each gets `1/k`, the loss is `−ln(1/k) = ln k`, and the perplexity is exactly k. Our example with 3 equal tokens: loss `ln 3 ≈ 1.099`, perplexity 3. So perplexity is like the number of equal options the model was choosing between. A perplexity of 1 means sure and right every time.

### Loss in a real training job

When you fine-tune a model, the job reports loss so you can watch training. In the [OpenAI fine-tuning docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning), each checkpoint (a saved copy of the model partway through training) has a `metrics` object, and the example shows `"full_valid_loss": 0.134` next to `"full_valid_mean_token_accuracy": 0.874`. That page does not give the formula behind `full_valid_loss`. So compare runs with each other, and don't assume it is exactly the formula above.

A small comparison: cross-entropy is a surprise meter. A token the model expected costs little. A token it thought was nearly impossible costs a lot.

## Worked example

Our example: you check how well a model predicts a short support reply. Here are the probabilities it gave to the 4 real tokens.

```text
token        p      loss −ln p
 Thanks      0.80   0.223
 for         0.50   0.693
 using       0.90   0.105
 Zorblex     0.02   3.912
```

` Zorblex` is a made-up product name that the model has rarely seen.

### Step 1: the mean loss (before)

- sum: `0.223 + 0.693 + 0.105 + 3.912 ≈ 4.93`
- mean: `4.93 / 4 ≈ 1.23`

Looking only at the mean, the model seems weak on this whole text.

### Step 2: the hard case

**This is the hard case.** Look at the losses one by one. Three tokens are cheap. One token, ` Zorblex`, costs 3.912 alone. That is about 79% of the sum (`3.912 / 4.93 ≈ 0.79`).

Without it, the mean of the other three is `(0.223 + 0.693 + 0.105) / 3 ≈ 1.02 / 3 ≈ 0.34`. The model is good at the common words. It is surprised by one new name. A small p is punished hard, so one surprise can move the whole mean.

### Step 3: the decision (after)

The problem is the product name, not the whole reply. Our example: suppose new training data lifts ` Zorblex` from 0.02 to 0.4. Its loss falls to `−ln 0.4 ≈ 0.916`, and the mean becomes `(1.02 + 0.916) / 4 ≈ 0.484`.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Find the cause.** Per-token losses show which token drives the mean.
- **Fix the right thing.** You work on the product name, not on every word.
- **Measure the fix.** The same mean loss, on the same text, shows the gain.

## Common mistakes

1. **Comparing losses with different logs.** Some tools use `log₂` (base 2). Our example: for `p = 0.5`, `−log₂ 0.5 = 1`, but `−ln 0.5 ≈ 0.693`. Fix: compare only losses that use the same log.
2. **Comparing summed losses of texts with different lengths.** Our example: 100 tokens at 0.3 each sum to 30, and 10 tokens at 0.9 each sum to 9. The long text looks worse, but each of its tokens is cheaper. Fix: compare the mean loss per token.
3. **Treating a training report's loss as this exact formula.** The OpenAI fine-tuning page does not define `full_valid_loss`. Fix: compare runs from the same job type, side by side.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Our example: a model spreads its probability evenly over 8 tokens, so each one gets `1/8`.

1. What is the loss when the real token is one of the 8? (`ln 8 ≈ 2.079`)
2. What is the perplexity?
3. Some tools use log base 2. What is the loss from part 1 in base 2? (`log₂ 8 = 3`)

### Answer

1. `−ln(1/8) = ln 8 ≈ 2.079`.
2. `e^2.079 ≈ 8`. The model was choosing between 8 equal options, and the perplexity says exactly that.
3. `−log₂(1/8) = log₂ 8 = 3`. Same model, same guess, a different number. Only compare losses that use the same log.

## Recap

- Cross-entropy for one token is `−ln p(right token)`. It is 0 for a sure, right answer and grows fast as p gets small.
- In `−Σ y log ŷ`, y is 0 for every wrong token, so only the real token's term is left. For a text, take the mean loss per token.
- Perplexity is `e^(mean loss)`, like the number of equal options the model was choosing between. Lower means more confident.
