## Why it matters

You have credentials, labels, limits and an incident plan. Now someone asks: "Can we launch?" That is a decision, and it needs a clear yes or no from a named person.

This lesson joins the realm into one gate. It also adds three things this realm has not covered yet: human review, test results that tell the truth, and files that disappear.

## A go or no-go gate

The gate below is our own tool. Each line has an owner, and each line is a yes or a no. One "no" on a blocking line means no launch.

| Line       | Question                                                 | From |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ---- |
| Provenance | Is the master kept, and is the export path tested?       | Ch 1 |
| Signals    | Do we promise only what a watermark can keep?            | Ch 2 |
| Labels     | Is the label decided for each asset and each platform?   | Ch 3 |
| Limits     | Does the sum fit, with a ramp, a staging test, an alert? | Ch 4 |
| Incidents  | Is the one-page plan filled in, with names?              | Ch 5 |
| Review     | Has a person looked at each asset?                       | Here |

## A person looks at the output

OpenAI says: "Wherever possible, we recommend having a human review outputs before they are used in practice." It adds that humans should "have access to any information needed to verify the outputs". Its example is a summary of notes. The reviewer needs the original notes.

For media, the reviewer needs the brief, the master file and the rules (the rubric). A reviewer who sees only the final image cannot tell if a logo is off.

Google's guidance agrees. It says tasks "that have greater human oversight (e.g., generating short-form content that will be reviewed by a human)" often pose a lower risk. Anthropic's agent post says, about its coding agent, that "human review remains crucial for ensuring solutions align with broader system requirements". The wording differs, but the lesson is the same: automated checks do not replace a person.

## Set the bar before you test

Google gives clear advice on testing:

- **Decide the pass level first.** "It's good practice to think about the minimum acceptable levels of safety metrics before testing", so that you can evaluate the test results against those expectations.
- **Look at the worst case.** Google suggests focusing "on worst-case performance across your evaluation set rather than average performance".
- **Test the hard inputs.** OpenAI says to test a representative set and also inputs from someone trying to break the app. Google says adversarial tests should use data "most likely to elicit problematic output".

Example (made-up numbers): a studio tests a brand tool with 50 prompts. 48 pass, so the average is 96 out of 100. But both failures made a look-alike of a real person. The average looks fine. The worst case does not. A rule that blocks look-alikes must be fixed before launch.

Google also says it is "not uncommon" to go through several rounds of changes and testing before feeling ready to launch.

## Roll out in steps

This is our own pattern. Do not release to everyone at once.

1. **Small group.** A few people inside the team use the real tool.
2. **Wider group.** A limited set of customers, for a short time.
3. **Everyone.** Only after the earlier steps passed the bar you set.

At each step, decide what you will watch and what makes you stop. Chapter 4 of Agent Citadel covered stop rules. They apply to a launch too. Anthropic's agent post recommends, for agents, "extensive testing in sandboxed environments, along with the appropriate guardrails". A rollout in steps is one way to do that with real users.

## Do not lose the files

Google's Veo page says generated videos "are stored on the server for 2 days", after which they are removed. To keep a copy, you must download the video within 2 days. Extended videos "are treated as newly generated videos". If a video is used for an extension, its 2-day timer starts again. Only videos from the last 2 days can be extended. Bootstrap Village, chapter 4, first showed the 2-day storage limit.

Example: a clip is generated on Monday for a campaign that starts on Friday. By Friday the server copy is gone. If nobody downloaded it, the studio must make it again, and the new clip is not the one that was approved.

So the launch gate needs a line: every approved file is downloaded and stored, with its credentials, in the master folder.

YouTube also asks for a decision at upload. Creators answer the "AI use" question in YouTube Studio while uploading. A gate line for labels should be checked before that button is pressed.

## Watch after launch

Launch is not the end. OpenAI says to set spend alerts and to review usage in its dashboard. Google says to monitor with a channel for user feedback. Keep both running. Chapter 5's plan starts when something goes wrong.

## What this means for your work

- **Write the gate, name the owners.** Yes or no, one per line.
- **Give reviewers the brief, the master and the rubric.**
- **Set the pass level before the test.** Judge the worst case, not the average.
- **Release in steps** and decide the stop signs first.
- **Download and store every approved file** before the server removes it.
- **Keep watching** spend, usage and feedback after launch.
