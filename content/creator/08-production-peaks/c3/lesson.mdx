## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Name the three layers that decide an AI label: the vendor's policy, the platform's rules and the law.
- Decide per asset and per platform whether a disclosure is needed, using the platform's own lists.
- Spot the cases that a simple "does it look real?" test gets wrong.

## The problem

Paper Lanterns, an indie band, releases a single next week. The manager plans three YouTube uploads:

1. A lyric video. The song is the band's own, played by the band. The pictures are AI-made ink swirls that drift across the screen.
2. A B-side instrumental. An AI music tool made the whole track. The video is one still image of the cover art.
3. A Spanish version of a band interview. The singer's own voice was cloned with AI to speak the Spanish lines.

The manager has one rule for all of them:

```text
If it looks real, say Yes to AI use. If it doesn't look real, say No.
```

The rule sounds fair. But it misses the one upload that clearly needs a Yes, and it ignores other platforms.

This lesson is not legal advice. It reports what the official pages say. For a contract or a legal question, ask a lawyer.

## The idea

Three separate layers decide whether you label AI work.

```text
vendor policy    what the AI company allows
platform rules   what YouTube, TikTok or Meta ask for
law              what a country or region requires
```

### Layer 1: the vendor's policy

- The [Google Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy](https://policies.google.com/terms/generative-ai/use-policy) forbids "Misrepresenting the provenance of generated content by claiming it was created solely by a human, in order to deceive."
- The [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup) says consumer-facing chatbots or AI agents that talk directly with external users must tell users they are talking to AI. The policy says this must happen "at a minimum at the beginning of each chat session or in the product interface". It also says the disclosure "does not need to identify Anthropic, Claude, or the model used".

Notice the words "in order to deceive" in the Google line. The aim matters.

### Layer 2: the platform's rules

**YouTube.** The [YouTube Help page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491) says creators must disclose when they use AI "to meaningfully alter or generate photorealistic content". Its list of what must be disclosed:

- "Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do."
- "Alters footage of a real event or place."
- "Generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur."
- "Creates music that's the main focus of the video."

Look at the last line. It is about music, not pictures.

The page also lists what needs no disclosure: non-realistic content, and minor edits. Examples from the page:

- "Someone riding a unicorn through a fantastical world"
- colour adjustment, beauty filters and caption creation
- "Production assistance, like using generative AI tools to create or improve a video outline, script, thumbnail, title, or infographic"
- "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs"

How it works on YouTube:

- In YouTube Studio, during upload, you pick Yes or No under "AI use".
- The page says: "Disclosing AI content won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money."
- YouTube may add a label itself, for content with C2PA metadata (a signed history record inside the file) or content its systems detect as AI. A creator can change a wrong label "in most cases". Not for content made with YouTube's AI tools, content with C2PA metadata, or content labelled after manual review.
- Creators who "consistently choose not to disclose" may get a label added by hand, or penalties such as removal of content or suspension from the YouTube Partner Program.

**TikTok.** In a [May 2024 newsroom post](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/partnering-with-our-industry-to-advance-ai-transparency-and-literacy), TikTok said it labels content made with its own AI effects, and has "required creators to label realistic AIGC for over a year". AIGC means AI-generated content. It also said it was starting to read Content Credentials, so it can label AI uploads made elsewhere, and planned to attach credentials to TikTok content.

**Meta.** The [Meta newsroom post](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/04/metas-approach-to-labeling-ai-generated-content-and-manipulated-media/) was first published in April 2024. Its label can appear when Meta detects industry-standard AI signals, or when people disclose. In July 2024 Meta renamed the label from "Made with AI" to "AI info". In September 2024 it moved the label into the post's menu for content that was only edited by AI tools. It kept the label on the post for content generated by an AI tool. Meta also says it may add a more prominent label when content creates a high risk of deceiving the public on a matter of importance.

These pages are dated, and platforms change them. Read the current page in the week you publish.

### Layer 3: the law, with the EU as one example

The [European Commission page on the Code of Practice](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content) says the transparency duties in Article 50 of the AI Act apply "from 2 August 2026". It splits the duties in two:

- **Providers** (the companies that build generative AI systems) must make sure outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as AI.
- **Deployers** (people and companies that use those systems; our reading is that this can include a studio or a band) must disclose a **deepfake**. The page defines it as image, audio or video content that "resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful". Deployers must also disclose AI text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, unless a person reviewed it and someone holds editorial responsibility.

The code itself is voluntary. But the page says: "Even though adherence to the code is voluntary, the transparency requirements under article 50 of the AI Act are legal obligations." The EU also made a set of icons that deployers may use. The [AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) adds that people should know when they are talking to a chatbot.

Whether one file counts as a deepfake is a legal question. Other countries have other rules.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak rule

```text
If it looks real, say Yes to AI use. If it doesn't look real, say No.
```

### Step 2: check each upload against YouTube's lists

| Upload                 | Weak rule says      | YouTube's list says                                  |
| ---------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Lyric video            | No                  | No: the pictures are not realistic                   |
| Spanish interview      | Yes (it looks real) | Not required: own-voice dubs are on the no-need list |
| AI B-side instrumental | No                  | **Yes: AI music that is the main focus**             |

### Step 3: the hard case

**The hard case is the B-side.** Nothing on screen looks real. It is one still picture. So the weak rule says No.

But the YouTube list has a line just for music: content that "Creates music that's the main focus of the video". In this upload the music is the whole point. So the answer to "AI use" is Yes.

The weak rule only asked about pictures. YouTube's list asks about people, places, scenes and music.

### Step 4: the lyric video, checked twice

The ink swirls are AI-made. They are clearly not realistic, like the unicorn example. So no disclosure is needed on YouTube. But the band will also post a short cut on TikTok and Instagram. Our habit: check each platform's own page. TikTok's 2024 post talks about realistic AIGC; Meta labels when it detects AI signals or someone discloses. One platform's answer is not another's.

### Step 5: the better check

```text
For each asset, before export:
  1. Vendor:   are we claiming human-only work in order to deceive? Never.
  2. Platform: read that platform's must-disclose and no-need lists.
               Check every line, including music and voices.
  3. Law:      which markets? Could it be a deepfake? Ask a lawyer.
  4. Record:   asset, platform, decision, page version, date.
```

### Step 6: why it is better

- **It uses the platform's own lists**, not a guess about "real".
- **It catches the music line.** The B-side gets its Yes.
- **It knows the no-need lines too.** The lyric video and the own-voice dub need no disclosure on YouTube, so the team does not guess.
- **It leaves a record** of what the team read, and when.

## Common mistakes

1. **Testing only the pictures.** Sound counts too. Fix: check the music and voice lines on the platform's list.
2. **Copying one platform's answer to all platforms.** Each one writes its own rule. Fix: decide per asset and per platform.
3. **Leaving the label to whoever uploads.** The uploader may not know how the asset was made. Fix: write the decision down before export, next to the master file.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

The band posts two images on Instagram:

- a real tour photo, where an AI tool removed a stray cable from the stage floor
- a poster made fully by an AI image tool

Meta detects industry-standard AI signals in both files.

1. Under Meta's September 2024 update, where does the "AI info" label go for each image?
2. What should the band check before relying on this answer?

### Answer

1. **Tour photo: in the post's menu. Poster: still shown on the post.** Meta's update moved the label into the post's menu "For content that we detect was only modified or edited by AI tools". It kept the visible label for content it detects "was generated by an AI tool".
2. **The current page.** The post has been updated several times; the last update shown is October 23, 2025. Read it again in the week you post.

## Recap

- Three layers decide a label: the vendor's policy, the platform's rules and the law. Each is separate, and each changes.
- Use each platform's own lists. On YouTube, realistic people, places and scenes need disclosure, and so does AI music that is the main focus; non-realistic content, minor edits and production help do not.
- Under the EU AI Act, the Article 50 duties have applied since 2 August 2026, and they are legal obligations, even though the code that helps you meet them is voluntary. Whether a file is a deepfake is a question for a lawyer.
