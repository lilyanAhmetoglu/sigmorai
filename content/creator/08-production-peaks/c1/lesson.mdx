## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Say what a Content Credential holds, and what it can never prove.
- Read every field of a provenance check, one entry at a time.
- Record the history of a file made from several parts, including a gap in that history.

## The problem

Inkwell is a small illustration studio. A publisher buys a book cover from them. The contract asks for a short provenance note: where the cover came from and which parts used AI.

The cover is a composite. A designer joined three parts: a photo of an old bookshop, an AI background of a stormy sky, and the publisher's logo.

The studio manager runs a provenance check on the final cover and writes one line in the archive sheet:

```text
cover-final.png   AI used: no   (the check said not_detected)
```

A week later the publisher opens the file in a viewer and sees an AI step in its history. Nobody lied. The manager read one field and stopped.

## The idea

### What a Content Credential is

C2PA (the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) writes an open standard for file history. The [C2PA explainer](https://c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.2/explainer/Explainer.html) calls the record a **Content Credential**, also known as a C2PA Manifest (a block of signed data that goes with the file). It describes it as "a cryptographically bound structure that records an asset's provenance". Provenance means the history of a file. An asset can be an image, video, audio recording or document.

A credential holds **assertions**: statements about the file. The explainer names three kinds: its origin, its modifications, and its use of AI.

The credential is signed with the private key of the software or hardware that did the work. A signature is a digital seal that anyone can check.

A comparison: a credential is like a receipt stapled to a parcel. It says who packed it and what went in, not whether the gift is nice.

### Tamper-evident, not true

The explainer says that any change to the file or to the credential, "however insignificant", stops the two from matching. The match uses a hash (a short fingerprint of the data, made by maths). So a change can be seen: the record is **tamper-evident**. It does not stop anyone from editing.

The explainer also says provenance "alone cannot tell you whether the digital content is true, accurate or factual". A credential shows that the record is well-formed and untouched. It does not judge the picture.

A credential is also not a lock. Unlike DRM (digital rights management, which limits who can open or copy a file), a credential puts no limit on access, the explainer says.

### Gaps, missing credentials and soft bindings

The explainer answers three questions.

- **Should you distrust a file with no credential?** The answer is "Maybe." Nobody has to add a credential, and files without one should not be "universally less trusted". The sender counts too, for example a publisher you know.
- **Is provenance always complete?** No. If a tool that does not know C2PA crops a file, the record may miss the crop. When the file later goes into a tool that does know C2PA, the new signer "implicitly attests" to the crop. The signer vouches for it without listing it.
- **Can a credential be removed?** Yes. So C2PA describes **durable** credentials: a hard binding (the hash) plus a **soft binding**, such as an invisible watermark (a hidden pattern mixed into the pixels or sound) or a fingerprint lookup. The soft binding lets the credential "be discovered in cloud-hosted storage even if it is removed from the asset".

### Files made from parts

When a file is made from other files, each part is an **ingredient**. The explainer says each ingredient is recorded in the new file's provenance, with its own provenance, which "creates a tree of provenance, much like a family tree".

There is one limit. The ingredient's own data is usually not inside the new file's credential. So its hard binding cannot be checked again later. Instead, it is checked at the moment it is added, and a record of that check is kept.

### Reading the OpenAI provenance check

The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/content-provenance) describe an API (a service your tools call) that checks images and audio. It accepts PNG, JPEG and WebP images. For an image, it returns two entries, one named `c2pa` and one named `synthid` (SynthID is an invisible watermark). Audio gets only a `synthid` entry. The docs say: "Read each applicable entry in `results` independently." No single `outcome` at the top of the response sums them up.

The `c2pa` entry has these fields:

| Field              | Values                                       | Tells you                                     |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| `validation_state` | `trusted`, `valid`, `invalid`, `not_present` | the state of the manifest                     |
| `issuer`           | a name, when known                           | who issued the manifest                       |
| `outcome`          | `detected`, `not_detected`                   | whether OpenAI AI-generation credentials show |

The rules in the docs:

- `detected` only appears when the manifest is `trusted` or `valid`, OpenAI is the issuer, and an AI-generation action is listed.
- A third-party manifest, a manifest without an AI-generation action, an `invalid` one or a `not_present` one all give `not_detected`.
- The issuer and validation state "can still describe a manifest even when the outcome is `not_detected`".
- "Don't treat an `invalid` manifest as reliable provenance evidence."
- `not_present` means no C2PA manifest is available in the image.

Three more lines from the same page:

- A `not_detected` result is "not proof that the content is human-created".
- "Check the C2PA issuer before attributing an image to a particular provider."
- "Verify the original file when possible." A copy may have lost what the original still has.

So `outcome` answers one narrow question: did OpenAI's own AI credential show up? It does not answer whether AI was used.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak record

```text
cover-final.png   AI used: no   (the check said not_detected)
```

The manager read one summary and stopped. The cover's history is not in the summary.

### Step 2: read every entry

The full check result for the cover (our example):

```text
c2pa     validation_state: valid
         issuer:           Brushwork Software Ltd
         outcome:          not_detected
synthid  outcome:          not_detected
```

Our reading:

- The record is intact and signed. It tells you who signed the final file (the studio's design app, a made-up name).
- Neither entry lists the parts of the cover. For that, open the full credential.

### Step 3: open the history

Open the cover in a viewer that shows the whole credential. It lists three ingredients:

```text
ingredient 1   bookshop.jpg    camera capture
ingredient 2   sky.png         own credential: AI generation action
ingredient 3   logo.svg        from the publisher
```

The AI step was in ingredient 2 the whole time.

### Step 4: the hard case

**The hard case is the sky.** Before the designer used it, a junior cropped it in a small photo app that does not know C2PA. The record shows the sky only as it was when it entered the design app. The crop is not listed.

Is this tampering? No. Provenance is not always complete. When the design app signed the cover, it implicitly attested to the crop. The gap is honest. Our habit: write it down, do not hide it.

### Step 5: the good record

```text
cover-final.png
  c2pa:      valid, issuer Brushwork Software Ltd, not_detected
  synthid:   not_detected
  history:   3 ingredients; sky.png has an AI generation action
  gap:       sky.png cropped in a non-C2PA app before use
  master:    /masters/cover-final.png (original export)
  note:      Background sky made with AI. Photo and logo are not AI.
```

### Step 6: why it is better

- **The history is read, not just the summary.** The ingredient list shows where AI was used.
- **The history and its gap are written down.** The record says which ingredient used AI, and where the crop happened.
- **The note is true.** A credential cannot make a caption true; the team writes a true one.

## Common mistakes

1. **One AI yes/no for a composite file.** A cover can mix AI and non-AI parts. Fix: note AI use per ingredient, as in the good record.
2. **Reading a missing crop as fraud.** A tool that does not know C2PA can leave a gap. Fix: look at who signed the next step, and note the gap.
3. **Archiving only the posted copy.** The OpenAI docs warn: "Editing, converting, or sharing a file can remove its metadata." Metadata is data about the file, stored next to the pixels. Fix: keep the original export as the master.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Five years later, a researcher asks Inkwell to prove that the sky inside the cover was never changed after it was added. A teammate says: "Easy. The cover's credential has the sky's credential inside it. We just check the sky's hash again."

1. Can the team check the sky's hard binding again from the cover alone?
2. What does the cover's credential offer instead?

### Answer

1. **No, most likely not.** The C2PA explainer says the ingredient's actual data is usually not included in the new file's credential. Without that data, the hash cannot be checked again.
2. **A record of the check made when the sky was added.** The ingredient's binding and credential are validated at that moment, and the result is kept in the cover's credential. If the team still has the original sky file, they can check that file itself.

## Recap

- A Content Credential is a signed record of a file's origin, edits and AI use. It is tamper-evident, but it cannot say whether a picture is true, and it does not lock the file.
- Read each check entry on its own. `outcome` only says whether OpenAI's AI credential was found; `issuer` and `validation_state` still matter, and an `invalid` manifest is not evidence.
- History can have gaps, and a file without a credential is not automatically suspect. Ingredients, soft bindings and a kept master fill in the story.
