## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Give a model your look by showing it reference images, with no training.
- Say when it is worth training a custom model or a LoRA, and what each one needs.
- Choose reference images and a match level that fit the job.

## The problem

Northbound is a travel magazine. Its look is papercut art: layers of coloured paper with soft shadows between them. Each month it needs two cover illustrations and about twenty small spot illustrations for the inside pages.

The designer collects 10 images for the look and sends them all to an image model: 6 papercut pieces, 2 watercolours the editor likes, and 2 travel photos. The results are a mix. Some look like paper, some look like paint, and one looks like a photo with a filter.

There are two ways to give a model a look. This lesson shows both, and how to choose.

1. **Show it.** Attach reference images to the request. No training.
2. **Teach it.** Train a model, or a small add-on, on your images. Then call it by name.

## The idea

### Way 1: show it, with references

**Recraft.** The [Recraft styles page](https://www.recraft.ai/docs/api-reference/styles) says its V4 styles are "built from your own reference images — one is enough, no training required". You give "from one to ten images". Then: "similar images sharpen the match, diverse ones widen the range."

There are two ways to apply a style:

- `style_id`: create the style once, then pass its id in each request.
- Style references: attach the images to the request. Recraft makes a private style and "returns the resolved `style_id` in the response so later requests can reuse it."

You can't send both: "a request with both a `style_id` and style references is rejected."

A style also stores a `match` value:

- `flexible`, the default, "matches the general vibe".
- `precise` "follows the style meticulously".

One more rule: "Passing `style_match` in a generation request overrides the style's stored value for that request".

**Ideogram 3.0.** The [Ideogram settings page](https://docs.ideogram.ai/create/image-settings) says **Style reference images** take up to 10 of your images "and copy their look". Its advice: "Pick images that share a look and mood for a steady style". For the references to come through: "turn Magic Prompt off and keep style words out of your prompt. Short prompts work best."

**FLUX.2.** The [FLUX.2 overview](https://docs.bfl.ai/flux_2/flux2_overview) lists multi-reference input: up to 8 images on the API for [max], [pro] and [flex], and up to 4 for [klein]. The [FLUX prompting guide](https://docs.bfl.ai/guides/prompting_unified_basics) says to "refer to references by position".

**OpenAI.** Its [image guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/image-generation) has an example that uses "4 input images to generate a new image of a gift basket containing the items in the reference images." That example copies items, not a style (our reading).

References are fast and easy to change. But you send them every time, and a match is a match, not a copy (our reading).

### Way 2: teach it, with a trained model

**Adobe Firefly** offers a subject kind and a style kind of custom model. The [Adobe docs](https://developer.adobe.com/firefly-services/docs/firefly-api/guides/concepts/custom-models/) say:

- Subject models "focus on representing specific characters, products, or objects". Their example is "your brand mascot".
- Style models "emphasize aesthetic qualities like color palettes, patterns, brush stroke techniques, or illustrative cues".

Each model has a unique **asset ID**. Adobe says asset IDs help with "tracking updates and maintaining a clear record of styles, subjects, or structures captured in versions over time." In the API, a request goes to `/v3/images/generate-async` with the header `x-model-version: image3_custom` and a `customModelId`.

**Ideogram custom models** are trained in the app. The [custom models page](https://docs.ideogram.ai/create/custom-models) lists, in October 2026:

- A **Pro** plan, or a **Team** plan in a team space. Desktop only.
- "**15 to 100 images** of the style or subject you want."
- A cost of 4,000 credits, which the **Start training** button displays.
- "Training usually takes a few hours."
- The base model is Ideogram 3.0, and "You can't change it."

Its tips: "Quality over quantity." "Add enough variety." And: "Make each caption say what is in the image."

Sharing: "Your model starts private." You can share it with your team in a team space. **Publish** is different: "Make your model discoverable in the community gallery. Anyone will be able to find and use your model."

### A LoRA: a small add-on

The [BFL training page](https://docs.bfl.ai/flux_2/flux2_klein_training) calls **LoRA** (Low-Rank Adaptation) "the most popular training method". The [diffusers docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/main/en/using-diffusers/loading_adapters) explain it: it works "by inserting new weights into the diffusion model and then only the new weights are trained instead of the entire model." **Weights** are the numbers a model has learned (our gloss).

So a LoRA is a small file that sits on top of a base model. BFL lists it as "Lightweight (typically 10-200MB)" with "Fast training (1-3 hours on consumer GPUs)". Its minimum hardware starts at an NVIDIA GPU with 12GB of VRAM (memory on the graphics card).

BFL's caption rules for a training set:

- "Include your trigger word consistently"
- "Describe everything visible except the style/concept you want to teach the model"

A **trigger word** is a made-up word that switches the LoRA on in a prompt (our gloss). The [diffusers quickstart](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/en/quicktour) adds: "Some LoRAs need a trigger phrase."

Strength: in diffusers, "A value of 0 is the same as only using the base model weights, and a value of 1 is equivalent to using the fully finetuned LoRA."

Two kinds of steps: BFL suggests "1500-2500 steps" of **training** for a style LoRA. That is not the same as the 4 **image** steps BFL lists for [klein] models (our note).

Our advice on rights: train only on images your client owns or has licensed. Ideogram also asks you to accept its terms before your first model.

## Worked example

Our example. The magazine and the files are made up.

### Step 1: the weak reference set

```text
6 papercut pieces   (layered paper, soft shadows, flat colours)
2 watercolours      (the editor's favourites)
2 travel photos     (for the places)
```

Recraft says diverse images "widen the range". This set is very diverse, so the style spreads across paper, paint and photo.

### Step 2: pick for one look

Keep only images that share the look. The designer picks 5 of the 6 papercut pieces. The sixth uses neon paper, so it is left out. The places go in the prompt as words, not in the references.

```text
references: 5 papercut pieces, same paper texture, same soft shadows
prompt:     "A harbour town at dusk with fishing boats and a lighthouse"
```

### Step 3: save the style once

The designer creates the style once and saves the returned `style_id`. Every request in the month uses that id. The 5 images are not sent again.

### Step 4: the hard case

**The hard case is the two kinds of picture.** Covers must hold the look closely: every layer, every shadow. Spot illustrations are small. A looser look is fine there, and the editor wants more variety between them.

One answer is two styles. But the two would drift apart over time. Recraft offers a simpler way: keep one style, and pass `style_match` per request.

```text
cover request:  style_id: northbound-paper   style_match: precise
spot request:   style_id: northbound-paper   style_match: flexible
```

The page says a request value overrides the stored value "for that request". A value the model doesn't support is rejected. So check the model: V2 and V3 take only `regular` (our note from the same page).

### Step 5: why it is better

- **One look, not three.** Similar references sharpen the match.
- **One saved style** for the whole month, with no images resent.
- **Two strengths from one style.** Covers stay tight, spots stay loose.

## Common mistakes

1. **Mixing up training steps and image steps.** A style LoRA trains for 1,500 to 2,500 steps. A [klein] image uses 4. Fix: say which kind of step you mean in every note.
2. **Sending more references than the model takes.** FLUX.2 [klein] takes up to 4; [pro], [max] and [flex] take up to 8 on the API. Fix: check the limit for the exact model first.
3. **Losing track of which model made an image.** A trained model can change over time. Fix: on Adobe, record the asset ID with each image.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A teammate plans a style LoRA for a pottery brand on FLUX.2 [klein] 4B Base. The training set has 20 images:

- All 20 are 400 px wide, copied from the shop's web page.
- 18 of them show the pots on the same white wall.
- 2 have a stock-photo watermark across them.

Using BFL's dataset tips, what would you fix before training?

### Answer

1. **Size.** BFL says to "Use high-resolution images (1024px or higher)". Find the original photos, not the web copies.
2. **Backgrounds.** BFL says to "Avoid repetitive backgrounds" and to vary settings. Otherwise the LoRA may learn the white wall as part of the look (our reading).
3. **Watermarks.** BFL says to "Remove artifacts and low-quality samples". Drop the two watermarked images. The watermark also hints that the brand may not own them (our reading).

## Recap

- To show a look, attach references. Recraft builds a style from 1 to 10 images with no training; similar images sharpen it, and `match` sets how closely it is followed.
- To teach a look or a subject, train: an Adobe style or subject model, an Ideogram custom model (15 to 100 images, Ideogram 3.0 base), or a LoRA on top of an open base model.
- Keep training data clean and owned, keep client models private, and record which model made each image.
