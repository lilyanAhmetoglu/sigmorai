## Why it matters

A bakery chain has a hand-drawn look: ink lines, soft wash, cream paper. It needs 60 menu drawings this year. It also has a mascot, a small fox, who must look the same in every one.

Prompt Forest, chapter 3, showed how to write a style block and how Google's Nano Banana takes style references. Context Library, chapter 2, showed how to build a reference set. This chapter goes one step further. There are two ways to give a model your look:

1. **Show it.** Attach reference images to each request. No training.
2. **Teach it.** Train a model, or a small add-on, on your images. Then call it by name.

## Way 1: style from references

Three vendors in this realm take a look from your images, with no training.

**Recraft.** Its V4 styles are "built from your own reference images — one is enough, no training required". You give "from one to ten images". Recraft's tip: "similar images sharpen the match, diverse ones widen the range."

You can apply a style in two ways:

- `style_id`: create the style once, then pass its id in each request.
- **style references**: attach the images to the request. Recraft creates a private style and "returns the resolved `style_id` in the response so later requests can reuse it."

Don't send both. "a request with both a `style_id` and style references is rejected."

A style also has a `match` value. `flexible` (the default) "matches the general vibe". `precise` "follows the style meticulously".

**Ideogram 3.0.** Its **Style reference images** setting takes up to 10 images "and copy their look". Pick "images that share a look and mood for a steady style". Ideogram's tip: "turn Magic Prompt off and keep style words out of your prompt."

**FLUX.2.** BFL's models take several input images at once: up to 8 on the API for [max], [pro] and [flex], and up to 4 for [klein]. BFL's prompting guide says to "refer to references by position: "image 1" is the first entry in `images`, "image 2" the second." Chapter 1 showed this too.

**OpenAI.** Its image guide has an example that uses "4 input images to generate a new image of a gift basket containing the items in the reference images." That example keeps items, not a style, but the input works the same way (our reading).

References are fast and cheap to change. But you send them every time, and the match is a "vibe" or a "blueprint", not a promise (our reading).

## Way 2: a trained model

A **trained model** has learned your look from a set of images. After that, a short prompt is enough (our plain gloss).

**Adobe Firefly custom models** come in two kinds:

- **Subject models** "focus on representing specific characters, products, or objects", such as "your brand mascot".
- **Style models** "emphasize aesthetic qualities like color palettes, patterns, brush stroke techniques, or illustrative cues".

Each model has an **asset ID**. Adobe says asset IDs help you track "styles, subjects, or structures captured in versions over time." In the API you call `/v3/images/generate-async` with the header `x-model-version: image3_custom` and a `customModelId`.

**Ideogram custom models** are trained in the app. The page lists, in October 2026:

- **15 to 100 images** of the style or subject.
- A **Pro** or **Team** plan, on desktop only.
- 4,000 credits, shown on the **Start training** button.
- "Training usually takes a few hours."
- The base model is Ideogram 3.0, and you can't change it.

Its tips: "Quality over quantity", "Add enough variety", and "Make each caption say what is in the image."

## A LoRA: the small add-on

BFL's FLUX.2 [klein] training page calls **LoRA** (Low-Rank Adaptation) "the most popular training method". Diffusers explains it: LoRA works "by inserting new weights into the diffusion model and then only the new weights are trained instead of the entire model." **Weights** are the numbers a model learned (our gloss).

So a LoRA is a small file that sits on top of a base model. BFL says LoRAs are "Lightweight (typically 10-200MB)" and train in "1-3 hours on consumer GPUs". To train on FLUX.2 [klein] 4B Base, it lists an NVIDIA GPU with 12GB of VRAM (graphics memory).

Two habits from the pages:

- **Captions.** BFL: "Include your trigger word consistently" and "Describe everything visible except the style/concept you want to teach the model." A **trigger word** is a made-up word that switches the LoRA on in a prompt (our gloss). Diffusers: "Some LoRAs need a trigger phrase. Check the LoRA's model card."
- **Strength.** In diffusers you load one with `load_lora_weights()`. A scale of 0 "is the same as only using the base model weights", and 1 is the full LoRA.

Note the word "steps" here. BFL suggests 1,500 to 2,500 **training** steps for a style LoRA. That is not the same as the 4 **image** steps BFL lists for [klein]. Chapter 4 covers image steps (our note).

## Which one is worth it?

Our advice, built from the pages above:

| Your job                                  | Start with                                             |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| A few images, this week                   | References (Recraft style, Ideogram 3.0, FLUX.2)       |
| A look you will reuse for months          | A saved style (`style_id`), then a trained style model |
| One product or mascot that must stay true | A **subject** model (Adobe) or a subject LoRA          |
| Fewer than 15 good images                 | References. Ideogram training needs 15 to 100          |

For the bakery: start with a Recraft style from 3 good drawings today. Train a fox subject model later, when you have 15 or more clean fox images (our example).

## Rights before training

Training copies a look into a model. So train only on images your client owns or has licensed. Bootstrap Village, chapter 6, covers what vendor policies say.

Two details from the pages. Ideogram asks you to accept its terms before your first model. A model "starts private". You can share it with your team in a team space. But **Publish** makes it "discoverable in the community gallery. Anyone will be able to find and use your model." Don't publish a client's model (our advice).

## What this means for your work

- **Try references first.** One image can be enough on Recraft. No training, no wait.
- **Send `style_id` or references, never both**, on Recraft.
- **Train when the look or the subject must last.** Pick subject or style on purpose.
- **Count your images.** Ideogram needs 15 to 100.
- **Caption what is in the image, not the style.** Use one trigger word.
- **Keep client models private**, and train only on images you may use.
