## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Say what open weights are, and read a model's licence before you use it for paid work.
- Name the parts of a local image pipeline and what each one does.
- Test speed and memory on your own machine before you promise a deadline.

## The problem

Brightfield is a two-person photo studio. A furniture shop wants 400 product shots in one week. Each shot needs a plain room scene behind a real product photo.

The studio would like to drop its per-image API bill and host a model on its own PC. One partner reads a vendor page that says "sub-second" and writes the quote: "400 images: less than 10 minutes of compute. Delivery: tomorrow."

The next morning the model does not fit in the graphics card's memory. When it finally runs, the first image takes over a minute. The deadline was based on a number from someone else's machine.

Running a model yourself changes more than the price. It changes the licence check, the setup, the speed, and the safety checks.

## The idea

### What "open weights" means

**Weights** are the numbers a model learned in training. **Open weights** means the vendor lets you download that file and run the model on your own machine (our plain gloss).

The [BFL FLUX.2 overview](https://docs.bfl.ai/flux_2/flux2_overview) says, in October 2026:

- "[klein] 4B is fully open under **Apache 2.0**."
- "[klein] 9B is available under the **FLUX Non-Commercial License**."

BFL also lists **Base** versions of [klein]. It calls them "ideal for fine-tuning, LoRA training, research, and custom pipelines". (Fine-tuning means more training on your own images.) And: "Base models are available as open weights for local development and research. They are not offered on the public API."

### Read the licence first

A **licence** is the legal text that says what you may do with the weights (our plain gloss).

| Model (October 2026)          | Licence on BFL's pages               |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| FLUX.2 [klein] 4B and 4B Base | Apache 2.0                           |
| FLUX.2 [klein] 9B and 9B Base | FLUX Non-Commercial License          |
| FLUX.2 [dev]                  | pricing row: "Free (non-commercial)" |

Our advice: for a paid job, a licence with "non-commercial" in its name is a stop sign. Read the full text, or ask whoever handles your contracts. A free download does not mean free for paid work. The licence comes with the weights, wherever you run them (our reading).

Some downloads are also **gated**: you must log in to get them. The [diffusers quickstart](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/en/quicktour) asks you to sign up for a Hugging Face account "to use gated models like Flux".

### The parts of a pipeline

On an API you send a prompt and get an image. On your own machine you load a **pipeline**. The quickstart says a pipeline "packages the pieces of a diffusion model (text encoder, scheduler, UNet or DiT, and VAE) into one class". UNet and DiT are two kinds of main model (our gloss).

The [diffusers FLUX.2 page](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/en/api/pipelines/flux2) lists the parts. In plain words:

- **Text encoder**: changes the prompt into numbers. FLUX.2 [dev] uses a Mistral model; the [klein] pipeline uses a Qwen3 model.
- **Transformer**: the main model. It is used "to denoise the encoded image latents". **Latents** are a small, packed version of the picture that the model works on.
- **Scheduler**: the [schedulers page](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/en/using-diffusers/schedulers) says it "tells the denoising loop how much noise to remove at each step. Different schedulers trade speed for quality."
- **VAE**: used "to encode and decode images to and from latent representations". In plain words, it turns latents back into a real picture.

A comparison: the text encoder reads the order, the transformer paints, the scheduler sets the pace, and the VAE frames the result.

About the scheduler, the same page says Flux ships a default one: "Keep that scheduler unless you are intentionally experimenting." Swap it "only when the replacement is compatible with the checkpoint".

One more note from BFL: "FLUX.2 [klein] does not include prompt upsampling." So: "Write detailed, descriptive prompts for best results."

### Hardware and speed

What the pages say, in October 2026:

- **BFL:** [klein] gives "sub-second inference", and "4B runs on consumer GPUs (~13GB VRAM)". **VRAM** is the memory on the graphics card.
- **Diffusers:** "Flux2 can be quite expensive to run on consumer hardware devices."
- **Less memory:** "To use less memory, load in 4-bit with bitsandbytes." (4-bit is a smaller number format.) The FLUX.2 page warns this saves memory "with a trade-off in inference latency": each image can take longer.
- **Compile:** the quickstart says you can compile parts of the model to speed up later runs. "The first generate after compile is a cold start and is slow. Later generates are faster."

Our reading: a vendor's speed figure was measured on the vendor's hardware. Yours may differ.

### What you get, and what you lose

Vendor APIs can add checks. The [OpenAI image docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/image-generation): "All prompts and generated images are filtered in accordance with our content policy." The [Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/image-generation): "All generated images include a SynthID watermark." (A watermark is a hidden mark that labels an image.)

On your own machine, nothing is added for you beyond the pipeline's own parts.

- The [diffusers legacy page](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/en/using-diffusers/legacy_checkpoints) offers a safety checker "for older Stable Diffusion checkpoints". It adds: "Newer models such as Qwen-Image do not have this checker."
- The FLUX.2 list of parts names neither a safety checker nor a watermark step (our reading of the list).
- About Stable Diffusion, the diffusers team and Hugging Face "strongly recommend keeping the safety filter enabled in all public-facing circumstances". Our advice: keep that idea for any public app.

## Worked example

Our example. The studio and all timings are made up.

### Step 1: the weak plan

```text
model:     FLUX.2 [klein] 4B
source:    "sub-second" on the vendor page
estimate:  400 images x 1 second = under 10 minutes
deadline:  tomorrow
```

The speed figure came from someone else's machine. Nobody checked memory.

### Step 2: check memory first

```text
studio PC:  one graphics card with 12GB VRAM
BFL says:   4B runs on consumer GPUs (~13GB VRAM)
```

Our reading: 12 is less than about 13, so it may not fit at full size. The studio loads the transformer and text encoder in 4-bit, as the quickstart shows. Now it fits, but the FLUX.2 page warns of slower images.

### Step 3: the hard case

**The hard case is the first timed image.** The studio also compiles the model for speed. Its first test image takes 70 seconds. The partner almost multiplies 70 × 400 and cancels the job.

But the quickstart says the first generate after compile "is a cold start and is slow". So the first image is not a fair sample. The studio runs one warm-up image, then times 20 images at the real size, 1024 × 1024:

```text
warm-up:          1 image, 70 s   (not counted)
timed run:       20 images, 120 s
per image:        6 s
400 images:     2,400 s = 40 minutes, plus one warm-up per session
```

### Step 4: the honest quote

```text
compute:    about 40 minutes (measured on our PC, at 1024 x 1024)
review:     a person checks each image against the product photo
delivery:   in 2 days, with time to re-run any rejects
```

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Memory is checked before the job,** not on the morning of it.
- **The test ignores the cold start,** so the per-image time is real.
- **The deadline includes people,** not only the GPU.

## Common mistakes

1. **Quoting the vendor's speed as your speed.** Fix: time 10 to 20 images on your machine, at your real size.
2. **Timing the first image after compile.** It is a cold start. Fix: run one warm-up image, then start the clock.
3. **Very short prompts on [klein].** BFL says [klein] does not upsample prompts. Fix: write detailed, descriptive prompts yourself.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A studio times its compiled pipeline. The first image takes 90 seconds. After that, 30 images take 240 seconds. The studio restarts the PC each morning, and it must make 900 images over 3 days.

1. What is the time per image after warm-up?
2. About how long is the total compute time?
3. What else must the plan include?

### Answer

1. **8 seconds.** 240 ÷ 30 = 8.
2. **About 2 hours and 4 minutes.** 900 × 8 = 7,200 seconds, which is 2 hours. Add one 90-second cold start each morning: 3 × 90 = 270 seconds, about 4.5 minutes.
3. **Review time and re-runs.** Compute is only part of the job. A person still checks every image, and some will need a second run (our advice).

## Recap

- Open weights let you run a model yourself, but a licence still comes with them. "Non-commercial" means stop and check before paid work.
- A local pipeline has four main parts: text encoder, transformer, scheduler and VAE. Leave the scheduler as shipped unless a test needs a change.
- Test memory and speed on your own machine, skip the cold start, and plan your own checks and labels: a local pipeline may have no filter and no watermark.
