## Why it matters

One good image is easy. Thirty images that look like one brand are hard. If each prompt describes the style in new words, each image gets a new look. A client will see that at once. This lesson shows how to turn "our look" into text you can reuse, and how to check that it holds.

## Style is something you name

Google's docs give a template for stylised images: "A [style] of a [subject, with details about accessories or actions] doing [activity]. The design features [visual qualities, e.g., bold outlines, cel-shading, etc.] and [color/background preference]." The guide says to describe the artistic style, subject and medium, and to be "specific about the visual detail (bold lines, colors, etc.) for consistent results".

Google's own example: "A kawaii-style sticker of a happy red panda wearing a tiny bamboo hat... The design features bold, clean outlines, simple cel-shading, and a vibrant color palette. The background must be white."

Notice the split. The subject (red panda, bamboo hat) is one part. The look (kawaii, bold outlines, cel-shading, white background) is another. You can change the first and keep the second.

## Write a style block

A **style block** is a short paragraph that describes the look and nothing else. It is our own method, built from the template above. Put the same block at the top of every prompt for the campaign. Change only the subject.

Example for a café brand:

> Style: flat vector illustration with bold, clean outlines and simple cel-shading. Palette: cream background, forest green, terracotta, small touches of mustard. Soft top-left light, no gradients. Plain cream background.

Then each post only adds a subject: "Subject: a hand holding a green cup." Or: "Subject: a croissant on a plate."

What to put in a style block:

- **Medium and technique.** Flat vector, studio photograph, 3D render, paper cut-out.
- **Line, shape and texture.** Bold outlines, soft edges, grain.
- **Colour.** Name the colours in words, and the background.
- **Light and camera.** Soft side light, low-angle, shallow focus.
- **Rules in positive words.** "Plain cream background", not "no busy background" (chapter 1).

Test the block (our habit). Results vary, so run it on three different subjects and compare the results side by side before you start the campaign.

## Style from pictures

Words are one tool. Pictures are another. Google lists "style references" for its Nano Banana Pro model, up to 3 per request (chapter 2). Google also has a style-transfer template: "Transform the provided photograph of [subject] into the artistic style of [artist/art style]. Preserve the original composition but render it with [description of stylistic elements]."

Naming a living artist or another brand's style can touch other people's rights. Realm 0, chapter 6 covers what the vendor policies say. Prefer your client's own references.

## Let a vision model help you write the block

Claude, OpenAI's models and Gemini can all read images and answer in text. Anthropic's vision docs say you can send several images in one request, and that Claude analyses them jointly. Label each image "Image 1:", "Image 2:" and so on.

A workflow, ours and not a vendor's: send 4 or 5 of the client's best existing pieces and ask for a plain-word description of the shared look: medium, line, colour, light. Then edit that draft into your style block.

You must check what comes back. Anthropic's docs say Claude "might hallucinate or make mistakes" with low-quality, rotated or very small images, and that you should "always carefully review and verify" its image interpretations. A vision model can call a vector illustration a watercolour. You know your client's work. Correct it.

Anthropic's prompting guide also says a few well-made examples improve accuracy and consistency. That advice is for text prompts, but the idea carries over: your approved images are the examples for your style block.

## Consistency is not guaranteed

OpenAI's limits list says the model "may occasionally struggle to maintain visual consistency for recurring characters or brand elements across multiple generations". Treat the style block as your best tool, not a promise.

Ways to keep a set together:

- **Keep an approved image as the anchor.** Give it as a reference in later prompts (chapter 2).
- **Stay in one conversation.** Google calls multi-turn conversation the recommended way to iterate. OpenAI's Responses API also supports multi-turn image work. For views of one character, Google advises including previously generated images in later prompts.
- **Review as a set.** Put the new image next to the anchor. Do the lines, colours and light match?

## What this means for your work

- **Separate subject from style.** The subject changes. The style block does not.
- **Write the block once, test it, then freeze it.** Version it like a file: "style-block v2".
- **Use pictures and words together.** Words are easy to reuse. Pictures carry what words miss.
- **Verify what a vision model tells you.** Treat its description as a draft.
- **Review every image against the anchor.** Drift is normal. Catch it early.
