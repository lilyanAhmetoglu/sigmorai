## Why it matters

A video prompt has everything an image prompt has, plus **time**: something moves, the camera moves, and there may be sound. A prompt that names only a subject leaves motion, camera and sound to the model's guess. This lesson shows how to write one shot, then how to plan a sequence.

A note on sources. OpenAI's prompting guide was written for Sora 2, and realm 0 chapter 4 covers that API's shutdown. We use the guide only for prompting ideas that do not depend on that model. The Veo and Runway pages are current.

## The parts of a video prompt

Google's Veo guide lists the elements a prompt should have:

- **Subject.** The object, person, animal or scenery.
- **Action.** What the subject is doing.
- **Style.** Creative direction, such as film noir or cartoon.
- **Camera positioning and motion** (optional). Aerial view, eye-level, dolly shot.
- **Composition** (optional). Wide shot, close-up, two-shot.
- **Focus and lens effects** (optional). Shallow focus, macro lens, wide-angle lens.
- **Ambiance** (optional). How colour and light set the mood: blue tones, night, warm tones.

Google's example puts several of them in one line: "Close up shot (composition) of melting icicles (subject) on a frozen rock wall (context) with cool blue tones (ambiance), zoomed in (camera motion) maintaining close-up detail of water drips (action)."

Compare Google's two versions of one idea. The short one: "The camera dollies to show a close up of a desperate man in a green trench coat... It looks like a movie scene." The detailed one adds the weathered coat, the brick wall, the green neon glow, the dolly-in and the shallow depth of field. Google uses the pair to show how more specific details refine the output.

## One shot, one action

OpenAI's Sora 2 prompting guide advises: when you describe several shots, "keep each shot block distinct: one camera setup, one subject action, and one lighting recipe at a time". Runway's Multi-Shot recipe says the same in its tips: "Write one clear idea per shot".

So a prompt like "the runner sprints, then we see her laces, then the logo appears, with a drone shot at the end" runs four shots together, with no camera or light for any of them. Give each shot its own distinct prompt, or its own block if your tool takes a shot list (Runway's Multi-Shot recipe has a mode for this). Veo 3.1 makes one 8-second clip per request, so there one prompt per shot is the simple choice.

For Sora 2, OpenAI found the model "generally follows instructions more reliably in shorter clips", and suggested two 4-second clips over one 8-second clip. Check what your model allows: on Veo 3.1, 1080p and 4k clips are 8 seconds only.

## Detail or freedom

For Sora 2, OpenAI's guide describes the trade-off. Shorter prompts "give the model more creative freedom", while longer, more detailed prompts "restrict the model's creativity". It also says: "Treat your prompt as a creative wish list, not a contract". The same prompt run twice gives different results.

For brand work you usually want control, so write the details that matter: the product, the colour, the camera move. Leave open what you don't mind, such as the extras in the background.

## Sound is part of the prompt

Veo makes audio with the video. Google's guide says you can give cues for three kinds of sound:

- **Dialogue.** Use quotes for specific speech: "This must be the key," he murmured.
- **Sound effects.** Describe them: tires screeching loudly, engine roaring.
- **Ambient noise.** Describe the soundscape: a faint, eerie hum in the background.

Example, added to the end of a shot prompt: footsteps splash on wet pavement, city traffic hums far away, and the runner says, "Run your city."

## Start from a picture

You can give a video model an image to begin from. Google's docs say Veo uses the input image as the initial frame, and to pick an image "closest to what you envision as the first scene". Google's own example makes an image with Nano Banana and then animates it with Veo.

Veo 3.1 can also take up to three reference images of a person, character, or product "to preserve the subject's appearance", or a first and a last frame to set how a shot starts and ends. Realm 0 listed the limits that go with these features.

Example: generate the perfect hero frame as an image (chapters 1 to 4), approve it, then animate it. You settle the look in a cheap image step and only then spend a video render.

## Plan a sequence as a shot list

Long ads are several short shots. Plan them like a film crew. This table is our own format:

| Shot | Length | Subject and action                         | Camera                    | Sound                         |
| ---- | ------ | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 1    | 8 s    | Runner sprints along a wet street          | Low tracking shot, wide   | Footsteps, traffic hum        |
| 2    | 8 s    | Same runner stops, close-up of the sneaker | Slow dolly-in, macro lens | Quiet breath, one spoken line |

Runway's tips add: "Reuse the same product or style language across shots for visual consistency." Copy the same subject description and the same style block (chapter 3) into every shot prompt.

## What this means for your work

- **Write the parts.** Subject, action, style, camera, composition, focus, ambiance.
- **One camera setup, one action, one lighting recipe per shot.**
- **Prompt the sound.** Quotes for speech, plain words for effects and ambience.
- **Settle the look in an image first**, then animate it.
- **Repeat the same subject and style words in every shot.**
- **Expect variation.** Run a shot more than once and pick the best.
