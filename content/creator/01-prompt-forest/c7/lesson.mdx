## Why it matters

Chapter 1 put "light" in the five-part checklist. Chapter 3 put the palette in the style block. This chapter goes one step deeper. It shows which words for light and colour the vendors' guides really use. It also shows how to change the light, or the colours, in an edit without losing the rest.

"Nice lighting" tells the model almost nothing. "Soft, diffused lighting from the top left" tells it the quality and the direction. That sentence is from Google's own example.

## Light has parts

To write light, we split it into four parts. The split is ours, but every example below is from a vendor's page.

- **Quality.** Soft, diffused, harsh. Google's product template asks for "soft, diffused highlights" and to "eliminate harsh shadows".
- **Direction.** Where the light comes from. Google's negative-space example says "lighting from the top left".
- **Setup or source.** What makes the light. Google's product template names "a three-point softbox setup". OpenAI's examples use "soft coastal daylight" and "soft studio lighting".
- **Time of day.** OpenAI's scene example says "The time of day is dusk". Google's older Imagen guide adds "golden hour" to a park scene at sunset.

Example, for a ceramics shop:

> A studio product photograph of a speckled cream mug on a linen cloth. A three-point softbox setup with soft, diffused highlights. Light from the top left.

## What each guide lists

| Guide                           | Light words in its examples                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Google Gemini (Nano Banana)     | "three-point softbox setup", "soft, diffused lighting from the top left", "soft, subtle lighting", a "[Description of the light]" slot |
| OpenAI GPT Image prompting      | "soft coastal daylight", "natural lighting", "soft studio lighting", "soft cinematic lighting"                                         |
| Google Imagen (Vertex AI guide) | a lighting row: "natural, dramatic, warm, cold"; "golden hour"                                                                         |

A note on the last row. It comes from Google's Vertex AI image prompt guide (October 2026). Google marks the Vertex AI docs as "no longer being updated", and the Gemini API page says Imagen models are shut down there. We use its tables as a word list, not as rules for today's models.

Imagen's guide also shows light as a refinement step. It starts with "A park in the spring next to a lake". Then it adds "the sun sets across the lake, golden hour". Then "red wildflowers". Write the core idea first, then add the light.

## Mood words are not enough

OpenAI's guide says: "Describe visible details. Name materials, lighting, colors, and the visual medium." For "wide, cinematic, low-light, rainy, or neon scenes", it says to "specify scale, atmosphere, and color instead of relying on mood words alone".

Example. "A moody café at night" leaves the model to guess. Try: "A small café at night, warm light from lamps over the counter, a deep blue street outside the window, wet reflections on the floor." Each phrase names something you can see.

## Colour words the guides show

The guides name colours in plain words, often with one quality word in front.

- **Google Gemini:** "a vibrant color palette", "a dramatic palette of deep blues and bright yellows", "a vast, empty off-white canvas". The sticker template has a "[color/background preference]" slot.
- **OpenAI:** "natural color balance", "realistic colors", "warm earthy colors", "muted blues and grays", "subtle natural accent colors".
- **Google Imagen:** "muted orange warm tones"; and "duotone (mention two colors)", with the example "blue and grey duotones".

Notice the pattern. A colour name plus a word like muted, vibrant, deep or earthy. The examples on these pages use words, not hex codes. If you try a hex code, test it and check the result (our advice).

## A light change or a colour change?

These are two different edits. Ask for them in two different turns.

- **Light change.** Google's tip for iterating is a follow-up like "can you make the lighting a bit warmer?"
- **Colour change.** Google's editing intro lists "adjust the color grading" as its own kind of edit, next to add, remove and change style.

OpenAI's guide gives the rule for any edit: "request one change, and repeat the details to preserve". It also says to list what must stay the same. For precise edits that list can include "saturation, contrast", the camera angle and the objects around the change.

So name what stays. Example, for a light change:

> Make the light warmer, like late afternoon sun from the left. Keep the label colour, the layout and the camera angle unchanged.

And for a colour change:

> Change the mug glaze from cream to deep ochre. Keep the lighting, the shadows and the background unchanged.

When you add something to a photo, ask it to match the light. OpenAI's clothing example says: "Match lighting, shadows, and color temperature to the original photo". Chapter 4 showed Google's version of the same idea.

## Exact brand colours

A colour word is not a colour code. "Forest green" names a family of greens, not your brand's green (our view). OpenAI's guide says: "Repeated edits can still change details you intended to preserve." It adds: "If a region must remain pixel-identical, composite the approved edit into the original image instead of relying on prompting alone."

So when a brand colour must be exact, check it with a colour picker. Fix it in your design tool if it is wrong (our advice).

## What this means for your work

- **Name the light in parts.** Quality, direction, setup, time of day.
- **Swap mood words for things you can see.** "Neon" becomes the sign, its colour and its reflection.
- **Name colours with a quality word.** Muted, vibrant, deep, earthy.
- **One edit, one change.** Light in one turn, colour in the next.
- **Say what must stay.** Colours when you change light; light when you change colours.
- **Check brand colours by hand.**
