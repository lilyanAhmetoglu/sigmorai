## Why it matters

A short prompt like "a carton of oat milk" leaves every choice to the model. Which angle? Which light? Which room? The model picks, and its picks are not your client's picks. A **shot prompt** is a prompt that describes the picture the way a photographer would describe a shot. This lesson shows what to put in it.

Realm 0 showed what image models can read and make. Now you write the input.

## The parts of a shot prompt

Google's Gemini docs give a template for photorealistic images: a **type of shot**, a **subject**, a **setting**, a **description of the light**, a **camera angle** and a **lens type**. Their product-photo template adds the lighting setup, the feature to show, the sharp-focus detail and the aspect ratio.

From these templates, Sigmorai uses a five-part checklist. It is our own checklist, not a vendor rule:

1. **Subject.** What is in the picture, with its details.
2. **Setting.** Where it is: surface, room, background.
3. **Light.** Soft window light, studio softbox, golden hour.
4. **Camera.** Shot type, angle, lens: wide shot, macro, low angle.
5. **Format.** Aspect ratio and what the picture is for.

Example, for an oat-milk brand:

> A high-resolution product photograph of a white oat-milk carton with a green label, on a pale oak kitchen table. Soft window light from the left. Slightly elevated 45-degree angle, sharp focus on the label. Landscape 3:2.

Every part answers a question the model would otherwise guess.

## What Google's guide adds

Google's "best practices" list for Nano Banana has tips that are worth trying with other image models too (our view). Each one below is quoted or closely paraphrased from the page.

- **Be hyper-specific.** Instead of "fantasy armor", describe it: "ornate elven plate armor, etched with silver leaf patterns". The more detail you give, the more control you have.
- **Give context and intent.** Say what the image is for. "Create a logo for a high-end, minimalist skincare brand" gives better results than "Create a logo".
- **Use semantic negative prompts.** Don't write "no cars". Describe what you want: "an empty, deserted street with no signs of traffic".
- **Control the camera.** Terms like wide-angle shot, macro shot and low-angle perspective steer the composition.
- **Break complex scenes into steps.** "First, a misty forest at dawn. Then, in the foreground, a stone altar. Finally, a glowing sword on the altar."

Anthropic's prompting guide is written for text prompts, not images. But its main rule is the same: "Claude responds well to clear, explicit instructions", and its "golden rule" is to show your prompt to a colleague with little context. If they would be confused, the model will be too. Use that test on your image prompts.

## Leave room for the layout

Designers often add text later in their own tools. Google's docs describe a "minimalist and negative space" prompt for backgrounds "where text will be overlaid". The example asks for a subject in the bottom-right and "a vast, empty off-white canvas".

Example: a banner needs a headline on the left. Ask for the product on the right and an empty, plain area on the left. Don't ask the model to place the headline too.

## Text inside the picture

If the image must contain words, check them. OpenAI's docs say its GPT Image models "can still struggle with precise text placement and clarity". They also say the model "may have difficulty placing elements precisely in structured or layout-sensitive compositions". Google says Gemini works best if you "first generate the text and then ask for an image with the text".

So read every letter of every price, name and slogan. If one is wrong, retry or set the text in your design tool.

## Iterate, don't restart

Google calls multi-turn conversation "the recommended way to iterate on images". Its tip: don't expect a perfect image on the first try. Follow up with small changes, such as "make the lighting a bit warmer".

OpenAI's Responses API also supports multi-turn image work: you keep the image in the conversation and refine it with new instructions.

## Drafts first, finals later

OpenAI's current image models (GPT Image 2.5) have a quality setting: `low`, `medium`, `high`, `xhigh`, `max` or `auto`. Earlier models stop at `high`. The docs say: use `quality: "low"` "for quick drafts. For final assets, compare higher quality settings to find the right balance of detail, latency, and cost." They also warn that complex prompts "may take up to 2 minutes".

Example: you have 6 prompt ideas for a hero image. Run all 6 as quick drafts, pick the best 2, then render those at higher quality.

## What this means for your work

- **Write the five parts.** Subject, setting, light, camera, format. Skip one only when you don't care how it turns out.
- **Say what you want, not what you don't.** Replace "no clutter" with "a clean, empty table".
- **Plan for text.** Leave blank space for headlines, and proof-read any words the model draws.
- **Draft cheap, finish careful.** Low quality for ideas, higher quality for the winner.
- **Change one thing per turn.** Small edits show you which words did what.
