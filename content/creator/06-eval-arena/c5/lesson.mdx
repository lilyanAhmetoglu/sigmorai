## Why it matters

A beautiful image that your client cannot publish is a failed image. Safety is a gate in your scorecard, next to the logo and the label. Realm 0, chapter 6 covered the rules: rights, policies and provenance. This chapter is about testing a model against them, and about what the safety tools do and do not tell you.

## Make safety a number

Chapter 1 showed Claude's rate-style criterion for safety. Google adds a habit: "think about the minimum acceptable levels of safety metrics before testing". Then you can compare results with a line you set in advance, not one you invent after seeing them.

Google's safety guide names two kinds of testing:

- **Safety benchmarking:** metrics that reflect how your application could be unsafe, tested on an evaluation set. Google warns about over-relying on "off the shelf" approaches. It says you will likely need your own test sets.
- **Adversarial testing:** "proactively trying to break your application". It tells you to "select test data that is most likely to elicit problematic output", including rare cases and edge cases.

## Layer 1: the vendor's filters

OpenAI's image guide says all prompts and generated images "are filtered in accordance with our content policy". Its `moderation` parameter has two values: `auto` (the default), which is "standard filtering", and `low`, which is "less restrictive filtering".

Google's Gemini API has adjustable safety filters for four categories: harassment, hate speech, sexually explicit and dangerous. It also has "built-in protections against core harms, such as content that endangers child safety", which "are always blocked and cannot be adjusted". A detail to know: the API blocks "based on the probability of content being unsafe and not the severity". So test with your own examples.

For an A/B test (chapter 4), use the same safety settings on both models. If one model runs with less strict filtering, a difference in blocked prompts may come from the setting.

## Layer 2: a moderation check

OpenAI's moderation model, `omni-moderation-latest`, accepts text and images. The guide says the endpoint is free to use and image files can be up to 20 MB.

Read the results with care:

- **A zero is not always a clean bill.** Some categories are "Text only", such as harassment, hate and illicit. The guide says that if you send only images, the model "will return a score of 0 for these unsupported categories". Our fix: send the image together with its prompt or caption, so the text categories have text to score.
- **Scores are signals.** OpenAI says to "Treat moderation scores as signals for your application's policy, not as an automatic blocking decision." Start with `flagged`, then use the categories and scores for logging, routing and review queues.
- **Scores can drift.** OpenAI plans to keep upgrading the underlying model, so "custom policies that rely on `category_scores` may need recalibration over time". Chapter 6 shows how to catch that.

OpenAI also says not to send known or suspected child sexual abuse material to the Moderation API, because it is not designed for that. Follow the vendor's separate child-safety guidance for that risk.

## Layer 3: people

Claude's vision docs say: "Do not use Claude for tasks requiring perfect precision or sensitive image analysis without human oversight." Google's guide says that plans should include monitoring, because "you can never guarantee perfection". A person should look at flagged images, and at a sample of the images that were not flagged.

Anthropic describes why layers help. It compares evaluation to the Swiss Cheese Model: "no single evaluation layer catches every issue", and with several methods, "failures that slip through one layer are caught by another."

## Test with three sets

Our design, following Google's adversarial testing advice:

- **Everyday set:** normal briefs.
- **Edge set:** briefs that can go wrong without bad intent, such as a request for "a tough-looking crowd" or a real brand name.
- **Adversarial set:** briefs written to push the model past its limits.

Google also notes that "LLMs are known to sometimes produce different outputs for the same input prompt", so a single round may miss problems.

Report each set on its own. Google says that with several metrics, "you may want to focus on worst-case performance across your evaluation set rather than average performance." Here are made-up numbers and a made-up target of 98% on every set:

| Set         | Images | Safe | Rate |
| ----------- | ------ | ---- | ---- |
| Everyday    | 300    | 300  | 100% |
| Edge        | 100    | 93   | 93%  |
| Adversarial | 50     | 38   | 76%  |

All together, 431 of 450 are safe, which is 95.8%. That looks close to the target. But two sets are far below it, and the adversarial set is at 76%. The scorecard should say fail.

## What this means for your work

- **Set a safety target before you test**, for every set.
- **Use the same filter settings on both models.**
- **Treat a moderation zero with care.** Send the text with the image.
- **Use scores to route images to people**, not to decide alone.
- **Build everyday, edge and adversarial sets**, and report each one.
- **Judge safety by the worst set**, not the average.
