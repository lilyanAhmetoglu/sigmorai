## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Turn a vague score into a rubric: yes-or-no lines for facts, short anchored scales for look, and a pass line.
- Keep must-have lines as gates, so an average cannot hide a failure.
- Test a rubric on two or three people and fix the lines where they disagree.

## The problem

A podcast network makes episode cover art for 12 shows with an image model. One freelancer used to review every cover with one question: "Rate it from 1 to 10." Anything 7 or above went out.

Then two things happened:

1. A second reviewer joined. On the same covers, the two often differed by 3 or 4 points. Nobody could say who was right.
2. A cover scored 9 and went live. The show name was cut off at the edge. The host saw it first.

The reviewers are not lazy. The sheet is the problem. "1 to 10" does not say what to look at, what a 6 looks like, or which mistakes are never allowed. This lesson turns that sheet into a rubric.

## The idea

### What a rubric is

A **rubric** is a scoring sheet: a list of lines, and for each line, how to score it. The [Claude docs on outcomes](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/managed-agents/define-outcomes) (a beta feature, so details may change) define it as "a markdown document describing per-criterion scoring". They also warn why each line must be clear: "The grader scores each criterion independently, so vague criteria produce noisy evaluations." A **grader** is whoever applies the rubric, a person or a model.

The test of a good rubric is simple. Two reviewers score the same image alone and get the same result.

A comparison: a rubric is like a driving test sheet. The examiner does not write "drove nicely: 7". The sheet says "stopped at the line: yes or no".

### Where the first draft can come from

Writing a rubric from nothing is slow. The Claude outcomes docs give a tip: give Claude "an example of a known-good artifact" and ask it to analyze what makes that content good, then turn the analysis into a rubric. The docs say this "often produces better results than writing criteria from scratch."

For a studio, the known-good artifact is approved work. Our advice: treat the model's list as a first draft. A person edits it, and people test it (see below).

### Yes or no, or a short scale

The [Claude docs on evaluations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests) say how a grader should answer:

- The output should be "Empirical or specific".
- Their example is to tell the grader "to output only 'correct' or 'incorrect', or to judge from a scale of 1–5".
- They add: "Purely qualitative evaluations are hard to assess quickly and at scale."

Our split:

- **Yes or no** for facts. Is the show name complete? Is the episode number right?
- **A short scale (1 to 5)** for look. Mood, polish, fit to the show's style.

### Anchor every level

A 1-to-5 scale with no examples means something different to each person. The [OpenAI evaluation docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices) recommend a "show rather than tell" policy: "providing examples of different score levels (e.g., 1, 3, and 8 out of 10)".

For images, an **anchor** is a real image pinned to a level. Keep one cover that is a 1, one that is a 3 and one that is a 5 next to the mood line. A reviewer who is unsure compares the new cover with those three.

The same docs say to "Include a pass/fail threshold in addition to the numerical score". A 4 means little until you know whether 4 passes.

### Gates, weights and partial credit

The [Anthropic post on evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) says scoring for a task "can be weighted (combined grader scores must hit a threshold), binary (all graders must pass), or a hybrid."

Our design for creators is a hybrid:

- **Gates.** Must-have lines, scored yes or no. One fail means the image fails. Example: the show name is complete and spelled right.
- **Weights.** Lines where more is better, scored 1 to 5. These add up to a score with a pass line.

Why gates? An average lets a strong line cover a weak one. Our maths: five lines scored 5, 5, 5, 5 and 3 have a mean of 23 / 5 = 4.6. If the pass line is 4.5, the image passes, even if the 3 is a cut-off name. Raising the pass line does not fix this. Only a gate does.

The same post also says: "For tasks with multiple components, build in partial credit." Our reading: keep the line-by-line results. An image that fails one gate is a near miss. An image that fails every line is far off. You want to see the difference, but the near miss still does not ship.

### Grade the image, not the path

The Anthropic post says "it's often better to grade what the agent produced, not the path it took." For a creator: score the cover, not the prompt or the tool that made it.

### One rubric per kind of job

The Claude docs say: "A given use case, or even a specific success criteria for that use case, might require several rubrics for holistic evaluation." A square cover and a vertical video still have different must-haves. Give each kind of job its own rubric.

### Test the rubric on people

The OpenAI docs recommend: "Conduct multiple rounds of detailed human review to refine the scorecard." To combine reviewers, they say "A simple way to aggregate multiple reviewers is to take consensus votes." A **consensus vote** means you go with what most reviewers chose.

Our habit: three reviewers score the same set alone. Where two differ by 2 points or more on a line, talk about those images. Make the line clearer or add an anchor, and run a new round on images nobody has scored yet.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak sheet

```text
Rate the cover from 1 to 10. 7 or above ships.
```

Two reviewers, no anchors, no must-haves. The cut-off name scored 9 because everything else looked great.

### Step 2: write the rubric

```text
EPISODE COVER RUBRIC (square, 3000x3000)

Gates (yes / no, every one must be yes)
  G1  Show name complete, spelled exactly, not cut by any edge
  G2  Episode number correct
  G3  Show name still readable when the cover is shrunk to 200x200

Weights (1 to 5, anchor covers pinned for 1, 3 and 5)
  W1  Mood fits the show's style guide
  W2  Polish: no melted edges, no odd hands, no extra objects

Pass: all gates yes AND W1 + W2 is 7 or more
```

The pass line and the sizes are our example.

### Step 3: the hard case

**The hard case is two failed covers.** Both fail, but not in the same way. The scores are our example.

```text
        Cover A                             Cover B
G1      yes                                 no  (show name misspelled)
G2      yes                                 no  (episode 41, not 14)
G3      no  (name unreadable at 200x200)    no
W1      5                                   2
W2      4                                   2
Result  FAIL                                FAIL
```

The weak reading: "Both failed, so redo both from zero." The better reading: keep the line-by-line result. Cover A misses one gate, and its weights add up to 9. Making the show name bigger is one fix. Cover B fails every gate and its weights add up to 4, so the prompt itself needs work. Neither ships, but they go to different queues. This is partial credit: the result shows how far each image is from a pass.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Must-haves are gates**, so a beautiful cover with a broken name fails.
- **Anchors** make a 3 mean the same thing to every reviewer.
- **A pass line** sits next to every score.
- **Line-by-line results** show a near miss apart from a far miss.

## Common mistakes

1. **Averaging must-have lines with look lines.** A high mood score buys back a wrong name. Fix: make must-haves yes-or-no gates.
2. **A scale with no anchors.** Each reviewer invents their own 3. Fix: pin a real image to the low, middle and top levels.
3. **One rubric for every job.** Lines that matter for one format are missing or wrong for another. Fix: give each kind of job its own rubric.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A video team writes rubric lines for vertical trailer stills. Say what you would do with each line: make it a yes-or-no gate, make it a 1-to-5 scale, rewrite it or drop it.

```text
A  The release date on the still is correct.
B  The colours feel like the show.
C  The designer used the approved prompt template.
D  The still looks good.
```

### Answer

1. **A: a gate.** It is a fact. A wrong date means the still cannot ship.
2. **B: a 1-to-5 scale with anchors.** It is about look, so pin a still for 1, 3 and 5, and add a pass line.
3. **C: drop it.** It grades the path, not the result. The Anthropic post says it is often better to grade what was produced. Check the still itself.
4. **D: rewrite it.** "Looks good" names nothing. Split it into lines you can check, such as polish and readable text.

## Recap

- A rubric is a list of lines and how to score each one. Use yes or no for facts and an anchored 1-to-5 scale for look, with a pass line next to every score.
- Make must-haves gates. An average, even with a higher pass line, lets a strong line hide a failed one. Keep the line-by-line results for partial credit.
- Start from approved work, give each kind of job its own rubric, and test it on people in several rounds before you trust it.
