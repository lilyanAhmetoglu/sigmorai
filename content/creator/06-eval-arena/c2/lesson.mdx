## Why it matters

Chapter 1 gave you criteria and targets. A **rubric** is the sheet a reviewer uses to turn a picture into a score. In Agent Citadel, chapter 3, you wrote yes-or-no lines for a review loop. This chapter goes further. You will score many images from two models, and some qualities, such as mood, do not fit a yes or no.

The test of a good rubric is simple. Two reviewers use it on the same image and give the same score.

## Where a rubric can start

Claude's Managed Agents docs describe a rubric as "a markdown document describing per-criterion scoring". That page is a beta feature, so details may change. It also gives a tip for when you have no rubric: give Claude "an example of a known-good artifact", ask it to analyze what makes that content good, and turn the analysis into a rubric. The docs say this "often produces better results than writing criteria from scratch."

The docs write this for any artifact. For a studio, the known-good artifact is an approved image. Treat the model's list as a first draft, then edit it yourself and test it on people (see below).

## Yes or no, or a short scale

Claude's guide says to be "empirical or specific". Tell the grader to output only "correct" or "incorrect", or to judge on a scale of 1 to 5. It adds that "Purely qualitative evaluations are hard to assess quickly and at scale."

A good split, which is ours:

- **Yes or no** for facts: Is the logo there? Does the label read "Fernhill"? Is the format 4:5?
- **A short scale** for look: polish, mood, fit to the brand.

## Anchor every level

A scale with no examples means a different thing to each person. OpenAI's guide recommends a "show rather than tell" policy: provide "examples of different score levels (e.g., 1, 3, and 8 out of 10)".

For images, that means pinning real images to the scale. For "mood, 1 to 5" you might keep three images on the rubric sheet: one that is a 1, one a 3 and one a 5. A reviewer who is unsure compares the new image to those three.

OpenAI also says to "include a pass/fail threshold in addition to the numerical score". A 4 means little until you know whether 4 passes.

## Gates and weights

Anthropic's post says a task can combine grader scores in three ways: "weighted (combined grader scores must hit a threshold), binary (all graders must pass), or a hybrid." It also says: "For tasks with multiple components, build in partial credit."

An example, with made-up numbers. An image gets these scores: brief fit 5, brand 5, text 5, logo 1. The mean is (5 + 5 + 5 + 1) / 4 = 4.0. If the pass line is a mean of 4.0, the image passes with a wrong logo. A client would not accept it.

A hybrid fixes this, and the design is ours:

- **Gates (binary).** Lines that must pass, such as an exact logo and exact label text. One fail means the image fails.
- **Weights (scored).** Lines where more is better, such as polish and mood. These count toward a score.

Partial credit still helps. It lets you see that a model that gets the logo and label right but misses the mood is better than one that fails all three. Keep the gates as gates, though.

## Several rubrics, not one

Claude's guide says that "A given use case, or even a specific success criteria for that use case, might require several rubrics for holistic evaluation." A packshot needs an exact product and label. A social story needs mood and a safe zone for text. Write one rubric for each kind of job.

## Test the rubric on people

OpenAI recommends to "Conduct multiple rounds of detailed human review to refine the scorecard." It also says: "A simple way to aggregate multiple reviewers is to take consensus votes."

A way to do it:

1. Have two or three people score the same 20 images alone.
2. Find the images where they differ by two points or more.
3. Talk about those images, fix the wording of the rubric, and add an anchor image if needed.
4. Score a fresh set and compare again.

Anthropic adds a point about what to grade: "it's often better to grade what the agent produced, not the path it took." For you, grade the image and not the prompt that made it. A model may reach a good image by a path you did not expect.

## What this means for your work

- **Start from approved work.** A known-good image can give you a first draft of the rubric.
- **Use yes or no for facts and a 1 to 5 scale for look.**
- **Pin an anchor image to each level** of every scale.
- **Add a pass line** next to every score.
- **Make must-haves gates**, and do not let an average hide them.
- **Write one rubric per kind of job.**
- **Test the rubric with two or three people**, then fix the lines where they differ.
