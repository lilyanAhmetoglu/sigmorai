## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Write a first message that says what you want, the details that matter and the shape of the answer.
- Read the answer as a draft, check it, and fix it with short follow-ups in the same chat.
- See when a chat has gone wrong, and start a new one with a clean summary.

## The problem

You open a chat app for the first time. It can be Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or another one. There is an empty box. You type three words and get a long, polite answer. It is not quite what you wanted, and you don't know what to do next.

Many people stop here. They think "AI is not for me", or they copy the answer without reading it. Both are mistakes. A chat is a conversation, not a search box. This lesson shows four moves. They are our summary of the vendors' advice, and they work in any chat app: **ask, read, follow up, start again**. The quotes below are from the help pages of each vendor (the company that makes the app), October 2026.

## The idea

**1. Ask like you talk to a colleague.** The [ChatGPT docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/use-chatgpt) say: "Write as if you were explaining the request to a helpful colleague." The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8114491-get-started-with-claude) gives the same advice: speak "like you would a coworker or friend". A good first message has three parts:

```text
What you want    the task and why ("a short email to my team")
The details      facts the app can't guess (names, dates, who reads it)
The shape        length, tone, format ("5 lines, friendly, no jargon")
```

Both vendors also describe the three parts:

- The ChatGPT docs: "State what you want to accomplish, add the details that change the answer, and describe the format you need."
- The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/7996857-my-prompt-isn-t-giving-me-a-helpful-answer): pretend you are writing for "someone with no background knowledge about what you are asking".

**2. Read the answer as a draft.** Every vendor warns that answers can be wrong:

- The [Gemini Apps Help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13275745): "Gemini Apps can make mistakes."
- The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8525154-claude-is-providing-incorrect-or-misleading-responses-what-s-going-on): answers "might look correct but are very mistaken."
- The ChatGPT docs: "Treat the first result as a draft you can inspect, challenge, and improve." Their checklist starts with: "Verify important numbers, names, dates, quotes, and claims."

**3. Follow up in the same chat.** You don't need a perfect first message (the docs call a message a _prompt_):

- The ChatGPT docs: "Your first prompt is only a starting point". Their examples of short follow-ups: "Make this shorter." and "What assumptions are you making?"
- The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8114491-get-started-with-claude): "Don't hesitate to refine your prompts or ask follow-up questions."
- Many apps also let you edit a message you sent and get a new answer. The Gemini Apps Help calls this "Edit your prompt".

**4. Start again when the chat goes wrong.** A chat has a size limit. The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11647753-how-do-usage-and-length-limits-work) calls it the context window, "Claude's working memory", and says that when you hit the limit "you can start a new conversation".

A very long chat can also get worse before it hits the limit. The [Anthropic engineering blog](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) calls this "context rot": "as the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model’s ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases." (Tokens are the small pieces of text the model reads.) So if a long chat starts to forget or mix up details you already fixed, a new chat is often better than one more follow-up. Our advice: when you start again, bring only what is still true. Paste a short summary of the facts and your request.

One warning: some apps have a **memory** feature. The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11817273-use-claude-s-chat-search-and-memory-to-build-on-previous-context) says Claude "can also remember context from your chats and carry it into new conversations". Our advice: if a wrong fact follows you into a new chat, check the app's memory settings.

A small comparison: a chat is like working with a new helper who is fast but has never met you. You explain the job, you check the work, you give short notes, and if the notes get messy, you start a fresh page.

## Worked example

Ana leads a small team. The Friday team meeting must move to Monday at 10:00, because two people are away on Friday. Ana wants a short message for the team.

### Step 1: the first message (before)

```text
write email meeting
```

The answer is a long, formal email about "an upcoming meeting". It has no day, no time and no reason. The app had to guess everything.

### Step 2: the first message (after)

```text
Write a short email to my team of 6.
Our Friday meeting moves to Monday at 10:00, same video link.
Reason: two people are away on Friday.
Keep it under 5 lines, friendly, and end with "Reply if Monday doesn't work."
```

What you want, the details and the shape are all there.

### Step 3: read the answer

The new answer is short and friendly. **This is the step people skip.** Ana checks every name, day and time against the facts, and they are right. But reading it, Ana sees a gap. Ana needs every reply by Thursday at 17:00, to plan the agenda. Ana forgot to say that, so the email has no deadline.

### Step 4: follow up

```text
Add one line before the end: please reply by Thursday at 17:00.
```

The next answer has everything Ana needs. One short note fixed it, and Ana didn't have to write the whole message again.

### Step 5: the hard case, a new chat that is not empty

Next week a new person joins, so the team is now 7. Ana opens a new chat for a different email. The answer says "your team of 6". Ana never typed that in this chat. **This is the hard case.** A new chat is not always a clean page. The app's memory may have saved the old team size from the last chat. Ana opens the app's memory settings, finds the old fact and deletes it. Then they ask again.

### Step 6: why it is better

- **Clear ask.** The app didn't have to guess the day, the time or the tone.
- **Checked.** Ana read the answer before sending it, and saw the missing deadline.
- **Cheap fixes.** One short follow-up added what was missing.
- **Clean memory.** The old team size was removed from memory, so it can't come back.

## Common mistakes

1. **Typing a few keywords, like a search box.** The app guesses the rest. Fix: say what you want, the details and the shape.
2. **Sending the first answer without reading it.** It can contain invented facts, or miss something you need. Fix: check every name, number, date and quote before you use it.
3. **Thinking a new chat knows nothing about you.** Some apps remember details from old chats. Fix: if a wrong detail shows up again, check the app's memory settings.

## Try it

Not graded. Open any chat app. Make this weak first message better, using the three parts.

```text
dinner ideas
```

You are cooking on Saturday for 4 people. One is vegetarian. You have about 45 minutes and a normal kitchen.

Then write one short follow-up for this problem: the ideas are good, but there is no shopping list.

### Answer

One good version:

```text
Suggest 3 dinner ideas for 4 people on Saturday.
One guest is vegetarian, so every dish must work for them.
I have about 45 minutes and a normal kitchen.
Give each idea in 2 lines: the dish and the main steps.
```

A good follow-up: "Add one shopping list for all 3 dishes." It is short, and it says exactly what to add.

## Recap

- Ask like you talk to a colleague: what you want, the details, the shape of the answer.
- Read every answer as a draft. Check names, numbers, dates and quotes, then fix it with short follow-ups.
- When a long chat starts to forget or mix up details, start a new one with a short summary of what is still true.
