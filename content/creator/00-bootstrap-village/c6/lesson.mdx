## Why it matters

A client asks for "our ad, with a famous singer's face, and don't say it's AI". Can you make it? The model may refuse. And even if it doesn't, the vendor's rules may forbid it. This lesson shows what the vendors' policies and docs say about people, rights and the origin of AI media.

**This is not legal advice.** It covers only what vendor policies and docs say. Laws differ by country. For a contract or a legal question, ask a lawyer.

## Each vendor has a usage policy

When you use a model, you agree to that vendor's rules. Two examples:

- **Anthropic Usage Policy** (effective September 15, 2025). It applies to "anyone who can submit inputs" to Anthropic's products.
- **Google Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy** (last modified December 17, 2024). Gemini's image docs say: "Your use of this generative AI service is subject to our Prohibited Use Policy."

OpenAI's image guide says: "All prompts and generated images are filtered in accordance with our content policy."

Read the policy of every model you use. The wording differs, but the rules below come up again and again.

## Rules that touch creative work

**Other people's rights.** Anthropic's policy forbids using its products to "infringe, misappropriate, or violate the intellectual property rights of a third party". Google's policy forbids content that "violates the rights of others, including privacy and intellectual property rights". Gemini's image docs add a reminder before you edit: "Make sure you have the necessary rights to any images you upload."

Example: a stock photo you have not licensed is not yours to upload as a reference.

**Real people.** Google's policy forbids "impersonating an individual (living or dead) without explicit disclosure, in order to deceive". Anthropic's policy forbids impersonating "real entities" or creating "fake personas to falsely attribute content or mislead others about its origin without consent or legal right".

**Passing AI work off as human.** Google's policy forbids "misrepresenting the provenance of generated content by claiming it was created solely by a human, in order to deceive". Anthropic's policy forbids using its products to "impersonate a human by presenting results as human-generated".

**Harmful content.** Both policies forbid sexual content involving minors. Google's also lists non-consensual intimate imagery, harassment and hate speech.

Note the word "deceive" in Google's rules. Google also says it "may make exceptions" for educational, documentary, scientific or artistic reasons. You don't decide those exceptions. The vendor does.

## What gets blocked, and what you see

Models check prompts and outputs. You will see some refusals. Plan for them.

- **OpenAI image models.** A blocked request returns the error code `moderation_blocked`. It can say whether the block came from the **input** (your prompt or images) or the **output** (the generated image). OpenAI's guide says not to retry these errors automatically "without modifying the prompt or input images".
- **Veo 3.1.** Google's docs say "Prompts that violate our terms and guidelines are blocked". Videos also pass through filters and "memorization checking processes that help mitigate privacy, copyright and bias risks". You are not charged for a blocked video.
- **People in Veo.** The `personGeneration` setting controls people in videos. When you start from an image or use reference images, only `allow_adult` is allowed. In the EU, UK, CH and MENA regions, `allow_adult` is the only allowed value.
- **Claude vision.** Claude "cannot be used to name people in images and refuses to do so". It also does not process explicit images that break Anthropic's policy.

## Provenance: a label inside the file

**Provenance** means where a piece of media came from and how it was made. Two technologies carry it.

- **C2PA Content Credentials.** C2PA is "an open technical standard" to show "the origin and edits of digital content". Its site compares Content Credentials to "a nutrition label for digital content". OpenAI's docs describe them as "signed metadata with issuer and AI-use details".
- **SynthID.** A watermark built into the media itself. Google's docs say all Nano Banana images include a SynthID watermark, and Veo videos are watermarked with SynthID. OpenAI's provenance check also looks for SynthID in images and audio.

The two behave differently when a file moves around:

| Signal                   | Where it lives             | What can remove it                                                            |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| C2PA Content Credentials | metadata in the file       | "Editing, converting, or sharing a file can remove its metadata." (OpenAI)    |
| SynthID watermark        | inside the pixels or audio | It "may survive some transformations", so not every edit removes it. (OpenAI) |

OpenAI's docs also say visible watermarks and labels are "separate from the provenance signals".

## A check is not a lie detector

OpenAI offers a provenance check for its own images and audio. It is not proof either way:

- It "isn't a general-purpose AI detector". It looks only for OpenAI's signals.
- A `not_detected` result does not prove a person made the file. The metadata may have been stripped, or another company's model may have made it.

Claude's docs say the same about vision models: Claude "cannot determine whether an image is AI-generated". Don't ask a model to judge it.

## What this means for your work

This is Sigmorai's checklist, not a vendor rule:

- **Own your inputs.** Upload only images you have the rights to use.
- **No real person without consent.** No look-alikes of real people meant to deceive.
- **Keep the credentials.** Ask how your export, edit and upload steps treat metadata. Keep an original file.
- **Don't promise "AI-free".** A failed check is not proof.
- **Read the policy, then ask a lawyer.** Policies tell you what the vendor allows. They don't answer legal questions.
