## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Review a creative brief against the vendors' usage policies: rights, real people, honest origin.
- Handle a blocked request without trying to get around the filter.
- Explain what C2PA metadata and SynthID watermarks are, what removes them, and why a provenance check is not proof.

**This is not legal advice.** It is limited to the rules written in vendor policies and docs. Laws differ by country. For a contract or a legal question, ask a lawyer.

## The problem

A city museum plans a poster and video series for a history festival. The brief has four ideas:

1. Use scans of paintings from the museum's own collection as style references.
2. Use 20 photos from a travel blog as references. Nobody asked the blogger.
3. Make a short video of a famous 19th-century writer "speaking" to visitors.
4. Export the final posters, edit them in a design tool, and post them online.

The team wants to start today. Some ideas are fine. Some break vendor rules. One needs a clear label. And one quietly removes the file's AI label.

## The idea

When you use a model, you agree to that vendor's rules. Think of them as the house rules of a rented studio: you can make almost anything, but some uses are not allowed.

**Each vendor has a usage policy.**

- The [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup) applies "to anyone who submits inputs to Anthropic's products or services". The version on the page is dated "Effective November 12, 2026".
- The [Google Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy](https://policies.google.com/terms/generative-ai/use-policy) ("Last Modified: December 17, 2024"). The [Gemini API docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/image-generation) say: "Your use of this generative AI service is subject to our Prohibited Use Policy."
- The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/image-generation) say: "All prompts and generated images are filtered in accordance with our content policy."

**Rules that touch creative work.**

- **Other people's rights.** Anthropic forbids using its products to "Infringe, misappropriate, or violate the intellectual property rights of a third party". Google forbids content that "Violates the rights of others, including privacy and intellectual property rights". The Gemini API docs add: "Make sure you have the necessary rights to any images you upload."
- **Real people.** Google forbids "Impersonating an individual (living or dead) without explicit disclosure, in order to deceive". Anthropic forbids impersonating "real individuals or entities", or creating "fake personas", to "mislead audiences about its origin". Anthropic adds: "This does not prohibit clearly disclosed parody, satire, or fiction."
- **Honest origin.** Google forbids "Misrepresenting the provenance of generated content by claiming it was created solely by a human, in order to deceive".
- **Harmful content.** Both policies forbid sexual content involving minors. Both also list non-consensual intimate imagery and harassment.
- **Filters.** Google forbids "Circumvention of abuse protections or safety filters".

Google also says it "may make exceptions" for educational, documentary, scientific or artistic reasons. You don't decide those exceptions. The vendor does.

**What gets blocked, and what you see.**

- **OpenAI image models.** A blocked request returns the code `moderation_blocked`. It can include a `moderation_stage`: `input` (your prompt or images) or the output. The OpenAI docs say: "Don't automatically retry these errors without modifying the prompt or input images."
- **Veo 3.1.** The [Veo docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/veo) say: "Prompts that violate our terms and guidelines are blocked." Videos also pass "safety filters and memorization checking processes that help mitigate privacy, copyright and bias risks". You are not charged for a blocked video.
- **People in Veo.** The `personGeneration` setting controls people in videos. From an image or with reference images, only `allow_adult` is allowed. "In EU, UK, CH, MENA locations, allow_adult is the only allowed value".
- **Claude vision.** The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision) say Claude "cannot be used to name people in images and refuses to do so". It also does not process explicit images that break Anthropic's policy.

**Provenance: a label inside the file.** **Provenance** means where media came from and how it was made. Two signals carry it:

- **C2PA Content Credentials.** [C2PA](https://c2pa.org) is "an open technical standard" to "establish the origin and edits of digital content". Its site says they work "like a nutrition label for digital content". The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/content-provenance) call them "Signed metadata with issuer and AI-use details".
- **SynthID.** A watermark inside the media itself. The Gemini API docs say: "All generated images include a SynthID watermark." Veo videos "are watermarked using SynthID". OpenAI's provenance check also looks for SynthID in images and audio.

| Signal                   | Where it lives             | What can remove it                                                         |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| C2PA Content Credentials | metadata in the file       | "Editing, converting, or sharing a file can remove its metadata." (OpenAI) |
| SynthID watermark        | inside the pixels or audio | It "may survive some transformations", so not every edit removes it.       |

The OpenAI docs add: "Visible watermarks and labels are separate from the provenance signals checked by the API."

**A check is not a lie detector.** OpenAI's provenance check "isn't a general-purpose AI detector". It checks for OpenAI's own signals only. A `not_detected` result means "the tool didn't find supported signals". The metadata may have been stripped, and the tool "doesn't currently detect content generated by another company's AI model". The Claude docs say the same about vision: Claude "cannot determine whether an image is AI-generated".

## Worked example

### Step 1: the brief before

```text
1. Our own painting scans as style references.
2. 20 travel-blog photos as references (no permission).
3. Video: famous 19th-century writer "speaking".
4. Export, edit in a design tool, post online.
```

### Step 2: check each item against a policy line

```text
1. Own scans     -> GO. The museum owns the rights.
2. Blog photos   -> STOP. "Make sure you have the necessary
                    rights to any images you upload."
                    Ask the blogger, license them, or use own photos.
3. Writer video  -> CHANGE. Google forbids impersonating a person
                    "(living or dead) without explicit disclosure,
                    in order to deceive". Label it clearly on
                    screen as an AI reenactment. The vendor may
                    still block it.
4. Export + edit -> CHANGE. Editing can remove C2PA metadata.
                    Keep the original files. Label the posts
                    if your platform or client asks.
```

**The hard case is item 3.** The writer is dead, but Google's rule covers "living or dead". The key words are "explicit disclosure" and "deceive". A video that says clearly "AI reenactment" is not trying to deceive. Still, this is the vendor's call, not yours, and a law in your country may say more. If the request is blocked, change the idea. Don't rephrase the prompt to slip past the filter.

### Step 3: the brief after

```text
1. Own painting scans as references.              GO
2. Own photos of the city, or licensed ones.      GO after licence
3. Writer video, labelled "AI reenactment".       TEST, then review
4. Keep originals; label posts where needed.      GO
```

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Every input is owned or licensed.**
- **No one is misled.** The reenactment says what it is.
- **The origin trail is kept.** Original files keep their metadata.
- **Blocks are handled honestly.** A block means change the request, not hide it.

## Common mistakes

1. **"It's only a reference."** A reference image is still an upload. Fix: use only images you own or have a licence for.
2. **Rewording a blocked prompt until it passes.** Google forbids getting around safety filters. Fix: read why it was blocked, then change the idea.
3. **Treating "no signal found" as proof.** Metadata can be stripped, and a check only finds its own vendor's signals. Fix: keep original files, and never promise that a file is "not AI".

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Mark each request GO, CHANGE or STOP, and give one reason.

```text
a. A shop asks for a product video made from photos its
   own team shot.
b. A band asks for a poster "in the exact photo" of a
   magazine cover they found online.
c. A client asks you to strip all metadata from AI images
   and post them as "hand-painted by our artist".
```

### Answer

- **a. GO.** The shop owns the photos.
- **b. STOP or CHANGE.** The magazine photo is not theirs to upload. Get a licence, or use their own photos.
- **c. STOP.** Google forbids claiming generated content "was created solely by a human, in order to deceive". Offer honest wording, such as "made with AI tools".

## Recap

- Read each vendor's usage policy. Use only inputs you have the rights to, and don't impersonate real people or hide AI origin to deceive.
- When a request is blocked, change the request. Don't try to get past the filter.
- C2PA metadata can be removed by editing or sharing; SynthID lives inside the media. A "not detected" result is not proof that a person made a file.
