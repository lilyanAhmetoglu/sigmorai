## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Spot bias in an answer: who is left out, or judged by a group and not by the facts.
- Say when AI helped you, and know when you must say it.
- Check the claims in an answer, and own the result you send.

## The problem

You now use a chat app for real tasks. The answers are fast and they sound good. But two new problems show up, and they are not about speed.

The first is **fairness**. An answer can treat people unfairly. It can guess things about a person from their name, age or gender. The second is **honesty**. Who wrote this text? Is this fact true? Who is to blame when it is wrong?

These are not only problems for experts. Every person who sends AI text to other people meets them. This lesson gives you four simple habits. The quotes below are from the docs and help pages of each vendor (the company that makes the app), and from NIST, a US standards body, October 2026.

## The idea

**1. Answers can carry bias.** Bias means an answer treats a person or a group unfairly, for example with a stereotype (a fixed, unfair idea about a group, like "old people can't use phones"). Models learn from huge amounts of human text, and human text has bias in it.

The vendors say this in plain words. The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/safety-best-practices) name it among the limits of language models: "From hallucinating inaccurate information, to offensive outputs, to bias, and much more, language models may not be suitable for every use case without significant modifications." The [Google AI docs](https://ai.google.dev/responsible/docs/evaluation) for model builders list tests that check "if a model conveys unintended socio-cultural biases". One test set covers "biases such as race, religion, or age".

[NIST](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-risk-management-framework-faqs) lists the traits of a trustworthy AI system. One of them is "fair with harmful bias managed". Managed means someone looks for it and deals with it. As a user, that someone is often you.

**How to spot it.** This checklist is ours:

```text
1. Who is in the answer, and who is missing?
2. Is a person judged by a group (age, gender, origin)
   and not by what they did or can do?
3. Swap test: change only the name or age, ask again.
   Does the answer change? Then the answer leans.
```

The [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup) draws two lines here:

- It does not allow using Claude to "engage in or promote discriminatory practices" against people because of traits like race, religion, gender or disability.
- When AI helps decide about a person's job, home, loan or school place, the policy calls it a "high-risk" use. Then "a qualified person must meaningfully review" the AI's advice before it is used.

So decisions about people stay with people.

**2. Be open that AI helped.** There is no one rule for every place. A school, a job, a club or a contest can each have its own rule. Our advice: read the rule of the place first. If there is no rule and you are not sure, ask, or just say it.

The Anthropic Usage Policy is clear on three points:

- Don't "plagiarize or submit AI-assisted work without proper permission or attribution". To plagiarize is to hand in someone else's work as your own. Attribution means you say where the work came from.
- No fake people: no "fake reviews, or bots that claim to be human".
- For the high-risk uses above, the person who gets the advice "must be clearly told that AI was used to produce it."

**3. Check the claims.** An answer can sound sure and still be wrong. The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8525154-claude-is-providing-incorrect-or-misleading-responses-what-s-going-on) warns that Claude "can display quotes that may look authoritative or sound convincing, but are not grounded in fact." When an answer cites web pages, it says you "should review Claude's cited sources".

The [ChatGPT docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/use-chatgpt) give a short list: "Verify important numbers, names, dates, quotes, and claims." They also suggest asking the app: "Cite the source for each major claim." Then you open that source yourself. A source the app names is a lead, not proof.

**4. You send it, you own it.** NIST calls a trustworthy system "accountable and transparent" (accountable: someone answers for it; transparent: people can see how it works). The ChatGPT docs say: "A polished response can still be incomplete or wrong, so review the details that matter before you use or share it." For its browser helper, the [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/12902428-use-claude-in-chrome-safely) says you "remain responsible" for what it does for you, including "messages sent". Our rule from this: the person who checks and sends the text answers for it. A mistake in your message is your mistake, even if an app wrote the first draft.

A small comparison: an AI draft is like a page a stranger hands you. You can use it. But before you sign your name under it, you read every line.

## Worked example

Noor leads a small customer support team at a phone shop. Noor is making a one-page guide for two new team members, and uses a chat app to help.

### Step 1: ask for help with a sorting rule

Noor wants a simple rule for which customer emails to answer first. To test it, Noor pastes four real emails and asks:

```text
Here are four customer emails. Rate each one 1-3 for how
urgent it is (3 = answer today). One line of reason each.
```

The app gives a rating and a reason for each email.

### Step 2: the hard case, the swap test

The ratings look sensible. **This is the hard case**, because nothing looks wrong. But one reason says the customer "may need extra help with technology". The email does not say that. The only thing that could suggest it is the customer's name.

So Noor runs the swap test. Same email, new chat, but a different name, and nothing else changed. Now the rating and the reason change. The answer leaned on the name, not on the problem in the email.

```text
Same email, name A:  urgency 3, "may need extra help"
Same email, name B:  urgency 2, no such line
```

Noor's fix: the guide gets a written rule based only on the problem, like "no phone at all = answer today". Names never go into the rating step, and a person on the team makes each final call.

### Step 3: check the facts in the draft

Noor asks the app to draft the guide from Noor's notes. The draft says new staff can give a refund "within 30 days". Noor checks the shop's own refund policy. It says 14 days. The app filled a gap with a common number. Noor copies the number from the policy.

### Step 4: say how it was made

The shop has a rule: documents made with AI help say so. Noor adds one line at the bottom:

```text
Drafted with a chat app from Noor's notes. Checked by Noor,
12 Oct. Questions: ask Noor.
```

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Fair.** The rule looks at the problem, not at the person's name.
- **True.** The refund number comes from the policy, not from the app.
- **Honest and owned.** Readers know how the guide was made, and who answers for it.

## Common mistakes

1. **Testing only one swap.** One name swap can miss a lean on age or town. Fix: swap one thing at a time (name, then age, then town) and compare.
2. **Hiding AI help when a rule asks you to say it.** Fix: read the rule of the school, job or contest first. If you're not sure, ask.
3. **Letting the app choose between people.** Fix: let it help you write a clear rule. A person makes the decision about another person.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer. For each case, decide: say that AI helped, no need to say it, or don't do it.

```text
a. An app fixes the spelling in a birthday card to your aunt.
b. Your class rule says "no AI for homework". You want an app
   to write your history essay.
c. You make a picture of a flooded street with an app, and want
   to post it in the town news group.
d. Your team rule says reports must name the tools used. An app
   drafted part of your monthly report.
```

### Answer

- **a. No need.** A spelling fix in a private card changes nothing for anyone. (This is our view; your own rules may differ.)
- **b. Don't do it.** The rule says no. Write it yourself, or ask the teacher first. Handing in the essay as your own would break the rule.
- **c. Say it, clearly, or don't post it.** A made-up picture in a news group can look like a real event. People may believe the street really flooded.
- **d. Say it.** Your team asked for it. Add one line that names the tool and says you checked the text.

## Recap

- Answers can carry bias. Ask who is missing, who is judged by a group, and use the swap test. Decisions about people stay with people.
- Be open about AI help. Follow the rule of the place, never pass AI work off as yours where that matters, and never invent people or reviews.
- Check every important claim in the original source, and own what you send.
