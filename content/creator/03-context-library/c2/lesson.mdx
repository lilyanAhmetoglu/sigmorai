## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Pick a small reference set from a large library: one job and one subject per image.
- Build a style set that agrees on palette, light and mood, and an object set that varies what you do not want copied.
- Retire old references and test a new set against the old one.

## The problem

A sneaker brand, Loop Runners, has 900 reference images from six years of shoots. A designer needs a spring ad: the new white shoe and a fresh, bright look.

The designer drags in eight images: two product shots, a moody black-and-white photo, a neon street scene, a pastel drawing, and three photos of the shoe on the same grey stairs. The results are a mess. The colours fight. And every image shows grey stairs, though the brief never asked for stairs.

The library is fine. The **set** is the problem. In this lesson we use two words of our own:

- **Library:** every reference image you own for a brand.
- **Set:** the few images you pick from the library for one job.

You choose the set. The model never sees the rest.

## The idea

**One image, one job, one subject.** Each reference does one job: object, character or style. The [Runway docs](https://docs.dev.runwayml.com/recipes/reference-media) list "One subject per reference" as a general rule. A photo where two or more subjects fight for attention is a weak reference. A shoe on a busy shop shelf is a poor object reference. The other shoes compete with it.

The Runway guide is written for its own Recipes. Using its rules with any tool is our method. It gives these general rules:

- "use the highest-quality source you have". Low-resolution or heavily compressed references "degrade the output".
- The subject should be "clearly the focus, free of clutter and distractions".
- "even, neutral lighting reproduces most faithfully". Harsh shadows and colour casts "can carry into the result".

For product images it adds: "Center the product and keep it unobstructed", "Prefer a clean or plain background", and "Avoid heavy reflections, watermarks, or overlaid text on the product." So a sharp, large photo with a stock watermark still fails. Size is only one rule.

**A style set agrees.** The Runway docs give three rules for style references:

- "Choose references that share a consistent palette, lighting, and mood."
- "Provide a few complementary images rather than one — a small, cohesive set defines the style more reliably."
- "Don't mix conflicting aesthetics in a single request; generate separately and curate."

Think of a choir. Three singers on the same note sound strong. Three singers on three songs sound like noise.

**The model limit is a ceiling, not a target.** The [Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/image-generation) say Nano Banana models can mix "up to 14 reference images", and Nano Banana Pro takes "Up to 3 images to be used as style references". The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/image-generation) say: "You can use one or more images as a reference to generate a new image." The docs give a maximum, not a target. Pick the fewest images that do the job.

**An object set varies the rest.** The model copies what all your references share. The [Claude prompting docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices) ask for text examples that are "Diverse": they should cover edge cases and vary, so the model does not learn a pattern you did not mean. That advice is for text. Using the idea for pictures is our reading:

```text
keep the SAME   what you want copied      the shoe, its logo, its sole colour
make DIFFERENT  what you do not want      the floor, the background, the props
```

**References age.** A logo changes. A product gets a new design. These habits are ours:

- Mark the old file **retired**, and note what replaced it.
- Move it to an **archive**. Do not delete it: an old job may need it.
- Keep it **out of every set**. No model picks "the newest logo" by itself.
- Treat a new set as a new version. Test it before you switch.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the set before

```text
S1  shoe-white-front.jpg     shoe on grey stairs
S2  shoe-white-side.jpg      shoe on grey stairs
S3  shoe-white-top.jpg       shoe on grey stairs
S4  bw-moody.jpg             black and white, hard shadows
S5  neon-street.jpg          blue and pink neon at night
S6  pastel-drawing.png       soft pastel illustration
S7  logo-2020.png            old logo
S8  logo-2025.png            current logo
```

What goes wrong:

- The three object photos share the stairs, so the model copies the stairs.
- S4, S5 and S6 are three clashing looks.
- Two logos in one set: the model may mix them.

### Step 2: fix the object set

Keep the shoe the same. Change the surface:

```text
S1  shoe-white-front.jpg     plain white background, even light
S2  shoe-white-side-sand.jpg on pale sand, even light
S3  shoe-white-top-wood.jpg  on a light wood floor, even light
```

All three show the same shoe, logo and sole. Nothing else repeats.

### Step 3: build a style set that agrees

The library has three photos from one spring shoot: soft morning light, mint and peach colours, different props. They share palette, light and mood. They become the style set. The neon and the pastel drawing stay out. If the client wants those looks too, make them as separate jobs, then choose the best results.

### Step 4: retire the old logo

```text
logo-2020.png   status: retired, replaced by logo-2025.png, moved to archive
logo-2025.png   status: approved
```

Only the 2025 logo goes into the set.

### Step 5: the hard case, a new style set next season

The art director proposes a new summer style set. Is it better? Do not roll it out to all the work and hope the client is happy. Do not split one campaign between two sets either: that gives one campaign two looks. Our habit: run the same three past briefs on the old set and on the new set. Put the results side by side and choose.

### Step 6: write a card for each reference

A file name says little. Keep a short card with each image in the library (our method):

```text
File:    shoe-white-front.jpg
Job:     object
Copy:    shoe shape, logo position, sole colour
Ignore:  the white background
Status:  approved
Quality: 4000 px long side, plain backdrop, no stock mark
```

The "Ignore" line can also go into the prompt.

### Why it is better

- **Each image has one job and one subject.**
- **The style set agrees**, so the look is clear.
- **The object set varies the surface**, so only the shoe is copied.
- **Old files are retired and archived**, not mixed into sets.

## Common mistakes

1. **Mixing looks "for variety".** Clashing references pull the result in many directions. Fix: one cohesive style per request. Make other looks as separate jobs and pick the best.
2. **Filling the model's limit.** Extra images keep the shared detail, and they cost more to read. Fix: the fewest images that do the job.
3. **Writing around a bad set.** "No stairs" in the prompt does not fix stairs in every photo. Fix: change the photos so they do not share it.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A candle brand needs a cosy winter style set. The library has: W1 (warm candle glow, amber and brown), W2 (warm fireplace light, amber and brown), W3 (cold blue snow scene), W4 (amber lamp light, brown wool, different props), W5 (a sharp photo with a large stock watermark). Which images make the style set, and why do the others stay out?

### Answer

- **Set: W1, W2 and W4.** They share the warm palette, the soft light and the cosy mood, with different props. Three images is a small, cohesive set.
- **W3 stays out.** The cold blue look clashes with the warm one. Make it as a separate job if the client wants it.
- **W5 stays out.** A watermark can carry into the result, however sharp the photo is.

## Recap

- Send a small set, not the library. One job and one subject per image, clear and evenly lit, with no watermark.
- A style set agrees on palette, light and mood. An object set keeps the object the same and varies everything else.
- Retire and archive old references. Test a new set side by side with the old one, using the same briefs.
