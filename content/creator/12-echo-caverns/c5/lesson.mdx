## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Say what a transcript has and what a subtitle file has, and pick a model that gives the output you need.
- Turn word times from a transcript into subtitle cues that people can read.
- Handle names, filler words, sounds and overlapping speech on purpose.

## The problem

A gardening channel posts a 5-minute video about planting tomatoes. The presenter talks, and a neighbour sometimes laughs or jumps in. The channel wants subtitles that viewers can switch on.

The team has a transcript with a time for every word. They are not sure how to turn it into subtitles. Their first try put a whole paragraph on screen for 12 seconds.

The models and limits below are from each vendor's page in October 2026.

## The idea

### Transcript, cue and subtitle file

Some words first (our plain glosses):

- A **transcript** is the text of what was said.
- A **timestamp** is the time in the audio when a word or part starts or ends.
- A **cue** is one subtitle block on screen, with a start time and an end time.
- **SRT** and **VTT** are two subtitle file types. A video player reads them to show the cues.
- **Captions** and **subtitles** mean the same thing in this lesson.

The [ElevenLabs subtitles docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/products/subtitles) compare the two:

| Feature (ElevenLabs)           | Transcripts | Subtitles                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Word-level timestamps          | Yes         | No - only start/end times of cues                                |
| Speaker names/labels           | Yes         | No                                                               |
| Constraints                    | No          | Yes - characters per line, lines on screen at once, cue duration |
| Overlapping segments supported | Yes         | No                                                               |

### OpenAI: pick the model by the output

The [OpenAI guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/speech-to-text) says to start with `gpt-transcribe`. It says to "Use a specialized model only if you need speaker labels, word timestamps, subtitle formats, or translation into English."

- **Timestamps:** "Use `whisper-1` when you need word or segment timestamps." Set `response_format` to `verbose_json` and `timestamp_granularities[]` to `word`, `segment` or both. The guide says this parameter "is only supported for `whisper-1`".
- **Formats:** the [API reference](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/audio/subresources/transcriptions/methods/create) lists `json`, `text`, `srt`, `verbose_json`, `vtt` and `diarized_json`. But: "For `gpt-4o-transcribe` and `gpt-4o-mini-transcribe`, the only supported format is `json`."
- **Speed:** "There is no additional latency for segment timestamps, but generating word timestamps incurs additional latency." **Latency** is waiting time.

### Who is speaking

**Diarisation** means marking which speaker said each part (our plain gloss).

- **OpenAI:** `gpt-4o-transcribe-diarize` returns `diarized_json`. You can "supply up to four short audio references with `known_speaker_names[]`", with clips "between 2–10 seconds". Two limits: this model "doesn't support prompts". Also, `timestamp_granularities` "is not available" for it.
- **ElevenLabs:** the [speech to text docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/speech-to-text) list "Speaker diarization, up to 32 speakers" and "Precise word-level timestamps" for Scribe v2. Each word has a `start`, an `end` and a `speaker_id`.
- **Google:** with Gemini you ask in the prompt. The [Gemini audio docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/audio) show "Identify distinct speakers (e.g., Speaker 1, Speaker 2)." and "Provide accurate timestamps for each segment (Format: MM:SS)." You can also ask for one part: "Provide a transcript from 02:30 to 03:29."

### Names and rare words

- **OpenAI** (`gpt-transcribe`): "Use `keywords` for literal terms you expect to hear." and "Use `prompt` for unstructured context about the recording." The warning: "Keywords are hints, not required output. Include only relevant terms, and evaluate whether they improve accuracy without causing unspoken terms to appear."
- **ElevenLabs:** **keyterm prompting** helps with "product names, names, or other specific terms". "Batch supports up to 1000 keyterms (50 characters each)". It costs extra.

### Clean or word for word

- **ElevenLabs:** with `no_verbatim`, "the model removes filler words, false starts and disfluencies". The docs call this "suitable for subtitles".
- **OpenAI:** a prompt can help with "Preserving punctuation, capitalization, and filler words."

ElevenLabs results can also include `audio_event` items: "Non-speech sounds like laughter or applause".

### Edit the cues

The ElevenLabs Subtitle Editor "uses red and green colors" to show if cues respect "characters per line, lines on screen, and cue length". You can change these rules. You can drag a cue's start and end, type exact times, split and merge cues, and export "SRT" or "VTT".

One trap: "the transcript and subtitles for a video are completely separate from each other". So a fix in one place is not a fix in the other.

## Worked example

The tomato video. The team used Scribe v2, so every word has a start and an end. Here is one short part (made-up times):

```text
word        start   end     speaker
Push        12.10   12.38   speaker_0
the         12.38   12.50   speaker_0
stem        12.50   12.86   speaker_0
deep,       12.86   13.30   speaker_0
right       13.62   13.90   speaker_0
up          13.90   14.08   speaker_0
to          14.08   14.20   speaker_0
the         14.20   14.30   speaker_0
first       14.30   14.62   speaker_0
leaves.     14.62   15.20   speaker_0
(laughter)  14.90   15.60   audio_event
Finally!    15.10   15.70   speaker_1
```

### Step 1: set our cue rules

These numbers are ours, for this channel. Set them in the editor's rules:

```text
max 2 lines per cue
max 42 characters per line
min 1 second on screen
```

### Step 2: group words into cues (before → after)

Before: one cue for the whole paragraph, on screen for 12 seconds. Nobody can read that.

After: start a new cue at a sentence end or a clear pause. There is a pause between "deep," (ends 13.30) and "right" (starts 13.62).

```text
Cue 1   12.10 -> 13.30   Push the stem deep,
Cue 2   13.62 -> 15.20   right up to the first leaves.
```

Each cue starts at its first word's start and ends at its last word's end (our method). Both cues fit our rules: one line, under 42 characters, more than 1 second.

### Step 3: the hard case

**This is the hard case.** At 14.90 the neighbour laughs, and at 15.10 says "Finally!" while the presenter is still talking. The table says subtitle cues don't support overlapping segments. So two cues can't be on screen at the same time.

Our fix:

- Let Cue 2 end on time at 15.20.
- Put "Finally!" in its own cue right after it.
- Decide on the laughter: this channel keeps it, in brackets.
- Check that the new cue is on screen for at least 1 second.

```text
Cue 3   15.20 -> 16.20   (laughs) Finally!
```

"Finally!" ends at 15.70, so the cue would only last 0.5 seconds. We keep it on screen until 16.20 to meet our 1-second rule.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Short cues follow the speech**, so a viewer can read each one.
- **Cues never overlap**, so the player shows one at a time.
- **Sounds are kept on purpose**, not by accident.

## Common mistakes

1. **One cue for a whole paragraph.** Viewers can't read it in time. Fix: split at sentence ends and pauses, then check your line rules.
2. **Building cues from MM:SS times alone.** MM:SS counts whole seconds, so a cue can start up to a second early or late (our note). Fix: check the times against the video.
3. **Keeping or dropping sounds by chance.** Laughter or music may come back as an event. Fix: choose a rule for the channel and use it every time.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Here are word times from a short bread video (made up):

```text
Knead   3.00 3.40 | the 3.40 3.52 | dough 3.52 3.90 | for 3.90 4.05
ten     4.05 4.30 | minutes. 4.30 4.90
(pause)
Then    5.60 5.80 | let 5.80 5.95 | it 5.95 6.05 | rest. 6.05 6.40
```

Use our rules: max 42 characters per line, min 1 second on screen.

1. Make the cues, with start and end times.
2. Does any cue break a rule? If yes, fix it.

### Answer

1. Two cues, split at the sentence end and the pause:

   ```text
   Cue 1   3.00 -> 4.90   Knead the dough for ten minutes.
   Cue 2   5.60 -> 6.40   Then let it rest.
   ```

2. Cue 2 is only 0.8 seconds. Keep it on screen until 6.60, so it lasts 1 second. Both lines are under 42 characters.

Your times can differ a little. Check that no cue is too short and no two cues overlap.

## Recap

- A transcript has word times and speakers. A subtitle file has cues with start and end times, and rules for length.
- Pick the model by the output: on OpenAI, `whisper-1` gives word or segment timestamps, and some models give `json` only.
- Build cues from word times, keep them short and apart, decide on sounds on purpose, and fix text in both the transcript and the subtitles.
