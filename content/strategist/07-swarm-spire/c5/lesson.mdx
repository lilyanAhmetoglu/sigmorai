## Why it matters

Giving people access is not adoption. **Adoption** means that people use the tool in their real work, again and again, on tasks where it helps. Many rollouts open access, see a spike in the first week, and then watch use fade.

This chapter covers two things: how to build the habits that keep use going, and how to read adoption data without fooling yourself.

## Adoption spreads person to person

Anthropic's Claude Code champion kit says that adoption of a developer tool rarely happens because of a rollout announcement. It happens because someone on the team starts using the tool well, talks about it openly, and makes it easy for others to follow. The kit is written for engineers, but the idea holds for any team.

It describes the **champion** role as three behaviors:

1. **Share what you discover.** Post prompts, screenshots and small wins where your team already reads.
2. **Be the person people ask.** Answer with the actual prompt you used, so a colleague can copy it.
3. **Grow the circle.** Set up a few light, repeating habits, such as a channel or a weekly thread.

The kit gives a reason for the second point: a concrete, runnable example closes the gap between curiosity and a first successful use, "which is where most adoption efforts stall".

Example: a sales manager posts, "For account prep I paste the last three emails and ask for open questions. Here is the exact request." A colleague can use it in five minutes.

## Share techniques, not results

The kit says the best posts describe a technique a colleague can reuse tomorrow, not an outcome that is already done. Techniques compound as they spread. Status updates do not.

It also says to post where the team already reads: the team channel, the notes attached to finished work, and weekly updates. The goal is to place examples in the path of normal work, not to build a new destination.

## Keep the champion's cost small

The kit sets a time budget. It lists about 15 minutes a week for posting wins, about 20 minutes for answering questions, and about 5 minutes for a weekly show-and-tell thread. Pairing sessions are optional, from 0 to 30 minutes. It says the role should stay "a multiplier on your existing work rather than an additional support responsibility".

That is about 40 minutes a week for the core work. Our reading: if the role grows into a second job, it is unlikely to last.

## A 30-day plan with signals

The kit suggests four weeks, each with a signal that tells you it is working:

| Week | Action                                         | Signal                                       |
| ---- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Seed the channel with 2 or 3 examples          | A few reactions and at least one question    |
| 2    | Start the weekly thread and answer publicly    | Someone else posts an example                |
| 3    | Offer short pairing sessions and a pinned FAQ  | The same colleagues come back, not just once |
| 4    | Name a second champion and report to your lead | Others answer questions                      |

The kit says adoption that depends on a single person is fragile. Week 4 of its plan names a second champion.

## Read the numbers

Both vendors' dashboards show adoption. OpenAI's page also warns about reading them too simply.

Anthropic's analytics page has an adoption chart with daily active users and daily sessions. It says dips in usage may point to friction or issues. It also says the leaderboard helps find heavy users who can share techniques, give feedback and help onboard others.

OpenAI's Usage Insights page tells admins to read Messages, Credits and Active users together. Its advice on thin use: "If a category has few active users, ask them how they're using it and where they're having difficulty." They may have a workflow worth sharing, or need more support. It also says a message count does not tell you how much work the team finished. Chapter 3 of the previous realm made the same point about activity.

So a number is a reason to ask a question, not an answer:

- Active users falling: ask what got in the way.
- One team far behind another: ask that team's manager and users.
- A few very active users: ask what they do that others could copy.

OpenAI's analytics page adds a privacy note. Downloaded reports are identifiable organizational data, so apply your organization's access, storage and retention policy to them.

Example: 30 people have access and 9 are active. Don't send a reminder. Ask the 9 what works, and ask the 21 what blocks them.

## Define "active" for your team

A login is not a habit. This definition is our own tool for this course: count a person as adopted when they use a named workflow at least once a week for a month. Week 3 of the kit's plan looks for the same thing: the same people returning, not trying once and stopping.

## Key points

- Adoption spreads through people who share reusable techniques, not through announcements.
- Keep the champion role to about 40 minutes a week, and name a second champion.
- Post examples where the team already reads, and include the exact request used.
- Read adoption numbers as questions to ask, not as scores.
- Define "active" as repeat use of a named workflow, and protect staff data in reports.
