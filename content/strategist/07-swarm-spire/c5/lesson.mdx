## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Set up a light champion role: what the person does, how much time it takes, and how it spreads to a second person.
- Read an adoption dashboard as a list of questions to ask people, not as a score.
- Spot two common reading errors: counting accounts as use, and scaling up sampled numbers.

## The problem

A travel group has 140 booking agents in 5 offices. Six weeks ago every agent got an AI assistant for customer emails, trip summaries and visa checks. IT sent one launch email.

The IT report says: "140 accounts created, 100% adoption." The sales director is not sure. Some offices talk about the tool every day. In others, nobody mentions it. The director wants a plan for the next two months, and a clear way to read the numbers each month.

**Adoption** means people use the tool in their real work, again and again, on tasks where it helps. An account is only a door. This lesson shows how use spreads from person to person, and how to read the dashboards honestly. The guidance below is from the Claude Code docs and the OpenAI ChatGPT Enterprise docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Use spreads through people.** The Claude Code [champion kit](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/champion-kit) says: "Adoption of a developer tool rarely happens because of a rollout announcement." It happens when someone on the team uses the tool well, talks about it openly, and helps others copy it. The kit is written for engineers. Our reading: the same pattern fits a sales or service team.

The kit calls this person a **champion** and describes three behaviours:

1. **Share what you discover.** Post prompts, screenshots and small wins in channels people already read.
2. **Be the person people ask.** Reply with the exact prompt you used, so a colleague can run it on their own task. The kit says a concrete example closes the gap to a first success, "which is where most adoption efforts stall".
3. **Grow the circle.** Start a few light, repeating habits, such as a channel or a weekly thread, so things keep going without you.

**Share a method, not a result.** The kit says the best posts describe "a technique a colleague can reuse tomorrow rather than an outcome that is already complete". Post where people already read, so the examples sit "in the path of normal work". Short is better: the kit says a long write-up tends to be saved and forgotten, while a short post with a screenshot tends to be copied and tried.

**Keep the role small.** The kit gives a weekly time budget:

```text
Posting wins and prompts           about 15 minutes
Answering questions in a channel   about 20 minutes
Weekly show-and-tell thread        about 5 minutes
Optional pairing                   0 to 30 minutes
```

Our maths: 15 + 20 + 5 = 40, so the core tasks take about 40 minutes a week. The kit wants the activities to "fit inside a normal working week", and the role should stay "a multiplier on your existing work rather than an additional support responsibility". Pairing is only for colleagues who are blocked.

**Plan for the second champion.** The kit warns: "Adoption that depends on a single person is fragile." Its thirty-day plan has a signal for each week:

- **Week 1:** seed the channel with two or three examples. Signal: a few reactions and at least one question.
- **Week 2:** start the weekly thread. Signal: someone other than you posts an example.
- **Week 3:** short pairing sessions and a pinned FAQ. Signal: the same colleagues come back, not try once and stop.
- **Week 4:** name a second champion and report to your lead. Signal: other people answer the questions.

**Numbers are questions.** Both vendors give admins a dashboard.

- The [Claude Code analytics page](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/analytics) shows an adoption chart with daily active users and sessions. It says to watch for "Dips in usage that may indicate friction or issues". Its leaderboard helps find heavy users who can share techniques, give feedback and help new users start.
- The OpenAI [Usage Insights page](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/usage-insights) says to read Messages, Credits and Active users together. It warns: "A message count doesn't tell you how much work the team finished." It also says to "Pair usage and spend with team records" to check effort, quality and results.

For a category with few users, the OpenAI page says: "ask them how they're using it and where they're having difficulty." Maybe they found a method others could copy. Maybe they need more help.

**Two reading traps.** The Insights page names two more:

- **Sampled numbers.** If a sampling notice appears, the counts are from a sample. The page says: "Don't scale them up to estimate total workspace activity."
- **Different shares.** Message share, credit share and token share describe different things. The page says to keep those labels as they are when you compare or share a result.

The [OpenAI workspace analytics page](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/workspace-analytics) adds a privacy note: "Treat downloaded reports as identifiable organizational data." Apply your company's access, storage and retention rules to them.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak plan

```text
Adoption plan, months 3-4
  Measure:   accounts created (140 of 140 = 100%)
  Actions:   a monthly newsletter from the sales director
             a second reminder email to all agents
  Champions: none
```

What goes wrong:

- **Accounts are not use.** 140 accounts says nothing about who uses the tool each week.
- **Messages from the top.** The kit says announcements rarely drive adoption. A newsletter from the director is one more announcement.
- **No people in the offices.** Nobody shows colleagues a working prompt on a real booking.

### Step 2: the good plan

```text
Champions: one per office (5), each with about 40 minutes a week
  - posts one real prompt + screenshot in the office channel
  - answers questions in the channel, not in private
  - runs a 5-minute Friday thread: "What did the assistant help with?"
  By week 4: each office names a second champion.

Measure each month:
  weekly active users per office (not accounts)
  for each use case: active users AND messages, read together
  one team record per use case: e.g. time to send a trip summary,
  and how many summaries agents had to correct
```

### Step 3: read the first month

```text
Weekly active users, month 3 (28 agents in each office)
  Office A  25 of 28     Office B  11 of 28
  Office C  23 of 28     Office D  20 of 28
  Office E  sampled data only (see the hard case)
```

Read it as questions:

- **Office B, 11 of 28.** Ask the office manager and the agents what gets in the way. Maybe their booking system is not connected.
- **Office A, 25 of 28.** Ask the most active agents what they do, and ask them to post it.

### Step 4: the hard case

**The hard case is Office E.** The Insights view, filtered to Office E's group, shows a sampling notice: 6 active users and 300 messages in the sample. A manager wants to write "about 3,000 messages a month" for the board, by multiplying the sample by 10.

Don't. The Insights page says sampled counts must not be scaled up to estimate total activity. Report the sample as a sample, with its label. Then ask the 6 users what they use the tool for. And even a correct message total would not show finished work; the trip-summary record does that.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Real use is measured,** per office, not accounts.
- **Help comes from peers** who share exact prompts, in about 40 minutes a week.
- **No single point of failure.** Each office has two champions by week 4.
- **Numbers lead to conversations,** and sampled data stays labelled.

## Common mistakes

1. **Counting accounts as adoption.** Access is not use. Fix: count weekly active users per team, and pair them with one work record.
2. **Making the champion a help desk.** Hours of support burn people out. Fix: plan roughly 40 minutes of core tasks each week, and save pairing for people who are stuck.
3. **Treating a number as a verdict.** A low count is a question. Fix: ask the people behind it what works and what blocks them.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A champion in a finance team sees these signals. Which week of the kit's thirty-day plan is each one the signal for?

```text
1. A colleague posts their own example in the channel.
2. A question in the channel gets answered, and not by the champion.
3. The same four colleagues use the tool again this week.
```

### Answer

1. **Week 2.** Its signal is that someone other than the champion posts an example.
2. **Week 4.** Its signal is that people other than the champion answer questions. That is also the week to name a second champion.
3. **Week 3.** Its signal is repeat use: the same colleagues come back, rather than trying once and stopping.

## Recap

- Use spreads through peers who share reusable prompts in the team's usual channels, not through announcements.
- Keep the champion role near 40 minutes a week, and add a second champion so it does not depend on one person.
- Read dashboards as questions: dips point to friction, few users mean "go and ask", and sampled counts are never scaled up.
