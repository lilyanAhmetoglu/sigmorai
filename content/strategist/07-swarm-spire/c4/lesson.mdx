## Why it matters

A new workflow can be well designed and still fail, because people do not use it, or use it in their own way. **Change management** is the work of moving people from the old way to the new way: who is affected, what changes for them, what help they get, and how you know it worked.

Tools change fast and people change slowly. This chapter is about the people part of a rollout. Chapter 5 covers what makes use stick, and chapter 6 covers incentives.

## Start small, on one workflow

OpenAI's guide on ChatGPT Work usage and cost describes a "controlled rollout" in five steps:

1. Confirm your agreement, remaining credits, rates and overage settings.
2. Select a limited group and a workflow your organization can evaluate.
3. Set access permissions, user limits, alerts and the overage limit.
4. Compare the group's usage with its existing baseline.
5. Review outcomes and any actual billing impact before expanding.

The guide names example workflows with a clear baseline: account research, meeting preparation, finance reporting. It says to compare the same workflow before and after, and to measure things like time, quality, rework and whether the deliverable was finished.

Anthropic's Claude Code admin guide also checks before it onboards. After the settings are set, a developer runs a status check to confirm they took effect. Only then does the guide share onboarding material. The rollout test comes before the broad invitation.

Example: a finance team picks month-end reporting drafts and 8 volunteers. It measures the last two closes as the baseline. It does not give all 60 accountants access on day one.

## Test with real people

OpenAI's rollout guide says to verify each boundary with representative identities, meaning real example users with the intended permissions. Its step for Codex cloud (hosted coding tasks) says to test with a representative member and with the designated administrator.

This is change management too. A setup that works for the IT admin may fail for an ordinary employee on their first morning. Find that out in the pilot.

## Plan the change for people

Technical rollout plans cover settings. A change plan covers people. This card is our own tool for this course, not a vendor template:

- **Who is affected:** which roles, how many people.
- **What changes:** the steps in their day that move to the new workflow.
- **What stays the same:** who is still responsible for the result.
- **What support they get:** a person to ask, a first example, a place to post questions.
- **How we check:** the numbers and the date of the first review.
- **When we pause:** the sign that makes us stop and fix before going on.

The "what stays the same" line matters most for trust. In chapter 2 you put a person at each approval point. Say so out loud: the person still decides.

## Meet concerns with a demonstration

Anthropic's Claude Code champion kit covers resistance to a new tool. It says healthy skepticism is expected. Its advice is not to argue the general case. Acknowledge the concern, give a short reframe, and offer one concrete demonstration on the person's own work. It adds that one successful experience resolves most concerns.

The kit's examples are for engineers, but the pattern fits any team:

- "I tried it once and it hallucinated." The kit says this is usually a context problem: the model lacked the right files or the real error text. Re-run the original request with proper context.
- "I do not trust AI to touch production code." The kit agrees that no change should land unread. It suggests reviewing the proposed change to the same standard as any pull request. For a business team, the same worry sounds like "I do not trust it with important work." Read AI output like a colleague's draft.
- "I am faster without it." The kit says this may be true for routine work. It suggests trying the tool on the tasks the person avoids, and timing one task both ways.

Example: a senior accountant says the tool made a number wrong. Don't tell her it will improve. Take her own report, supply the right data, and run it with her review step in place.

## Review after launch

Rollout does not end when access opens. OpenAI's guide says to review access, connected capabilities, credit use, support feedback and the workflows teams actually use, and then adjust the scope and the guidance for administrators.

Write the review date into the plan before the launch. A dated review turns "let's see how it goes" into a decision.

## Key points

- Start with a limited group and one workflow that has a baseline.
- Test setup with ordinary users, not only with administrators.
- Write a change plan: who, what changes, what stays, support, check, pause.
- Answer concerns with a demonstration on the person's own work, not an argument.
- Set a review date and adjust the scope based on what teams actually do.
