## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Turn an "everyone gets it on Monday" launch into a controlled rollout with a small group, a baseline and a dated review.
- Write a one-page change plan that says what changes for people and what stays the same.
- Answer a worried colleague by showing the tool once on a real task of theirs, not with an argument.

## The problem

A hospital group has an HR team of 30 advisers. They answer about 2,000 staff questions a month: leave, pay, shift swaps, sick notes. The HR director bought an AI assistant that drafts answers from the HR policy files.

The director's plan fits in one line: "All 30 advisers get it on 1 November, with an email that explains it." Nobody has written down how the team will know if it works. Nobody owns questions from the advisers. Two senior advisers have already said they "don't want a robot answering nurses".

The plan covers the software, not the people who must change how they work. That people part is called **change management**: moving people from the old way of working to the new one, and checking that it worked. The guidance below comes from the OpenAI ChatGPT Enterprise docs and the Claude Code docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Start small, on one workflow.** The OpenAI guide [ChatGPT Work: usage and cost](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/chatgpt-work-usage-and-cost) has a short checklist for a **controlled rollout**. "Before expanding access", it says to:

1. Confirm the agreement, the credits that remain, the rates and the overage settings.
2. "Select a limited group and a workflow your organization can evaluate."
3. Set up access, user limits, alerts and the overage limit.
4. Compare the group's use against its existing baseline.
5. "Review observed outcomes and any actual billing impact before expanding the rollout."

A **baseline** is the number before the change. Without it, you can't say if anything got better.

**Measure the work, not only the use.** The same guide says to pick "a recurring workflow with a clear existing baseline". Its examples are account research, meeting preparation and finance reporting. It says to "compare the same workflow before and after", and lists what to measure:

- time to finish the work,
- output quality, completeness or consistency,
- rework, manual coordination or error rates,
- whether the team finished the deliverable.

It also warns: "Usage alone doesn't establish savings." Our advice: always put one speed number and one quality number side by side. A faster draft that needs more fixing may not save anything.

**Test as a normal user before you invite everyone.** The [OpenAI admin rollout guide](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/admin-setup) says to test sign-in and feature access "with a member who has the intended permissions". Its last step says to "Verify every applicable boundary with representative identities", meaning test accounts with the same rights as real users. The [Claude Code admin guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/admin-setup) uses the same order. After the settings are in place, it asks a developer to run a status check. Only then does it share the getting-started material. Our reading: a test by IT is not enough, because IT accounts often have more rights.

**Plan for people, not only settings.** A settings plan says what the tool can do. A change plan says what happens to people. This six-line card is our own tool for this course, not a vendor template:

```text
Who is affected:     which roles, how many people
What changes:        the steps in their day that move to the new way
What stays the same: who signs off, who is responsible
Support:             a named person to ask, a first example, a place for questions
How we check:        the baseline numbers and the date of the first review
When we pause:       the sign that makes us stop and fix before going on
```

The "what stays the same" line builds trust. People fear a tool most when they don't know who is still responsible.

**Meet worries with a demonstration.** The Claude Code [champion kit](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/champion-kit) is written for engineers, but its advice on doubts fits any team (our reading). It says:

- "Healthy skepticism is expected".
- The best response "is rarely to argue the general case".
- Instead: say the worry is fair, give a short new way to see it, and offer to show the tool once, on a real task from that person.
- "Most concerns are resolved by a single successful experience."

The kit also lists answers to common worries:

- **"I tried it once and it hallucinated."** (Hallucinated means it made something up.) The kit says: "This is usually a context problem rather than a model problem." Re-run the same request with the right files and the real error text.
- **"I am faster without it."** The kit says that is likely true for routine work. It suggests trying the tool on the work the person tends to avoid, and timing one task both ways.

The kit also says that adoption "rarely happens because of a rollout announcement". So an email to everyone is not a plan.

**Review after launch, on a date.** The OpenAI rollout guide says: "After the initial rollout, review access, connected capabilities, credit use, support feedback, and the workflows teams actually use." Then it says to adjust the rollout scope and the guidance for administrators when those signals change. Our advice: write the review date into the plan before launch day.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak plan

```text
HR assistant launch
  1 November:   all 30 advisers get access
  Support:      one email with a how-to link
  Measurement:  none
  Review:       none
  Worries:      "the seniors will get used to it"
```

What goes wrong:

- **Everyone at once.** Any problem hits all 30 advisers and 2,000 questions on the same day.
- **No baseline.** In three months nobody can say if answers got faster or better.
- **No owner for questions.** Advisers who get stuck will quietly stop using it.
- **Worries ignored.** Doubts spread.

### Step 2: the good plan

```text
Pilot: 6 advisers, one workflow (leave and shift-swap questions)
  Baseline (last 2 months, same 6 people):
    days to answer a question, and % of answers sent back for correction
  Week 0:   a test account with normal adviser rights checks sign-in,
            the policy files and the draft feature
  Weeks 1-8: pilot; HR systems lead answers questions in one channel
  Week 8:   review on a fixed date: access, support feedback, the
            workflows people used, credit use;
            then decide: widen, change or stop

Change card
  Who:          30 advisers; first 6 in the pilot
  Changes:      the first draft of an answer comes from the assistant
  Stays:        the adviser checks, edits and signs every answer
  Support:      HR systems lead, 3 sample prompts, a questions channel
  Check:        days to answer + % sent back, against the baseline
  Pause:        if the % sent back rises for 2 weeks in a row
```

### Step 3: the hard case

**The hard case is the week 8 result.** It looks like a win at first:

```text
                              Baseline    Pilot
Days to answer a question       3.0        2.4
Answers sent back to fix        5%         8%
```

Our maths: (3.0 − 2.4) ÷ 3.0 = 0.2, so answers are 20% faster. But the share sent back for fixing rose by 3 points, from 5% to 8%. Fixing takes time too, and a wrong answer about pay or leave hurts a real nurse.

So the review says "change", not "widen". The team reads the returned answers to find the cause. Maybe the policy files in the tool are out of date. Fix the cause, run the pilot again, and compare both numbers again.

### Step 4: the worried senior

One senior adviser says: "It told a nurse the wrong number of leave days." Don't promise that the tool will get better. Use the kit's pattern:

1. **Acknowledge:** "You're right to check. A wrong answer about leave matters."
2. **Reframe:** this kind of error often means the tool had the wrong policy file, not that it can't do the job.
3. **Demonstrate:** take that real question, give the tool the current leave policy, and run it together. The adviser still reviews the answer before it goes out.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Problems show up in a small group,** not in front of the whole hospital group.
- **Two numbers, not one.** Speed and quality are read together against a baseline.
- **People know what stays the same.** The adviser still owns every answer.
- **A date, not a hope.** The week 8 review turns "let's see" into a decision.

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating launch as an announcement.** An email gives access, not new habits. Fix: start with a small group on one workflow, with a named contact.
2. **Reading only the speed number.** Faster work with more rework may save nothing. Fix: pair each speed number with a quality number and a baseline.
3. **Arguing with doubters.** Studies and promises rarely change a mind. Fix: one short demonstration on that person's own task.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Three colleagues in a sales team say these things about a new AI assistant. For each one, what does the champion kit suggest?

```text
1. "Honestly, I write these emails faster by hand."
2. "We're too busy to learn yet another tool."
3. "Is our customer data safe in this thing?"
```

### Answer

1. **Try it on the work they avoid.** The kit says this is likely true for routine work. It suggests the tasks people tend to avoid, and timing one tedious task both ways.
2. **Give it one session.** The kit's reply is that "If it does not return value within the first session, it is reasonable to set it aside." Offer a short start on one real task.
3. **Send it to the administrator.** The kit says to refer security and data questions to the administrator, and that champions "should not improvise this answer".

## Recap

- Start with a limited group and one workflow that has a baseline. Review the results before you expand.
- Write a change plan. Its most important line says what stays the same: who signs off and who is responsible.
- Answer doubts by showing the tool once on a real task of that person's. Put a review date in the plan.
