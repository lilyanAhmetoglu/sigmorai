## Why it matters

A company that does AI one pilot at a time ends up with many tools, no order and no owner. An **AI roadmap** is one page that says what you will build, in what order, who owns each item, and what must be true before each step.

This chapter pulls together the whole course. The roadmap is built from the risk register (chapter 2), the controls (chapters 1 and 3), the incident plan (chapter 4) and the rollout waves (chapter 5). The roadmap format here is ours, not a vendor standard.

## Three horizons

Sort every item into one of three columns:

- **Now (next 3 months):** approved, owned, and ready to start.
- **Next (3 to 9 months):** planned, but waiting for a gate.
- **Later (9 to 18 months):** an idea worth keeping, with no commitment.

Dates get less certain as you go right. Say so on the page. Example: a retailer lists "Meeting summaries" under Now and "Claims agent" under Later.

## What each item needs

Every roadmap line should answer seven questions:

1. What is the use, in one sentence?
2. Which risk tier is it (chapter 2)?
3. Who owns it, by name?
4. How will we measure value (realm 6)?
5. What must exist first: data, controls, training?
6. What is the gate, and what is the stop rule?
7. Which model and vendor does it depend on?

An item that cannot answer these stays in Later. This keeps the roadmap honest.

## Order by simplicity and dependency

Anthropic's Building effective agents post recommends finding the simplest solution possible and adding complexity only when needed. It says agentic systems often trade latency and cost for better task performance. For a roadmap, that means: a prompt or a fixed workflow comes before an agent, and one agent comes before a team of agents.

Dependencies decide the rest. A claims agent that can pay out needs a human approval step and audit logs first. A knowledge assistant needs clean, permissioned data first (realm 3). Put the prerequisite on the roadmap as its own line, with its own owner.

Example: a high-tier item in Now with no approval step planned is a gap, not a plan.

## Put the model calendar on the roadmap

Vendors retire models. A roadmap that ignores this will have dates that break.

Anthropic's deprecations page uses four states: **Active**, **Legacy**, **Deprecated** and **Retired**. A deprecated model still works but is no longer recommended. Anthropic gives a replacement and a retirement date. Requests to a retired model fail. The page warns that deprecated models are likely to be less reliable than active ones.

It also gives practical steps:

- Anthropic notifies customers with active deployments, with at least 60 days' notice for publicly released models.
- To find your own use, open the Usage page in the Console and export a CSV. It shows usage by API key and model.
- Test your application with newer models well before the retirement date.

Example from the page, checked on 2026-10-06: `claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929` was deprecated on 30 September 2026, with retirement on 30 November 2026 and `claude-sonnet-5-5` as the recommended replacement. A project that plans to launch on the old model in January 2027 has a dated problem.

OpenAI's deprecations page says it notifies customers who are actively using a model by email, documents each deprecation on the page, and uses blog posts for larger changes. At the same date, its newest entry lists models deprecated on 1 October 2026 and removed on 1 April 2027, with six months' notice. Other entries have shorter notice, such as at least three months. Realm 5 covered contract and budget effects of this.

So the roadmap needs a **model-change row**: a standing line each half year to list models in use, check the vendors' pages, and re-run your tests (realm 6) on the replacement.

## Review on a rhythm

Review the roadmap every quarter. Also review it early when:

- a vendor sends a deprecation notice,
- an incident happens (chapter 4),
- a use changes tier (chapter 2),
- a pilot hits its stop rule (realm 2).

OpenAI's safety guide also tells you to understand and communicate the system's limits, so users' expectations match. A roadmap shown to the board should state limits the same way: what is a plan, what is a hope.

## Know what to remove

A roadmap that only grows becomes a wish list. At each review, ask: what did we stop, and why? Moving an item to a "Stopped" list, with the reason, is a good result. It means the stop rule worked.

## Key points

- Use three horizons: Now, Next, Later (our format). Dates get less certain to the right.
- Every item has a tier, an owner, a value measure, prerequisites, a gate and a model dependency.
- Order by simplicity and by dependency; prerequisites get their own lines.
- Add a model-change row; vendors retire models with documented notice, and deprecated models may be less reliable.
- Review every quarter and early on a vendor notice, an incident, a tier change or a stop rule.
- Keep a list of what you stopped.
