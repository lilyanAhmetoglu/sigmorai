## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Turn a list of AI ideas into a one-page **AI roadmap** with three horizons: Now, Next and Later.
- Give each roadmap line an owner, a tier, the things it needs first, a gate and the model it depends on.
- Put the vendors' model retirement dates on the roadmap, so the plan does not break on a date you could have seen.

## The problem

An online language school has 500 staff and 40,000 students. After a conference, the CEO writes an AI roadmap on one slide:

```text
Q1 2027  AI tutor team (5 agents) for all courses
Q1 2027  Automatic exam grading
Q2 2027  Feedback on student essays, all students
Q2 2027  Chat helper for course questions
```

Every line has a date. No line has an owner. Nobody knows what each item needs first. The essay feedback tool already runs as a small test on one vendor model, and nobody has checked that model's future. The board must decide what to fund next month, and asks: "Which of these dates can we trust?"

A roadmap that is only a wish list of dates breaks the first time a vendor, a data gap or an incident gets in the way. The vendor facts in this lesson come from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

An **AI roadmap** is one page that says what you will build, in what order, who owns each item, and what must be true before each step. It pulls together the earlier lessons: the policy, the risk tiers, the compliance file, the incident plan and the rollout waves. Our roadmap format is a tool for this course, not a vendor standard.

**Three horizons.** Our advice: sort every item into one of three columns.

- **Now (next 3 months):** approved, owned, and ready to start.
- **Next (3 to 9 months):** planned, but waiting for a gate.
- **Later (9 to 18 months):** an idea worth keeping, with no promise yet.

Dates get less certain as you move right. Say so on the page. Think of a weather forecast: tomorrow is fairly sure, next month is a guess.

**Seven questions per line.** An item that can't answer them stays in Later.

1. What is the use, in one sentence?
2. Which risk tier is it?
3. Who owns it, by name?
4. How will we measure value?
5. What must exist first: data, controls, training?
6. What is the gate, and what is the stop rule?
7. Which model and vendor does it depend on?

**Order by simplicity and by what comes first.** Anthropic's post [Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) says: "we recommend finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed." It also says: "Agentic systems often trade latency and cost for better task performance". (**Latency** means waiting time.) Our reading for a roadmap: a prompt or a fixed workflow comes before one agent, and one agent comes before a team of agents.

Things that must come first need a line of their own. A high-tier item that can act needs its approval step and its logs first. A tool that answers from your documents needs clean data first. Our advice: give each prerequisite its own line and owner, so it can be tracked and gated.

**Models retire on a calendar.** The [Claude deprecations page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations) uses four states:

- **Active:** "fully supported and recommended for use."
- **Legacy:** no more updates, and it "may be deprecated in the future."
- **Deprecated:** "still functional but no longer recommended." Anthropic names a replacement and a retirement date.
- **Retired:** "Requests to retired models will fail."

The page warns: "Deprecated models are likely to be less reliable than active models." Customers with a live deployment get "at least 60 days' notice before model retirement for publicly released models." To find where you use a model, go to the Usage page in the Claude Console and export a CSV, which shows usage "broken down by API key and model." It also says to test "with the new models well before the retirement date."

The [OpenAI deprecations page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations) says OpenAI notifies "customers who are actively using the model by email" and also lists the change on that page, with blog posts for larger changes. It also lists minimum notice periods, which differ by model type (see Try it).

So our advice: add a **model-change row** to the roadmap. Each half year, list the models in use, check both vendors' pages, and test the replacements.

**Review on a rhythm, and early when needed.** Our advice: review every quarter. Review early when a vendor sends a deprecation notice, an incident happens, a use changes tier, or a pilot hits its stop rule. Keep a **Stopped** list with the reason for each stop. A stopped item is a success: the stop rule did its job.

**Say what is a plan and what is a hope.** The [OpenAI safety guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/safety-best-practices) asks you to understand the system's limits and make sure users' "expectations are calibrated appropriately." A roadmap for the board should do the same.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak roadmap

The four lines above. What goes wrong:

- **No owners**, so no one is responsible for any date.
- **The hardest item comes first.** A team of five agents starts before one simple tool works.
- **No prerequisites.** Exam grading decides students' results, a high-tier use in our tiers, but no approval step or appeal process is planned.
- **No model check.** The essay tool's model may retire before Q2.

### Step 2: answer the seven questions

Our example, with made-up names:

```text
NOW (next 3 months)
  Chat helper for course questions
    Tier medium | Owner: A. Moreno, head of support
    Needs first: approved course FAQ pages
    Gate: red-team test passed | Stop: wrong answers above
    the agreed level | Model: one active model, named

NEXT (3 to 9 months)
  Essay feedback for all students
    Tier low (teachers read it first) | Owner: K. Osei
    Needs first: model change (see hard case)
  Exam grading helper: suggests a grade, a teacher decides
    Tier high | Owner: R. Lind, head of exams
    Needs first: teacher approval step, appeal process,
    audit logs (each its own line and owner)

LATER (9 to 18 months)
  AI tutor: start as one simple workflow for one course.
  A team of agents only if the simple version falls short.

STANDING ROW
  Model change: each half year, list models, check both
  vendors' deprecation pages, test replacements.
  Owner: CTO

STOPPED
  (empty for now: the list exists so stops stay visible)
```

### Step 3: the hard case

**The hard case is the essay tool's model.** It runs on OpenAI's `gpt-5.1`. On the OpenAI deprecations page, the 2026-10-01 entry says `gpt-5.1` is deprecated and "will be removed from the API on April 1, 2027, with six months' notice." The recommended replacement is `gpt-6-sol`.

The CEO's plan rolls the tool out to all students in Q2 2027, which starts on April 1. So any launch date in Q2 falls on or after the day the old model is removed.

Our fix: the item stays in Next, but its first step is a model change. The team tests `gpt-6-sol` on a sample of past essays with known teacher grades, well before April. The rollout to all students starts only after that test passes. The model-change row catches the next case like this one before it reaches the board.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Owned.** Every line has a person, so every date has someone who answers for it.
- **Ordered.** Simple tools come first; the agent team waits for proof.
- **Dated honestly.** Known model dates are on the page, and Later says "no promise".

## Common mistakes

1. **Starting with the most complex item.** A team of agents adds cost and waiting time before a simple tool is proven. Fix: start with the simplest version, and build up only when tests show the need.
2. **A prerequisite hidden inside a big item.** Nobody tracks it, so the date slips without warning. Fix: give each prerequisite its own line and owner.
3. **A roadmap that only grows.** It turns into a wish list. Fix: keep a Stopped list with reasons.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A team picks three OpenAI models for business-critical tools. Using the OpenAI deprecations page, what is the minimum notice before each could retire, and which choice is risky?

```text
1. A generally available model
2. A specialized variant, such as a Codex variant
3. A model with "preview" in its name
```

### Answer

1. **At least 6 months.** The page lists "At least 6 months" for generally available models.
2. **At least 3 months.** The page lists "At least 3 months" for specialized variants.
3. **Possibly much less.** Preview models "may be retired with much shorter notice, such as 2 weeks." The page says it doesn't recommend them "for business-critical production workloads unless you can migrate on short notice." This is the risky choice.

The page adds one exception: if "safety or compliance concerns" require a faster timeline, OpenAI will give as much notice as it reasonably can. Our advice: write the model type next to each model on the roadmap.

## Recap

- A roadmap sorts work into Now, Next and Later (our format). Every line has an owner, a tier, prerequisites, a gate, a stop rule and a model.
- Start simple; make it more complex only when a test shows the need. Each prerequisite gets its own line and owner.
- Vendors retire models with documented notice, and retired models stop working. Keep a model-change row, review every quarter and early when something changes, and keep a Stopped list with reasons.
