## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Write an **AI policy** that answers five questions: tools, data, review, access and reporting.
- Tie each data rule to a real vendor setting, and name the products and models that setting does not cover.
- Turn a vague rule ("use AI responsibly") into a rule that someone can check.

## The problem

A hotel group has 1,800 staff in 22 hotels. AI use grew fast and without a plan. The guest-relations team writes replies to online reviews with an OpenAI API project. The finance team reads supplier invoices with Claude. A revenue manager opened a new API project last week and pasted in a list of guest bookings to "see what the AI says".

The CFO asks the head of operations for a company AI policy. It must fit on two pages. Today the only rule is one line in the staff handbook: "AI tools are allowed. Be careful with guest data."

That line sounds safe, but nobody can check it. It does not say which tools, which data, who reviews, or who holds the keys. The vendor facts in this lesson come from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

An **AI policy** is a set of written rules. It says who may use which AI tools, with which data, and who checks the results. Habits are enough for one person. A company of 1,800 people needs rules, and the rules need settings behind them.

Our advice: a useful policy answers five questions. This list is a tool for this course, not a legal template.

1. **Tools and accounts.** Which AI tools and accounts are approved?
2. **Data.** Which kinds of data may go into which tool?
3. **Review.** When must a person check the output before it is used?
4. **Access.** Who may create keys, change settings and see usage?
5. **Reporting.** How do staff and customers report a problem, and who answers?

Think of a policy like the rules for a company car. "Drive carefully" is a wish. "Only staff with a licence check, one fuel card per driver, report damage to the fleet manager" is a policy.

**Data rules must name the setting.** **Data retention** means how long a vendor keeps your prompts and outputs. **Zero data retention (ZDR)** is an arrangement where the vendor does not keep them after the reply. ZDR is not one switch for a whole company:

- The [OpenAI data controls page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data) lets approved customers pick Zero Data Retention or Modified Abuse Monitoring for the whole organization. Each project can then select `default` "to inherit the organization-level setting", pick its own control, or select None "to disable these controls for that project".
- The [Claude retention page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention) says: "ZDR is enabled per organization". A second organization starts without it, and enablement "does not automatically extend to other organizations under the same account."
- The same Claude page lists what ZDR does not cover. One item is the Claude Console: "Any usage in the Claude Console, including playground."
- It also names some models. Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Mythos 5.1, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 "require 30-day data retention and are not available under ZDR unless expressly authorized by Anthropic."
- The same page gives one path for them. An organization with ZDR "can make these models available in a specific workspace by enabling 30-day retention for that workspace only."

So "we have ZDR" is not a full rule. The rule must say which organization, which projects, which products and which models.

**Review rules say who checks what.** The [OpenAI safety guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/safety-best-practices) says: "Wherever possible, we recommend having a human review outputs before they are used in practice." It adds: "This is especially critical in high-stakes domains, and for code generation." Reviewers should know the limits of the system. They also need easy access to the facts behind the output, such as the original notes behind a summary.

**Access rules say who holds the keys.** An **API key** is like a door key to your AI account.

- The [OpenAI production guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/production-best-practices) says: "We strongly recommend setting an expiration date when you create a project API key and establishing a regular key rotation process." Administrators can also enforce a maximum key lifetime and restrict which types of keys can be created.
- The [Claude Admin API page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/admin-api) says: "There are five organization-level roles."
- The same page says a developer "Can use playground and manage API keys". An admin can do all of that, "plus manage users".
- Its best practices include "Regularly audit member roles and permissions" and rotating keys periodically.
- The [Claude workspaces page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/workspaces) says workspaces can "separate different projects, environments, or teams", each with its own members, API keys and limits.

**Reporting rules say who answers.** The OpenAI safety guide says users should have "an easily-available method for reporting improper functionality". Its examples are a listed email address or a ticket form. It adds: "This method should be monitored by a human and responded to as appropriate." It also tells you to understand and communicate the system's limits, so users' expectations match what it can do.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak policy

```text
AI policy (handbook, page 14)
AI tools are allowed. Be careful with guest data.
```

What goes wrong:

- **Tools:** no list, so the revenue manager's new project counts as "allowed".
- **Data:** "be careful" names no setting. Nobody checked the new project's retention control.
- **Review, access, reporting:** missing. Nobody knows who checks a review reply, who holds keys, or where a guest complains.

### Step 2: make two rules checkable

Our example rewrite of the first two questions for the hotel group:

```text
1 Tools   Approved: OpenAI project "guest-replies" and Claude
          workspace "finance-invoices". Nothing else.
          A new project or workspace is approved only after IT
          records three things: owner, retention setting, date.
2 Data    Data class              Allowed in
          Public (menus, prices)  any approved project
          Staff rotas             approved projects
          Guest personal data     only where IT recorded the
                                  retention setting
          Card numbers            no AI tool
```

The three recorded items and the data classes are our own choices, not vendor rules. Your company sets its own.

### Step 3: check each rule against the docs

- **Rule 1** names accounts, not vendors. "OpenAI is approved" would also approve the revenue manager's new project, and an OpenAI project can select None for its retention control.
- **Rule 2** sorts data by class. One line per class is easier to check than "be careful".
- **Review, access and reports** follow the same pattern: an action, an owner, and a setting where one exists.

### Step 4: the hard case

**The hard case is a new product, not a new project.** Finance asks to use Claude for Excel on supplier invoice sheets. The group has ZDR with Anthropic, so the team assumes the data is covered.

The Claude retention page says otherwise: "Claude for Excel: Not currently ZDR-eligible." It also lists the Claude Teams and Claude Enterprise product interfaces as not ZDR-eligible.

So the data rule needs a products line. Our example:

```text
2b Products  ZDR covers API calls from our Claude organization.
             Not covered: the vendor console and playground,
             Claude for Excel, chat products. No guest or
             supplier personal data in these.
```

Our advice: list products by name, so staff don't have to guess.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Checkable.** Each rule names an account, a data class or an owner. An auditor can see if it happened.
- **Tied to settings.** Rules 1 and 2b name the retention setting and the products it does not cover.
- **Easy to extend.** A new tool joins through one recorded check.

## Common mistakes

1. **Approving a vendor instead of an account.** "OpenAI is approved" also approves every new project anyone opens. Fix: approve named projects and workspaces.
2. **A policy with no owner or review date.** Nobody notices when it stops matching the settings. Fix: name a policy owner and a review date.
3. **Using our five questions as a legal template.** They are a course tool. Fix: legal and the data protection lead approve the final policy.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A hotel group uses OpenAI. Its admin set a maximum key lifetime of 90 days for the organization. IT also limited new keys to service-account keys only. Answer two questions:

```text
1. A project admin wants 180-day keys for one project.
   Can the project allow that?
2. 30 user-owned keys were made before the new rule.
   Does the rule stop them?
```

### Answer

1. **No.** The OpenAI production guide says: "Project limits cannot exceed the organization limit." A project can be stricter, not looser.
2. **No.** The guide says these key-type controls apply only to new key creation, and "existing API keys are unaffected". Our advice: list the old keys and replace or revoke them. A new rule does not clean up the past.

## Recap

- An AI policy answers five questions: tools, data, review, access and reporting (our list). Each rule needs an owner and an action someone can check.
- Retention settings differ by project, organization, product and model. Name what the rule covers; some models need 30-day retention and sit outside ZDR.
- Review is recommended wherever possible, most of all for high-stakes uses. Give the smallest role, expire and rotate keys, and give reports a human owner.
