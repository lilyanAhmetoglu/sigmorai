## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Place any AI use in a **risk tier** with two questions: who is the first reader, and can the AI take an action?
- Pick controls that fit each tier, from the vendors' safety guides.
- Say when a use must be placed in a tier again.

## The problem

A property company manages 9,000 rented flats and has 700 staff. It runs four AI uses:

- a monthly newsletter that staff draft with AI and then edit,
- a website assistant that answers tenants' questions about house rules,
- a tool that suggests deductions from a tenant's deposit when they move out, and writes them into the accounts,
- a summary of each lease for the staff who handle move-outs.

After a news story about an AI mistake, the risk committee writes one rule: "Legal approves every AI output before use." In week one, legal gets 3,400 items. The newsletter is late, and tenants wait two days for an answer about bin days. Staff start to use personal AI accounts to get work done.

One rule for every use failed. Heavy checks on small risks pushed people away from the approved tools. The vendor facts in this lesson come from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

Not every AI use carries the same risk. A typo in a newsletter is a small problem. A wrong deduction from a deposit is a big one. **Risk tiers** sort AI uses into levels, so each level gets the controls it needs. Think of a hospital: a cold gets a nurse, surgery gets a team and a checklist.

Our three-tier model is a tool for this course. Your risk team may use other names.

| Tier   | What the AI does                                      |
| ------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Low    | Drafts for staff, who read and edit before use        |
| Medium | Text that reaches customers or the public first       |
| High   | Actions or decisions with money, legal or safety risk |

Two questions place a use:

1. **Who sees the output first?** A member of staff, or someone outside the company?
2. **Can it act?** Does it only write text, or can it change something, such as a payment or a record?

```text
Can it act? -- yes --> High
     |
     no
     |
Who sees it first? -- outside --> Medium
     |
     staff --> Low
```

**Low tier: staff review, and check the facts.** Staff are the first readers, so their review is the main control. The [Claude guide to reducing hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations) lists techniques that help. It also warns: "while these techniques significantly reduce hallucinations, they don't eliminate them entirely. Always validate critical information". So even a low-tier draft needs a fact check for numbers and names.

**Medium tier: limit what goes in and out.** The [OpenAI safety guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/safety-best-practices) gives several controls:

- **Constrain input and output.** "Limiting the amount of text a user can input into the prompt helps avoid prompt injection." Limiting output tokens "helps reduce the chance of misuse." (A **prompt injection** is text that tries to make the AI ignore its rules.)
- **Use validated materials.** Returning an existing, checked support article "can be safer than returning novel generated content".
- **Know your customer.** Users should generally need to register and log in.
- **Red-team.** The guide recommends "red-teaming" your application: test it with normal inputs and with inputs from someone trying to break it.

The [Claude jailbreak guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/mitigate-jailbreaks) adds a **harmlessness screen**: a lightweight model checks user input "before it reaches your main conversation". It also suggests system prompts that set clear limits and say how to refuse.

**High tier: a person approves before the action.** The [OpenAI guide to guardrails and human review](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/agents/guardrails-approvals) names two controls that work together:

- **Guardrails** "validate input, output, or tool behavior automatically."
- **Human review** "pauses the run so a person or policy can approve or reject a sensitive action." Its examples include pausing "before side effects like cancellations, edits".

The Claude jailbreak guide adds the **principle of least privilege**: give the AI only the data and actions it needs, "so that a successful injection can do minimal damage". Anthropic's post [Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) says: "The autonomous nature of agents means higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors." It recommends "extensive testing in sandboxed environments, along with the appropriate guardrails."

**Tiers can change.** Our advice: place the use in a tier again when one of three things changes:

- the audience (staff to customers),
- the actions (it only wrote text, and now it can change records),
- the data (public data to personal data).

A **risk register** is a simple list of every AI use, its tier, its controls and its owner. It answers "what AI do we run, and who checks it?" on one page.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak rule

```text
AI rule v1
Legal approves every AI output before use.
```

What goes wrong:

- A newsletter draft and a deposit deduction get the same check.
- Legal can't keep up, so slow work pushes staff to tools nobody controls.
- The real high-risk use, the deposit tool, gets no special control at all.

### Step 2: ask the two questions for each use

```text
Use                    Sees it first   Can it act?        Tier
Newsletter draft       staff           no                 low
Website assistant      tenants         no                 medium
Deposit deduction      accounts        yes: writes money  high
Lease summary          staff           no                 low
```

### Step 3: write controls per tier

Our example register:

```text
Newsletter (low)       Author edits. Dates and amounts checked
                       against the source. Owner: comms lead.
Website assistant      Answers only from approved house-rule pages.
(medium)               Input screened, answer length limited.
                       Red-teamed before launch. Owner: digital lead.
Deposit tool (high)    Tool proposes deductions; a property manager
                       approves each one before it is written.
                       No other write access. Tested in a sandbox
                       first. Owner: head of accounts.
Lease summary (low)    See the hard case.
```

### Step 4: the hard case

**The hard case is the lease summary.** Staff see it first, and it can't act. So it is low tier. But staff use the summary to decide deposit deductions. A wrong notice period or a wrong pet clause can cost a tenant money.

The tier does not change, because the AI still only writes for staff. The control gets sharper. The Claude guide asks for checks of critical facts, "especially for high-stakes decisions." Our rule:

```text
Lease summary (low)    Staff check every date, amount and clause
                       number against the lease before using it.
                       The summary shows the clause number for each
                       fact, so the check is fast.
```

Watch the next step. If the company lets the summary feed the deposit tool directly, with no staff step, the audience and the actions change. Then place it again.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Matched.** Legal is no longer a queue for newsletters.
- **Focused.** The one use that moves money gets a person before each action and the least access.
- **Visible.** Each use has a tier, controls and an owner on one page.

## Common mistakes

1. **One rule for every use.** Heavy checks everywhere slow the work and push staff to unapproved tools. Fix: tier first, then set controls per tier.
2. **A check after the action for a high-tier use.** A monthly sample finds the error after the money moved. Fix: a person approves before the action.
3. **A tier set once and never changed.** A use grows a new audience or new access. Fix: place it again when the audience, actions or data change.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

The OpenAI guardrails page has a table of controls: input guardrails, output guardrails, tool guardrails and human-in-the-loop approvals. Match each need to one control:

```text
1. Stop a request that asks for another tenant's address
   before the main model runs.
2. Remove a phone number from the final answer before it
   leaves the system.
3. Check that a "book repair" call has a valid flat number.
4. Pause before the assistant cancels a tenant's contract.
```

### Answer

1. **Input guardrail.** The page lists it for blocking "disallowed user requests before the main model runs".
2. **Output guardrail.** The page uses it to "Validate or redact the final output before it leaves the system".
3. **Tool guardrail.** The page uses it to "Check arguments or results around a function tool call".
4. **Human-in-the-loop approval.** A cancellation is a side effect, so a person approves or rejects it first.

## Recap

- Two questions place a use: who is the first reader, and can the AI change something? Our tiers are low, medium and high.
- Low: staff review and fact checks, because no technique makes hallucinations go away fully. Medium: limit input and output, use validated materials, screen input, red-team. High: a person approves before the action, with least privilege and sandbox tests.
- Place a use in a tier again when its audience, actions or data change, and keep a register with an owner for each use.
