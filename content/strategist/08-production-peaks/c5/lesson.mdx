## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Turn a pilot that worked into a **rollout plan** in waves, each with a gate to start, a rule to stop, and one person who owns it.
- Check that controls, test areas and spend limits are in place before each wave.
- Write a way back: what makes you stop, how you stop, and what staff do meanwhile.

## The problem

An accounting firm has 1,500 staff in 20 offices. Forty accountants tested an AI tool that writes first drafts of tax letters to clients. The pilot met its go rule (the pilot result that meant "go ahead"). The tool calls an AI vendor's API.

The managing partner writes the rollout plan in one email: "One new office joins every Monday for 20 weeks. IT gives each office a key. Training is a video."

The plan has a date for each office, but no check between them. If office 3 finds a serious problem, offices 4 to 20 still join on schedule. Nobody tests the settings. Nobody owns a wave. Nobody says what happens if the tool must stop.

A **rollout plan** says how a proven pilot grows to the whole company without breaking things. The vendor facts in this lesson come from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

A rollout plan has five parts: waves, checks, separate test areas, limits, and a way back.

**Part 1: waves.** A **wave** is a group that gets access at the same time. Our wave model is a tool for this course, not a vendor standard. The [ChatGPT usage and cost guide](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/chatgpt-work-usage-and-cost) describes the same idea as a controlled rollout:

- "Select a limited group and a workflow your organization can evaluate."
- Compare use against the group's existing baseline.
- "Review observed outcomes and any actual billing impact before expanding the rollout."

Our advice: give each wave three things.

- **An entry gate:** what must be true before it starts. A date alone is not a gate.
- **A stop rule:** what makes you pause or go back.
- **An owner:** one named person at your company, not a team and not the vendor.

Think of a new bridge. Engineers let a few trucks on, measure, then open more lanes. They don't open it to rush hour on day one.

**Part 2: test that controls work.** A setting you have not tested is only a hope.

- The [Claude Code admin guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/admin-setup) says that after you set managed settings, a developer runs `/status`, and the `Setting sources` line should show `Enterprise managed settings`.
- For example, the [ChatGPT admin rollout guide](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/admin-setup) says, for local computer access: "Test an action the policy should allow and an action it should block". It adds: "If a restriction does not work as expected, resolve the issue before users rely on it."

**Part 3: separate test areas.** Don't test on the system your staff use.

- The [Claude workspaces page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/workspaces) suggests one workspace for each stage: development, then staging (a copy of the live system for final tests), then production. Development has "lower rate limits". Production has "full rate limits and monitoring".
- The [OpenAI production guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/production-best-practices) says that as you scale, "you may want to create separate projects for your staging and production environments."

**Part 4: limits before volume.** A **spend limit** caps money per month. A **rate limit** caps how many requests or tokens you can send per minute.

- The Claude workspaces page says you can set workspace limits "lower than (but not higher than) your organization's limits".
- The same page says "Organization-wide limits always apply", and you "cannot set limits on the Default Workspace".
- It also lets you set alerts "when spending reaches certain thresholds."
- The same page says the Claude Code workspace "is the only workspace that supports per-user monthly spend limits."
- The [OpenAI rate limits page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/rate-limits) says limits are set per organization and per project, "not user level."
- The OpenAI production guide also recommends spend alerts.

**Ramp traffic in steps.** Rate limits are about speed of growth too. Both vendors warn about it. The exact point varies by model and traffic.

- The [Claude rate limits page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits) says you can get 429 errors "because of acceleration limits on the API if your organization has a sharp increase in usage."
- Its advice: "ramp up your traffic gradually and maintain consistent usage patterns."
- The OpenAI rate limits page says a `slow_down` error means "Your request rate increased too quickly."
- It "can occur even when your traffic is within its requests-per-minute and tokens-per-minute limits."
- Its rule of thumb (OpenAI's own; check your vendor's page): "once your traffic reaches 1 million input tokens per minute (TPM), increase it by no more than 50% every 15 minutes."

**Part 5: the way back.** Our advice: a rollback plan answers three questions.

1. **Trigger:** what exact event means "go back"?
2. **Action:** how do we stop, and who may decide?
3. **Fallback:** what do staff do meanwhile?

Keep the old manual process alive until the new one has passed several review cycles.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak plan

```text
Rollout v1
Mon each week: one more office, A to T. 20 weeks.
Keys: one per office, all in the production workspace.
Testing: in the production workspace.
Training: one video.
```

What goes wrong:

- **No gate.** A date moves the rollout on, even after a bad week.
- **No checks.** Nobody proves the blocked actions are really blocked.
- **Tests and staff share one area.** A broken test script can use the production budget.
- **No way back.** Nobody can say what happens if the tool must stop.

### Step 2: waves with gates

Our example plan:

```text
Wave 1  40 pilot users
  Gate   pilot go rule met
  Stop   any letter with another client's data
  Owner  Head of tax

Wave 2  2 offices (150 people)
  Gate   wave 1 ran 4 weeks with accepted drafts at
         the pilot level; controls tested
  Stop   as wave 1, or more than 20 support tickets
         in one week
  Owner  Head of tax

Wave 3  8 offices (600 people)
  Gate   wave 2 gate met; spend inside its cap
  Stop   as wave 2
  Owner  COO

Wave 4  all offices
  Gate   wave 3 gate met
  Stop   as wave 2
  Owner  COO
```

The 4 weeks, the 20 tickets and the group sizes are our own choices. In the real plan, each owner line holds one person's name, not only a job title.

### Step 3: one check per part

Our example. Each line names who checks and where the result is written:

```text
Before each wave          Checked by        Result goes to
  Controls tested         IT security       wave log
  Test area in use        platform lead     wave log
  Limits and alerts set   finance partner   wave log
  Start times spread      office managers   wave log
  Way back written        wave owner        wave log
```

The wave owner signs the log before the wave starts. A blank line means the wave waits. The roles are our own choices.

### Step 4: the hard case

**The hard case is a cap per person.** Finance asks for a monthly AI budget per accountant. It sounds simple, but the vendor limits don't work that way for this tool:

- The OpenAI rate limits page says limits are set per organization and project, not per user.
- On Claude, only the Claude Code workspace can have a monthly spend limit for each user. The letter tool runs in a normal workspace.

So our advice: cap spend at the workspace level, where the vendor offers a cap. If finance needs a cap per person, the firm's own app must count use per person. Write that as a task with an owner, not a vendor setting.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Gated.** Each wave starts on results, not on a date.
- **Tested.** Controls are proven before people rely on them.
- **Safe to stop.** No wave starts until its way back is written and signed.

## Common mistakes

1. **Training as the only preparation.** A video does not test settings or limits. Fix: train, and also run the checks before each wave.
2. **A stop rule nobody measures.** "Too many errors" with no number never fires. Fix: write the number and where it is measured.
3. **Counting usage as success.** The ChatGPT usage and cost guide says: "Usage alone doesn't establish savings". Fix: compare against the group's baseline before you expand.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A tool on OpenAI's API returns two kinds of errors in its first busy week. Use the OpenAI rate limits page. What does each one mean, and what should the team do?

```text
A. HTTP 429, code slow_down
B. HTTP 503, code server_is_overloaded
```

### Answer

The OpenAI rate limits page says a 429 is a rate limit error. A 503 is an overload error, a different problem.

- **A** means: "Your request rate increased too quickly." The page says to follow `Retry-After` when it is there, "reduce your request rate, and then increase it gradually."
- **B** means: "The requested model is temporarily overloaded." The page says to follow `Retry-After` when it is there, then retry, and to increase the delay between retries if it continues.

The first is about your own speed of growth. The second is about the vendor's capacity. Our advice: watch both in each wave.

## Recap

- Grow in waves. Each wave needs a gate to start, a rule to stop, and one named person at your company who owns it (our model).
- Before each wave, test one allowed and one blocked action, keep tests out of production, and set limits and alerts. Workspace limits can never go above the organization's, and per-user caps are not a general vendor setting.
- Ramp traffic in steps, since fast growth can cause `slow_down` errors inside the limits, and write the way back before launch: trigger, action, fallback.
