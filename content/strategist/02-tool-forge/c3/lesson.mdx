## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Score each AI candidate on five questions: value, fit, success, risk and readiness.
- Back every score with one line of evidence.
- Read a score card for warning signs, not only the total.

## The problem

A logistics company has three AI ideas for next quarter. The operations director asks the team to rank them. In the meeting, the person who talks the most wins, and nobody writes down why.

A simple **score card** fixes this. Everyone answers the same questions, with evidence, so others can check the result and argue with it. **Our advice:** use the five questions below. Each one comes from the vendor guides. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**1. Value: is it worth it?** The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) say to start with "a rough picture of how much a failure by the LLM costs you, and how much a success saves or earns you". Value is roughly volume × time saved, plus any gain the business cares about.

**2. Fit: does the task suit a language model?** The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/use-case-guides/ticket-routing) list signs that a model beats older machine learning for sorting tasks. Among them:

- "You have limited labeled training data available". The model can work "with just a few dozen labeled examples".
- Categories that are "likely to change or evolve over time".
- "You need to handle complex, unstructured text inputs" (free text, not form fields).
- "You need multilingual support without maintaining separate models".

**3. Success: can you say what good looks like?** The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests) say good criteria are "Specific", "Measurable", "Achievable" and "Relevant". Their contrast: "Safe outputs" is bad. "Less than 0.1% of outputs out of 10,000 trials flagged for toxicity by the content filter" is good. Be realistic too: the OpenAI docs say models are "unlikely to get to 99.999% accuracy using off-the-shelf methods". The [Anthropic post on agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) adds that agents add the most value for tasks that "have clear success criteria".

**4. Risk: what does a mistake cost?** The OpenAI docs compare "a bad copy that a human needs to fix" with "refunding a customer $1000 rather than $100". In their customer service example, when a fraud case could be resolved wrongly, "the human would drive and the AI would function as an assistant". So high risk can mean a different design, not only "no".

**5. Ready: can you test it?** The ticket routing guide lists "A sample set of historical support tickets for testing" as something you need before you start. No past examples with known answers means no way to check the model.

Score each question from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong). In the risk column, a 3 means a mistake costs little and is quick to put right.

Our example: a score card is like a school report with teacher comments. One low mark in a key subject matters more than a good average.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak ranking

```text
1. Automatic spare-part prices   "biggest money"
2. Sort supplier emails          "boring but fine"
3. Driver incident summaries     "nice to have"
```

What goes wrong: no evidence, no shared questions, and nobody asked what a mistake costs.

### Step 2: collect the evidence

```text
Supplier emails:   6,000 a month, free text in 3 languages,
                   new topics each season, 2 years of sorted emails
Spare-part prices: AI sets the price alone; a wrong price is live
                   in the web shop at once; success = "better prices"
Incident reports:  new form started last month, 40 reports so far,
                   no agreed good summary yet
```

### Step 3: score with one line of evidence each

```text
                    value fit success risk* ready total
Supplier emails       3    3     3     3     3    15
Spare-part prices     3    2     1     1     2     9
Incident summaries    1    2     2     2     1     8

* Risk 3 = a mistake costs little
```

- **Supplier emails, fit 3:** free text, three languages, topics that change.
- **Supplier emails, success 3:** "95% of emails sorted to the right team, on 500 past emails".
- **Spare-part prices, success 1:** "better prices" can't be measured.
- **Incident summaries, ready 1:** no past reports with agreed good summaries to test against.

### Step 4: the hard case

**Spare-part prices has high value but a 1 on risk and on success.** The total (9) hides two warning signs. Do not just drop it. Change the design and score it again:

```text
New design: AI suggests a price and a reason; a buyer approves it.
New success line: "On 300 past price changes, the suggestion is within
5% of the buyer's price 80% of the time."
Risk: 3 (nothing goes live without a person). Success: 3.
```

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Evidence on every score.** Others can check and argue with each number.
- **Warning signs are visible.** A 1 on success or risk is read before the total.
- **Design over drop.** High risk led to "AI suggests, a person decides".
- **Easy to update.** After a pilot, replace guesses with real numbers.

## Common mistakes

1. **Reading only the total.** Close totals or a hidden 1 can mislead. Fix: read every 1 first, and discuss totals that are close.
2. **Scores with no evidence.** A number with no reason can't be checked. Fix: one line of evidence per score.
3. **Vague success.** "Make it better" can't be tested. Fix: write a number, a test set and a target.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Rewrite these two success lines so they are specific and measurable. Then say which risk score (1 to 3) fits each task.

```text
P. Summarize customer calls for the account manager.
   Success: "useful summaries"
Q. Decide alone whether to cancel a supplier contract.
   Success: "the right call every time"
```

### Answer

- **P:** "On 100 past calls, the account manager rates 90% of summaries as usable with no edits." Risk 3: a weak summary is cheap to fix.
- **Q:** "On 50 past decisions, the AI's recommendation matches the legal team's decision 90% of the time." "Every time" is not achievable. Risk 1: a wrong cancellation is expensive, so a person should decide and the AI should assist.

## Recap

- Score every candidate on value, fit, success, risk and readiness, with evidence for each score.
- Read every 1 before the total. A low score on success or risk needs a fix or a new design, whatever the sum says.
- Write success as a number you can test, and keep past examples with known answers to test it on.
