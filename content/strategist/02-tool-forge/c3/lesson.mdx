## Why it matters

After mapping and counting, you usually have several candidates. Without a shared way to compare them, the loudest idea wins. A simple score card makes the choice visible, so others can check it and argue with it.

The score card below is a tool for this course. Each question comes from the vendor guides.

## Question 1: Is there real value?

OpenAI's guide on optimizing accuracy says to start with a rough picture of how much a failure by the model costs you, and how much a success saves or earns you. This question covers the success side; Question 4 covers failures.

Use your numbers from the last chapter: how often it happens and how long it takes. Value is roughly volume × time saved, plus any gain in speed or quality the business cares about.

Example: 2,500 listings × 12 minutes is about 500 hours a month. A yearly board deck is a few weeks once a year.

## Question 2: Does the task suit a language model?

Anthropic's ticket routing guide lists signs that an LLM is a better fit than traditional machine learning for a classification task. Among them:

- You have **limited labeled data**. The guide says Claude can classify tickets with just a few dozen labeled examples.
- Your **categories are likely to change** over time.
- The inputs are **complex, unstructured text**.
- The rules depend on **meaning**, not keywords.
- You need **explanations** for each decision.
- You need **many languages** without separate models.

Example: routing customer emails written in free text, in four languages, with categories that change each season, scores high. Sorting forms where a field already says "Billing" scores low: a simple rule can do it.

## Question 3: Can you say what success looks like?

Anthropic's guide on success criteria says good criteria are **specific, measurable, achievable and relevant**. It gives a contrast. Bad: "safe outputs". Good: "less than 0.1% of outputs out of 10,000 trials flagged for toxicity". It also lists criteria beyond accuracy, including tone, privacy, latency and price.

Achievable matters too. OpenAI's accuracy guide says LLMs are unlikely to reach 99.999% accuracy using off-the-shelf methods. So "never make a mistake" is not a target you can meet.

Anthropic's "Building effective agents" post makes a related point. It says agents add the most value for tasks that have **clear success criteria**, enable **feedback loops** and include **meaningful human oversight**.

Example: "Route 90% of tickets to the right team on the first try" scores high. "Make support better" scores low.

## Question 4: What does a mistake cost?

OpenAI's guide on optimizing accuracy says to enter any discussion of accuracy with a rough idea of what a failure costs and what a success saves. Its example: a bad piece of copy that a human must fix is very different from refunding a customer $1000 instead of $100.

The same guide describes a customer service case. Where a mistake was very costly, such as a fraud case resolved wrongly, the team decided a human would lead and the AI would only assist.

So a high cost of error doesn't always mean "no". It can mean "AI drafts, a person decides".

Example: a mistake in a product listing is fixed in minutes and scores low risk. A mistake in a refund over 1,000 euros scores high risk, so that use case scores lower unless a person approves each refund.

## Question 5: Are you ready to test it?

Anthropic's ticket routing guide lists a sample set of historical tickets for testing as a prerequisite. Without real examples, you can't check the model against the work.

Ask: do we have past examples with known right answers? Is there an owner who can judge results?

Example: a support team with two years of labeled tickets is ready. A new process that started last week isn't.

## Put it together

Score each question from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong), and write one line of evidence next to each score. The evidence matters more than the total.

```
                     Value  Fit  Success  Risk*  Ready  Total
Email routing          3     3      3       3      3     15
Refund decisions       2     2      1       1      2      8
Board deck             1     2      1       2      1      7

* Risk: 3 = mistakes are cheap and easy to fix
```

Read the card with care:

- A **1 on success or risk** is a warning, even if the total is high. Fix that first, or change the design so a person decides.
- **Close totals** need a discussion, not a winner by one point.
- **Re-score** after a pilot, with real numbers instead of guesses.

## Key points

- Score every candidate on the same questions, with evidence for each score.
- Value: volume × time saved. Fit: free text, changing categories, meaning-based rules.
- Success must be specific and measurable, not "make it better".
- Price the cost of a mistake. High risk can mean "AI assists, a person decides".
- No past examples to test with means the use case isn't ready yet.
