## Why it matters

A sponsor needs four answers before saying yes: what it saves, what it costs, what can go wrong, and how you will know if it works. "AI will make us faster" answers none of them.

A business case puts numbers on each one, including the cost of mistakes.

## Price every outcome, not only success

OpenAI's guide on optimizing accuracy describes a customer service case. The team first listed the main success and failure cases and put an estimated value on each:

- A case solved by AI instead of a person saves **$20**.
- A case wrongly escalated to a person costs **$40**.
- In the worst case, a frustrated customer leaves, costing **$1000**. The guide assumes this happens in 5% of cases; in its table, that is 5% of the AI failures.

Then they asked: at what accuracy does this break even? The guide's table, where the cases add up to 1,000, looks like this:

```
Event                     Value   Cases    Total
AI success                 +20    815     +16,300
AI failure (escalation)    -40    175.75   -7,030
AI failure (customer lost) -1000  9.25     -9,250
Result                                       +20
Break-even accuracy                         81.5%
```

So below about 81.5% accuracy, this design loses money. Above it, it saves money. That one number turns "Is the AI good enough?" into a question the business can answer.

## Measure today's baseline

You can't show a gain without knowing where you start. OpenAI's guide says the team also measured the process itself, comparing human and AI on customer satisfaction scores, decision accuracy on reviewed cases and time to resolution.

Anthropic's ticket routing guide lists success criteria that apply whether or not an LLM is used. Examples: routing accuracy, time-to-assignment, rerouting rate, average handling time, customer satisfaction and cost per ticket. Its customer support guide adds business measures such as deflection rate: the share of questions handled without a person.

Example: before the pilot, a support team measures one month. Average handling time 9 minutes, rerouting rate 18%, cost per ticket 6 euros. Those are the numbers the business case promises to move.

## Estimate the running cost

OpenAI's production guide suggests one useful way to think about cost: the number of tokens times the cost per token. To estimate the number of tokens, it says to consider traffic levels, how often users will interact with your application and the amount of data you will process.

Anthropic's pricing page gives a worked example: 10,000 support tickets at about 3,700 tokens each, on Claude Haiku 4.5 at $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens, cost about $37 in total. Use this as a sense of scale only. Check the live pricing page yourself, and write down the page and the date you checked it, so others can verify your number.

Both guides also list ways to lower cost. OpenAI's names smaller models for some tasks, shorter prompts and caching common queries. Anthropic's names choosing the right model, prompt caching, the Batch API for work that isn't time-sensitive, and tracking usage.

Model tokens are only part of the bill. Add the people costs too: building, reviewing outputs and keeping the system up to date.

## Let the numbers shape the design

The business case is not only a yes or no. In OpenAI's example, the numbers changed the design. The team found that even 85% accuracy could be acceptable if most of the failures were early escalations to a person. Where a failure was very costly, such as a fraud case resolved wrongly, they had a human lead and the AI assist.

Example: if your break-even accuracy is 81.5% and the pilot shows 84%, the margin is thin. OpenAI's guide lists one option: tell the model to hand off to a person when the intent is unclear. That gives up some savings now, but an escalation costs $40 and a lost customer costs $1000.

## A one-page structure

1. **Problem**: the step from your map, with volume and time.
2. **Baseline**: today's numbers.
3. **Value model**: the value of success and of each failure, and the break-even accuracy.
4. **Running cost**: token estimate, price page and the date you checked it, plus people costs.
5. **Risks and controls**: where a person stays in charge.
6. **The ask**: a pilot, with the numbers that will decide what happens next.

## Key points

- Put a value on success and on each kind of failure, then find the break-even accuracy.
- Measure today's baseline before you promise a gain.
- Running cost is roughly tokens × price per token; name the price page and the date you checked it.
- Add people costs: building, review and maintenance.
- Let the numbers shape the design, such as handing off to a person when unsure.
