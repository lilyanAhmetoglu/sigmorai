## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Put a money value on success and on each kind of failure, and find the **break-even accuracy**: the share of right answers where the AI stops losing money.
- Measure today's baseline and estimate the running cost, with a dated price.
- Use the numbers to change the design, not only to say yes or no.

## The problem

A car insurer gets about 12,000 chat questions a month about claim status. The customer team wants an AI assistant to answer them. The sponsor asks four things: what does it save, what does it cost, what can go wrong, and how will we know it works?

"AI will make us faster" answers none of them. A **business case** puts a number on each answer, including the cost of mistakes. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Price every outcome, not only success.** The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) show this with a customer service example. A case solved by AI "may save **$20**". A wrong escalation to a person "might cost **$40**". In the worst case a customer leaves, "costing us **$1000**". The guide assumes this "happens in 5% of cases". Their table adds it up over 1,000 cases:

```text
Event                    Value   Cases    Total
AI success                +20    815     16,300
AI failure (escalation)   -40    175.75   7,030
AI failure (churn)      -1000      9.25   9,250
Result                                      +20
Break-even accuracy                       81.5%
```

Below 81.5% right answers, the design loses money. Above it, it saves money. That one number turns "Is the AI good enough?" into a question the business can answer. The OpenAI docs add that "an accuracy of even 85% could be ok, if those 15% were primarily early escalations", because an early hand-off costs far less than a lost customer.

**Measure today first.** In the same example, the OpenAI docs measure human and AI side by side on "The CSAT score" (customer satisfaction), "The decision accuracy for retrospectively reviewed cases" and "The time to resolution". The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/use-case-guides/ticket-routing) list similar measures, such as rerouting rate, average handling time and cost per ticket, and say they apply "regardless of whether an LLM is used". The [support guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/use-case-guides/customer-support-chat) adds **deflection rate**: "The percentage of customer inquiries successfully handled by the chatbot without human intervention".

**Estimate the running cost.** The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/production-best-practices) say to "consider costs as a function of the number of tokens and the cost per token". To estimate tokens, consider "traffic levels, the frequency with which users will interact with your application, and the amount of data you will be processing". A **token** is a small piece of text; the same page says 1,000 tokens are "roughly equal to 750 words".

**Prices change, so date them.** The [Claude pricing page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing) lists input and output tokens separately. For example, Claude Haiku 5.5 (prompts up to 100,000 tokens) is "$0.10 / MTok" for input and "$0.50 / MTok" for output (MTok = a million tokens). **Our advice:** next to every price, write down the page and the day you looked, so others can check it.

**Both vendors list ways to cut cost.** The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/production-best-practices) name "switching to smaller models for some tasks", "using shorter prompts" and "caching common user queries". The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing) name choosing the right model, prompt caching, the Batch API "for non-time-sensitive tasks", and tracking token use. **Prompt caching** means reusing the same long prompt text instead of paying for it in full each time.

Our example: a business case is like a household budget for a new car. The price tag is only one line. Fuel, insurance and repairs decide if you can afford it.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak case

```text
"The AI will answer claim-status chats. It's cheap and it will
save a lot of agent time. Model cost: small."
```

What goes wrong: no value per outcome, no baseline, no token estimate, and nothing on mistakes.

### Step 2: price each outcome

The team agrees these values (made up for this example):

```text
AI gives a right answer   saves 6 EUR in staff time
AI gives a wrong answer   the customer phones in angry: costs 30 EUR
AI passes it to an agent  costs 2 EUR (the agent reads the AI's note)
```

### Step 3: find the break-even accuracy

With no hand-off, every answer is right or wrong. Break-even is where savings equal losses:

```text
6 × right = 30 × wrong
right share = 30 ÷ (6 + 30) = 83.3%
```

Below 83.3% right, the assistant loses money.

### Step 4: measure the baseline and the running cost

```text
Baseline (one month, today):
  handling time 4 min, cost per chat 3.10 EUR,
  agents give the right status 97% of the time

Tokens per month (estimate):
  12,000 chats × 2,000 input tokens = 24 million  -> about 2.40 USD
  12,000 chats × 300 output tokens  = 3.6 million -> about 1.80 USD
  (Claude Haiku 5.5, Claude pricing page, checked October 2026)

People: 2 days a month to review samples and update the prompt
```

The model tokens are small here. The people costs are bigger, so they belong in the case.

### Step 5: the hard case

The pilot shows **86% right**. That is above 83.3%, but only just.

```text
Per 1,000 chats, no hand-off rule:
  860 right × 6  = +5,160
  140 wrong × 30 = -4,200
  Result         =   +960
```

**This is the hard case: the margin is thin.** A small drop in accuracy wipes it out. So the team changes the design. The model hands off to an agent when it is not sure, an option the OpenAI docs also list ("hand off to a human if the intent is unclear"). A new test shows:

```text
Per 1,000 chats, with hand-off rule:
  840 right × 6   = +5,040
  100 handed off × 2 = -200
   60 wrong × 30  = -1,800
  Result          = +3,040
```

Fewer chats are answered by AI, but the result is more than three times better.

### Step 6: why it is better

- **Every outcome priced**, so the real break-even point is visible.
- **A baseline**, so the gain can be proved later.
- **A dated cost estimate**, with people costs included.
- **Design changed by numbers.** The hand-off rule turned costly mistakes into cheap hand-offs.

## Common mistakes

1. **Reporting accuracy alone.** 80% right sounds good, but it can lose money. Fix: price each kind of failure and compare with break-even.
2. **No baseline.** You can't show a gain without the starting point. Fix: measure one month of today's process first.
3. **Only the token bill.** Review and upkeep often cost more than tokens. Fix: add people costs to the running cost.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A shop's AI answers return questions. A right answer saves 3 EUR. A wrong answer costs 12 EUR. There is no hand-off.

```text
1. Where is the break-even point?
2. The pilot shows 85% right. Does it make or lose money per 1,000 questions?
```

### Answer

1. **80%.** 12 ÷ (3 + 12) = 0.8.
2. **It makes money, but not much.** 850 × 3 = 2,550. 150 × 12 = 1,800. Result: +750 per 1,000 questions. The margin is thin, so a hand-off rule for unclear questions is worth testing.

## Recap

- Put a value on success and on every kind of failure, then work out the break-even accuracy.
- Measure today's baseline. Estimate running cost from the token count and the price of each token, say where and when you found the price, and add the cost of people.
- Let the numbers shape the design, for example a hand-off to a person when the model is unsure.
