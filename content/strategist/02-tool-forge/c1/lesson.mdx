## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Map how a piece of work really runs today, one step at a time.
- Write each step with the same five fields, so steps are easy to compare.
- Mark each step as an AI candidate, a person's decision, or not worth it.

## The problem

An HR team at a logistics company hires about 30 people a month. The head of HR says: "Let's use AI for onboarding." Nobody can say which part of onboarding they mean.

"Onboarding" is many small jobs. Some are simple and repeat every week. Some need a person's judgment. If you pick a tool first, you automate a guess. This lesson shows how to draw the work first, so you can see where AI fits. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Start with how people do it today.** The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/use-case-guides/ticket-routing) say: "Before you automate, it's crucial to understand your existing ticketing system." They suggest questions such as "How are edge cases or ambiguous tickets handled?" and "Are there any automated rules or workflows already in place? In what cases do they fail?" The reason, in short: when you know how people handle each kind of case, you can "work with Claude to do the task" better. The guide is about support tickets, but the habit works for any process.

**Break the big job into tasks.** The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/use-case-guides/customer-support-chat) call support chat "a collection of multiple different tasks". They say to break the ideal interaction "into every task" before you build, so you can "prompt and evaluate" each one. The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices) show the same idea with an order question. The workflow has three steps: "Extracting an Order ID", "Looking up the order details", and "Providing the order details to a model for a final response". The guide then lists what to test at each step.

**Say what "good" looks like.** An [OpenAI prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/chatgpt/chatgpt_prompt_guide/chatgpt_prompt_guide) asks: "What will I judge as 'good'?" Its rule of thumb: if "good" takes more than one or two sentences to describe, the model "can't reliably hit it".

**See if the path is fixed.** The [Anthropic post on agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) separates two designs. A **workflow** runs "through predefined code paths" (steps written in advance). An **agent** decides its own steps and tools as it goes. The post recommends "finding the simplest solution possible", which "might mean not building agentic systems at all". Agents fit open-ended work, but they bring "higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors" (small mistakes that grow).

**Our advice:** write every step with the same five fields.

```text
Trigger   what starts the step
Input     what the person reads or uses
Action    what they do
Output    what they produce
Owner     who does it, and how long it takes
```

Our example: a map is like a recipe written down while you watch the cook, not copied from the cookbook. The real kitchen has shortcuts and "a bit of this" that the book never shows.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak plan

```text
Goal: use AI for onboarding.
Next step: ask three vendors for a demo.
```

What goes wrong:

- "Onboarding" is too big to test. Nobody can say if the AI did it well.
- The demo will show what the tool does, not what HR needs.

### Step 2: watch the real work

You sit with the HR coordinator for a morning. Together you walk through the last five new hires, from signed offer to first day. You write down what really happened, including the shortcuts.

### Step 3: write the map

```text
1. Trigger: offer signed       Input: offer, HR form
   Action: create employee record          Output: record
   Owner: HR, 10 min
2. Trigger: record created     Input: job title
   Action: request email and system logins Output: IT ticket
   Owner: HR, 5 min
3. Trigger: start date known   Input: role, manager's wishes
   Action: choose laptop and access level  Output: order
   Owner: HR and manager, 1-2 days
4. Trigger: one week to go     Input: template, start date
   Action: write welcome email, first-day plan  Output: email
   Owner: HR, 15 min
5. Trigger: first day          Input: ID and work permit
   Action: check documents in person       Output: signed check
   Owner: HR, 20 min
```

### Step 4: ask "what is good?" for each output

- Step 2: good = the right ticket with the right list of systems. Easy to say.
- Step 4: good = the template filled in with the right name, date, place and manager. Easy to say.
- Step 3: the coordinator says, "The manager just tells us. Some contractors get full access, some don't." Nobody can say what good looks like.

### Step 5: mark each step (the hard case)

```text
1. Create record      AI candidate: clear input, clear output
2. Request logins     AI candidate
3. Laptop + access    Person decides: no written rule, and wrong
                      access is a security risk
4. Welcome email      AI candidate: AI drafts, HR sends
5. Check documents    Person decides: a legal check, in person
```

**Step 3 is the hard case.** It takes the longest, so it looks like the best target. But the rule lives in managers' heads, and a mistake gives someone access they should not have. First write the rule down ("contractors get these three systems"). Then look at it again.

### Step 6: why it is better

- **Small tasks.** "Draft the welcome email" can be tested. "Use AI for onboarding" can't.
- **Real work.** The map shows the shortcut at step 3 that no handbook shows.
- **Fixed path.** The same steps happen for every hire, so a simple workflow is enough. No agent is needed.
- **Better demo questions.** You can now ask a vendor about steps 2 and 4 only.

## Common mistakes

1. **Mapping the official process.** Written procedures hide shortcuts and unwritten rules. Fix: watch real recent cases with the people who do the work.
2. **One giant task.** "Automate HR" can't be tested or measured. Fix: split it until each step has one clear output.
3. **Automating a step nobody can judge.** If people can't agree what good looks like, the model can't hit it. Fix: write the rule first, or leave the step with a person.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

An events team handles requests from people who want to speak at its conference. Mark each step: AI candidate, person decides, or not worth it.

```text
1. Copy name, topic and email from each request into a sheet
   (300 a year, 3 min each)
2. Decide which talks fit this year's theme (no written rule)
3. Send a "thanks, we got your request" email (already automatic)
4. Write a short speaker bio for the website from their CV
   (a template exists)
```

### Answer

1. **AI candidate.** Clear input and a clear output: the right fields in the sheet.
2. **Person decides.** "Fits the theme" has no rule anyone can write in two sentences.
3. **Not worth it.** A simple automatic reply already does it.
4. **AI candidate.** A template exists, so "good" is easy to describe. A person checks the bio before it goes online.

## Recap

- Map the work as people really do it today, not as the official process says.
- Break big jobs into small steps, each with one clear output you can judge.
- Mark each step. Keep steps with unwritten rules or high risk with a person, and choose a simple fixed workflow when the path is the same every time.
