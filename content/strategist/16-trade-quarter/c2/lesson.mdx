## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Use AI to pull the key terms out of a long contract into a short sheet a person can check.
- Spot the two ways a contract summary goes wrong: a point that is missing, and a point that is made up.
- Know where the line is: AI helps you read, and a qualified person (for a contract, a lawyer) checks before anyone signs.

## The problem

Contracts are long, and most people at work are not lawyers. A supplier sends 20 pages. A partner sends a data agreement. Someone has to say what is in it, and fast.

AI tools are good at reading long text. But a contract summary can go wrong in a quiet way. It can skip the one clause that costs money. Or it can state a term that the contract does not have. Both look the same on the page: a clean, sure summary.

This lesson shows how to use AI to read a contract, and where a person must step in. The quotes are from the docs of Anthropic, OpenAI and Google, and from the Anthropic Usage Policy, read in October 2026.

## The idea

**1. What AI does well: read long documents and pull out the key terms.** The tools can read very long files and find the parts you name.

- The [Claude docs on legal summarization](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/use-case-guides/legal-summarization) say a model can "extract and categorize important metadata from legal documents, such as parties involved, dates, contract terms, or specific clauses."
- The [Gemini API docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/document-processing) say Gemini can "Analyze and interpret content, including text, images, diagrams, charts, and tables, even in long documents up to 1000 pages."
- The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/file-inputs) explain what the model sees. For a PDF on a vision model, the API "extracts both text and page images". For a Word file it "extracts text only".

**2. The main risk: a missing or made-up clause.** There are two ways to fail.

- **Missing.** The Claude docs say: "There is no single correct summary for any given document. Without clear direction, it can be difficult for Claude to determine which details to include." If you don't ask for a term, the summary may skip it.
- **Made up.** The [Claude docs on hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations) say a model "can sometimes generate text that is factually incorrect or inconsistent with the given context". A hallucination is text like this: it sounds right, but it is not true.

The same page gives three ways to cut this down:

- Let the model say it does not know: "Explicitly give Claude permission to admit uncertainty."
- Ground it in the text. For long documents (over 20,000 tokens; a token is a small piece of a word), "ask Claude to extract word-for-word quotes first before performing its task."
- Check every claim. Have the model "cite quotes and sources for each of its claims". Then: "If it can't find a quote, it must retract the claim" (retract: take back).

The page also warns: these methods "don't eliminate them entirely. Always validate critical information, especially for high-stakes decisions."

**3. The human check: a qualified person (for a contract, a lawyer) reads before anyone signs.** The [ChatGPT docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/use-chatgpt) are direct: "Legal, financial, medical, security, and other high-stakes decisions require appropriate expert review. Use ChatGPT to support informed judgment, not replace it."

The [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup) draws the line in more detail. Legal is one of its "High-risk Areas": "advising an individual on their legal rights, obligations, or liability, or on what to do in a specific legal matter, or preparing legal documents for them to sign or file." (Obligations: what you must do. Liability: what you must pay if something goes wrong.) For those uses, "A qualified person must meaningfully review" the AI's output first. But some uses are not high-risk. The policy lists "General information or educational content, including explaining what a law says", and "Internal drafting, research, summarization, or analysis".

Our reading of these pages: a summary for your own team to read is fine. Advice that a person will act on, or a document someone signs, needs a qualified person. This lesson is not legal advice. For a real contract, ask your lawyer.

A small comparison: the AI summary is a map of the contract. A map helps you find your way. You still walk the road before you sign.

## Worked example

Leila runs operations at a 40-person online shop. A software vendor sends a 14-page subscription agreement for a new stock system. Leila's boss wants one page: what do we pay, for how long, and how do we get out?

### Step 1: the weak prompt

```text
Summarize this contract.
```

The tool returns a neat page about "the services", "the parties" and "fees". It is all true, but it does not say how long the deal runs or how to cancel. Leila did not ask, so the tool picked other points.

### Step 2: say exactly what to pull out

Leila uses the company's approved tool, uploads the whole file with its annexes, and writes:

```text
You help me read a software subscription agreement
(attached). I am the customer.

1. Find these terms. For each one, give a word-for-word
   quote and its clause number:
   - Price, and how the price can change
   - Length of the deal (the term)
   - Renewal: does it renew by itself?
   - How to cancel, and the notice period
   - What happens to our data when we leave
   - Limits on what the vendor pays if things go wrong
2. If a term is not in the document, write NOT FOUND.
   Do not guess.
3. Then write a one-page summary that uses only
   the quotes above.
```

### Step 3: the hard case, a term that hides

**This is the hard case.** The weak summary in Step 1 never said a word about renewal. Now the list asks for it, and the tool finds it in a place nobody looks: Annex B, page 13, not the main text. The quote says the deal **renews by itself for another 12 months** unless the customer cancels at least 90 days before the end. The current term ends on 31 December.

Ninety days before the end is a date someone must put in a calendar. Leila adds a line to the top of the summary: "Cancel by 2 October, or we pay for another year." Then Leila searches the PDF for each of the six quotes. All six are really in the file.

### Step 4: mark what is still open

Leila adds a short list to the top of the summary: three clauses that need a lawyer's view (the auto-renewal, the data clause, and the vendor's limit on what it pays). Each one has its quote, so a reader can find it in the contract fast.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Nothing important is left to chance.** The prompt names the terms the business cares about.
- **Every point can be checked.** Each one has a quote and a clause number.
- **Open points are named.** The three clauses that need expert review sit at the top, each with its quote.

## Common mistakes

1. **Uploading the contract without its annexes.** Annexes (extra pages at the end) often hold the prices and the service levels. Fix: upload the whole document, and ask the tool to list the annexes it found.
2. **Not saying which side you are on.** "What are the risks?" means different things for the buyer and the seller. Fix: start the prompt with "I am the customer" or "I am the supplier".
3. **Putting a contract into a tool your company has not approved.** Many contracts have a confidentiality clause (a rule that you keep the contract secret). Fix (our advice): use only the approved tool, and check that clause first.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

Your team is booking a hotel hall for a 2-day staff event. The hotel sends a 9-page booking agreement. Write the list of terms you would ask an AI tool to find. Aim for six terms.

### Answer

One good list (yours may differ):

- The price, and what it includes (room, food, tech)
- The deposit, and when it is due
- Cancel rules: the dates, and how much you lose at each date
- The minimum number of guests you pay for, even if fewer come
- Who pays for damage to the hall
- What happens if the hotel cancels

Then add the two rules from the worked example: NOT FOUND when a term is missing, and a summary built only from the quotes. A booking agreement with a lot of money in it still goes to a person who can sign for the company.

## Recap

- AI can read long contracts and pull out the key terms, but only the terms you ask for. Name them.
- Make every point carry the contract's exact words and where they sit, and let the tool say a term is not there.
- A summary for your team is fine. Advice someone acts on, or a document someone signs, needs a qualified person. Our advice: for a contract, that is a lawyer.
