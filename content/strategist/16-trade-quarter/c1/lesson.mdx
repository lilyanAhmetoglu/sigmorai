## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Name the HR jobs where AI can safely help: drafts, summaries and answers about written policy.
- Spot the HR uses that Anthropic, Google and the EU treat as high-risk: AI that decides about a person.
- Set the human check for those uses: who reviews, when, and who must be told.

## The problem

HR (human resources, the team that hires people and looks after staff) has a lot of writing. Job ads, policy answers, interview notes, letters. AI tools can help with all of it, and many vendors now sell AI for HR.

But HR work is also about people's jobs and money. A wrong answer in a slide is a small problem. A wrong decision about who gets a job, or who gets fewer hours, can hurt a real person, and that person may never know why.

So the question for HR is not "AI or no AI". It is: **which part of the work can AI do, and where must a person decide?** This lesson uses the usage policies of Anthropic and Google, the European Commission's page on the EU AI Act (the EU's law on AI), NIST (a US standards body) and the OpenAI docs, all read in October 2026.

## The idea

Each chapter in this realm looks at a work area in three parts: what AI does well, the main risk, and the human check.

**1. What AI does well in HR: words that a person checks.** First drafts of job ads, plain answers about a written policy, short summaries of long notes. The [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup) has a list of uses that are _not_ high-risk. It includes "Internal drafting, research, summarization, or analysis that is not the ultimate High-risk AI Recommendation delivered." It also includes "Business operations that do not advise or decide about a specific individual."

**2. The main risk: AI that decides about a person.** The same policy has a list of "High-risk Areas". These are uses that can affect a person's "health, legal rights, finances, livelihoods" (livelihood: the way a person earns a living). One area is Employment:

> "screening, ranking, advancing, or rejecting candidates; hiring; promoting or demoting; setting or changing shifts, hours, or pay; evaluating a worker's performance or behavior, including through disciplining, suspending, or terminating a worker."

Look at how wide this list is. It is not only hiring. It covers people who already work for you.

Google's policy points the same way. The [Google Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy](https://policies.google.com/terms/generative-ai/use-policy) bans using its AI for content that "Makes automated decisions that have a material detrimental impact on individual rights without human supervision in high-risk domains". Its first example is "employment".

The EU goes further, with a law. The [European Commission's AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) says:

- High-risk uses include "AI tools for employment, management of workers and access to self-employment (e.g. CV-sorting software for recruitment)".
- "Starting on 2 December 2027, high-risk AI systems will be subject to strict obligations before they can be put on the market" (obligations: things you must do). One duty is "appropriate human oversight measures".
- Why: it can be hard to tell "whether someone has been unfairly disadvantaged, such as in a hiring decision".

The page also lists nine practices that are banned. One is "emotion recognition in workplaces and education institutions": AI that tries to read people's feelings at work.

Why is hiring so risky? Bias. The [NIST FAQ page](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-risk-management-framework-faqs) lists the traits of a trustworthy AI system, and one is "fair with harmful bias managed". The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/safety-best-practices) name bias among the known limits of language models. We found no OpenAI page on hiring itself. Its general rule is in the next part.

**3. The human check: a qualified person decides, and the person affected is told.** The Anthropic Usage Policy sets two rules for a high-risk use:

- **Review first.** "A qualified person must meaningfully review a High-risk AI Recommendation, with the authority to change or modify it, before it is delivered as advice to an individual who might rely on it or before it is used to implement a decision." A qualified person is "someone with the training or experience to evaluate the output in that field".
- **Tell the person.** Someone who is the subject of such a decision "must be clearly told that AI was used to produce it." The policy adds: "Users do not need to identify Anthropic, Claude, or the model used."

The policy also says local law comes first: "Users should always default to following local laws if those laws conflict with or add additional requirements".

The OpenAI docs give the general rule for any field: "Wherever possible, we recommend having a human review outputs before they are used in practice. This is especially critical in high-stakes domains". The person checking should "have access to any information needed to verify the outputs".

This is our reading of public pages, not legal advice. Rules differ by country. Before your team turns on an AI tool that screens, ranks or plans staff, ask your legal team or a qualified employment adviser.

## Worked example

Rui works in HR at a 120-person hotel group. Staff send HR the same policy questions every week: holidays, sick days, uniforms. Rui wants a chat app to draft answers from the staff handbook.

### Step 1: the weak start

Rui's first try:

```text
How many sick days do hotel workers get?
```

The answer is a general one about "many countries". It is not the hotel's rule, and it sounds sure. Nothing in it comes from the handbook.

### Step 2: ground it in the handbook

Rui uses the company's approved AI tool, attaches the handbook, and writes:

```text
You draft answers to staff questions about our staff
handbook (attached). Use only the handbook.

For each answer:
- Quote the handbook section you used, with its number.
- If the handbook does not answer the question, write:
  "Not in the handbook. Please ask HR."
- Never give advice about one person's own case. Write:
  "For your own situation, please talk to HR."

Question: How many days of sick leave can I take
without a doctor's note?
```

Now the draft quotes section 7.2 of the handbook, and Rui can check it in one minute.

### Step 3: the hard case, a gap in the handbook

**This is the hard case.** A worker asks how many days of leave a new parent gets. The draft gives a number of days. But it quotes no section. Rui searches the handbook: there is no section on parental leave at all. The number came from nowhere.

Rui does not send it. Rui asks the HR lead, who answers from the law and the contract. Then the HR lead adds a new section to the handbook, so next time the draft can quote it.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Checkable.** Every answer points to a section, so a person can check it fast.
- **Honest about gaps.** "Not in the handbook" is a better answer than a confident guess.
- **People stay with people.** Questions about one person's own case go to HR, not to the app.

## Common mistakes

1. **Pasting whole CVs or staff files into a personal AI account.** Fix (our advice): use only the tool your company approved, and leave out every detail the task does not need.
2. **A job ad that describes a type of person, not the work.** Fix (our advice): list the tasks and the must-have skills. Then ask who the wording might leave out.
3. **Filing AI-made interview notes as the record without the interviewer reading them.** Fix (our advice): the interviewer checks the notes against their own notes the same day. The OpenAI docs say the reviewer should have "easy access to the original notes".

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

An interviewer used an AI tool to turn rough interview notes into a clean summary. Here is the interviewer's own rough note and the summary. Find three things to fix before the summary is filed.

```text
ROUGH NOTE (interviewer)
  Talked 30 min. Knows the booking system well. Has run
  a front desk for 2 yrs. Wants mornings. Asked about
  the training plan.

AI SUMMARY
  The candidate has five years of front desk experience
  and knows the booking system well. Seems tired and
  not very motivated. Mentioned young children at home,
  so may not be reliable. Asked about training.
```

### Answer

- **A wrong fact.** The note says 2 years, the summary says five. Fix it from the note.
- **A guess about feelings.** "Seems tired and not very motivated" is not in the note. Cut it. A summary records what was said and done, not a reading of someone's mood.
- **A personal detail and an unfair guess.** Children at home are not in the note, and they have nothing to do with the job. "May not be reliable" judges a person by their family. Cut both.

The interviewer reads the fixed summary against the note before it goes in the file.

## Recap

- In HR, AI can help with words a person checks: job ads, answers from a written policy, summaries of notes.
- The Anthropic policy lists screening, ranking, rejecting, shifts, pay and performance as high-risk. Google names employment as a high-risk domain, and the EU AI Act lists employment tools as high-risk.
- For those, a qualified person reviews before the decision and can change it, and the person affected is told that AI was used.
