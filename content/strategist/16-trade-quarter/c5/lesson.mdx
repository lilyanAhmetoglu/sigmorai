## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Use AI as a tutor that asks questions and gives practice, not only answers.
- Spot the main risk in learning with AI: the learner copies the answer and learns nothing.
- Set the human check: the teacher writes the rules for the tool and checks what the learner really knows.

## The problem

Teaching happens in schools, and it happens at work too: a trainer shows new staff the systems, a team lead explains a new process, an apprentice studies for an exam. AI tools can help every one of them. They explain, they make practice questions, and they never get tired of the same question.

But a tool that answers everything can also do the learning _for_ the learner. A learner asks, copies the answer, hands it in, and remembers nothing a week later. The work looks done. The skill is not there.

So the question is: **how do you use AI so the learner does the thinking?** This lesson uses the Claude Help Center, the ChatGPT docs and Gemini Apps Help, read in October 2026.

## The idea

Like every chapter in this realm, the idea has three parts: what AI does well, the main risk, and the human check.

**1. What AI does well: a patient tutor and a practice partner.**

[Gemini Apps Help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/16448384) says: "You can use Gemini Apps as a tutor with guided learning, visual aids, and educational resources." On a computer, the page says you pick Guided Learning under "More tools" before you type your question. The same page has a section on OpenStax study tools, where you can "Ask Gemini Apps to give you practice questions with feedback and hints." That section has limits: for now these features are "only available in English and in the United States", and "not available to users under the age of 18 or users signed in with a work or school account". Gemini's [quiz page](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/16275879) adds that during a quiz you can "get hints, ask questions, and practice with more questions."

The [Claude Help Center page for students](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11139144-use-claude-for-education-at-your-university) lists study uses. Three of them:

- "Create practice questions on course material"
- "Explain complex concepts in simpler terms"
- "Provide step-by-step explanations for problem-solving"

The [ChatGPT docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/use-chatgpt) suggest short follow-ups that slow the tool down. One of them is "Ask me questions before you continue."

**2. The main risk: the learner copies the answer and learns nothing.**

The Claude Help Center puts it in one line for students: "While Claude can help you understand material and improve your work, it should not be used to complete assignments that you're expected to do independently." (Independently: on your own.)

The tool will not stop this by itself. If a learner asks for the answer, a helpful tool gives the answer. The ChatGPT docs also warn that "A polished response can still be incomplete or wrong". So a learner who copies may copy a mistake too, and never notice.

**3. The human check: the teacher sets the rules, and checks what the learner knows.**

The teacher (or trainer) owns two jobs. AI does neither of them.

**Job 1: set the rules for the tool.** Both apps let you save instructions that every chat in a project follows:

- The [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/10185728-understanding-claude-s-personalization-features) says project instructions "only apply to chats within that project". You can use them to "Define roles or perspectives Claude should adopt within the project".
- The [ChatGPT docs on projects](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/projects) say: "Project instructions apply across its chats."

So a teacher can set up one project for a class and write the tutor rules into it. Our advice for those rules: the tool asks the learner a question first, offers a hint before any solution, and explains only after the learner has tried. The teacher tests the project before learners use it.

**Job 2: check the learning, not only the hand-in.** A finished page can come from the tool. Understanding cannot. Our advice: check what the learner can do **without** the tool. Ask them to explain one step in their own words, or to solve a new small problem on paper. If they can, the tool helped them learn. If they can't, it did the work for them.

There is one more rule, about learners' data. The Claude Help Center page on [Claude for Teachers](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/15926041-claude-for-teachers-your-data-and-our-terms) says:

- "Whether and how identifiable student information may be used in Claude for Teachers is determined by your school or district's policies, not by Anthropic."
- "If you're on an individual teacher account and have not received school or district authorization to make that decision, don't include identifiable student information in Claude for Teachers."

(Identifiable: it shows who the person is.) Our reading: the school or the employer decides what learner data may go into an AI tool. Until it has decided, leave names out. This is our reading, not legal advice. Ask the person who looks after data protection where you work.

A small comparison: a good tutor is like a coach at the gym. The coach shows the move and corrects you, but you lift the weight. If the coach lifts it for you, you get no stronger.

A small diagram (ours):

```text
AI may          →  explain, give practice, give hints
The teacher     →  writes the tool's rules, tests them
The learner     →  tries first, does the thinking
The teacher     →  checks what the learner can do alone
```

## Worked example

Femi is the training lead at a 60-person accounting office. Every month, two or three new assistants join. They must learn the office's expense rules: what staff can claim, and which receipts they need. The rules are in a 6-page company document.

### Step 1: the weak start

Femi asks a chat app:

```text
Explain expense rules to new staff.
```

The answer is a general page about expenses "in most companies". It mentions rules this office does not have. It is the wrong source.

### Step 2: use the real document, at two levels

Femi uses the company's approved tool, uploads the expense document, and writes:

```text
You help me teach our expense rules (attached).
Use only the attached document.

1. Write a 150-word explanation for someone in their
   first week who has never filed an expense claim.
   Use one everyday example.
2. Write a 100-word version for someone who has
   filed claims at another company. Point out what
   is different here.
3. After each rule, put the section number in
   brackets, like (3.2).
```

The two versions read well. The first one uses a taxi ride as its example. The second one says, "Unlike many companies, we need the receipt photo within 5 days (3.2)."

### Step 3: the hard case, a rule that is not in the document

**This is the hard case.** The first-week version says: "Small costs under 10 do not need a receipt." It has no section number in brackets. Femi searches the document. There is no such rule. In this office, every claim needs a receipt.

This is exactly the kind of mistake a new person would believe and repeat. Femi deletes the sentence and adds a line to the prompt: "If a rule has no section number, do not include it."

### Step 4: Femi checks every rule

Femi reads both versions next to the document. Each rule now has a section number, and each number points to the right rule. Then Femi asks one experienced assistant to read the first-week version and say if anything is unclear.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **The right source.** The explanations come from this office's rules, not from "most companies".
- **Two levels.** A beginner and an experienced person each get what they need.
- **Every rule is checkable.** The section numbers make the check fast, and a rule with no number stands out.

## Common mistakes

1. **Planning a lesson around a feature before you check that your learners can use it.** Fix: read the feature's help page for limits on country, language, age and account type. For example, Gemini's quiz page says that on a work or school Google Account "you can’t share practice tests, quizzes, flashcards, or study guides".
2. **Letting the tool answer from general knowledge when you have a course text.** Fix: attach the course material. Gemini's quiz page says you can "select the source material you want to upload as context".
3. **Writing the tutor rules once and never trying them.** Fix (our advice): before learners start, play the learner yourself for five minutes and see if the tool follows the rules.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A team lead is planning a one-hour training session on a new booking system. Sort these six tasks into two groups: **AI may draft it (a person checks)** and **a person does it**.

```text
a. A welcome email for the session
b. Choosing what everyone must be able to do
   by the end of the hour
c. Three practice exercises in the test system
d. A one-page glossary of the system's terms
e. Talking with a person who is stuck and upset
f. Deciding who is ready to use the live system
```

### Answer

- **AI may draft it, a person checks:** a, c, d. These are drafts. The team lead reads them against the real system before the session.
- **A person does it:** b, e, f. Choosing the goals is the teacher's job. Helping an upset person needs a person. Deciding who is ready is a judgement about a person's skill, so a person makes it.

A tool can help with f only as an input, for example by making the practice exercises. The decision stays with the team lead.

## Recap

- AI can be a patient tutor: it explains at different levels, gives practice questions, and offers hints.
- The main risk is a learner who copies the answer. The tool looks helpful, and the learner learns nothing.
- The teacher writes the tool's rules in a project, checks what learners can do without the tool, and follows the school's or employer's rule on learner data.
