## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Pick one safe first AI workflow for a small team or a nonprofit with little money.
- Spot the two risks that small teams meet most: private data in a personal tool, and a job that nobody owns.
- Set the human check: one named owner and one clear data rule, written down.

## The problem

Small teams and nonprofits (organisations that work for a cause, not for profit) have the same jobs as big companies, with fewer people. Newsletters, grant reports, thank-you letters, rotas. There is no IT team and no legal team. Often there is no budget at all.

So AI use starts in a quiet way. One person tries a free app at home. A volunteer likes it and tells the others. Soon five people use five different accounts, each with their own habits. Nobody chose this, so nobody is in charge of it.

That is how private data ends up in the wrong place, and how a useful tool turns into a mess. This lesson shows a better start. It uses the Claude Help Center, the ChatGPT docs and Google's Responsible Generative AI Toolkit, read in October 2026.

## The idea

Like every chapter in this realm, the idea has three parts: what AI does well, the main risk, and the human check.

**1. What AI does well: one repeated job, done with less effort.**

A small team gets the most from AI on a job it does again and again, like a monthly newsletter or a weekly volunteer update. The [ChatGPT docs on usage insights](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/usage-insights) say to pick "workflows to assess with the people responsible for them". They also say how to judge the result:

> "To assess ROI, compare the value of the improvement with the costs of AI, setup, training, and ongoing support. Include time spent reviewing and correcting the work. Time saved isn't automatically a cash saving; check how the team uses that capacity."

(ROI, return on investment: what you get back for what you put in. Capacity: the time people now have free.)

Our advice for the first workflow: choose **one** job that comes back often, where a person reads every result before it is used, and where the tool needs no private details about anyone. A newsletter from a coordinator's own notes fits. A letter that decides who gets help does not.

Money matters too. If the team pays for a plan, set a limit. The [Claude Team plan page](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9266767-what-is-the-team-plan) lists "Spend controls: Set spending caps at organization and individual user levels." It also says "Team plans require a minimum of two members."

**2. The main risk: private data in a personal tool, and a job that nobody owns.**

**Private data.** A personal account follows personal terms. The Claude Help Center has an [article for its consumer plans](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8325621-i-would-like-to-input-sensitive-data-into-my-chats-with-claude-who-can-view-my-conversations) (it says it is about "Claude Free, Pro, Max"). It explains the protections "When you allow us to use your chats or coding sessions to help improve Claude". It also recommends "being thoughtful about sharing highly sensitive personal details such as":

- "Financial information (SSN, credit card numbers, bank account details)"
- "Health records or medical information"
- "Passwords or private login credentials"
- "Confidential business or personal documents"

Work plans are set up differently. The [ChatGPT docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/chatgpt-work-cloud-security) say: "Business, Enterprise, and Edu workspace data is encrypted in transit and at rest and is not used to train OpenAI models by default." On a Claude work plan, the [Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9265372-who-owns-and-manages-the-data-of-my-team) says "Your organization's designated Primary Owner manages your Work account and all associated data."

Our reading: a personal account is a private choice. The data of the people you serve is not yours to share. Before anyone puts it into a tool, the organisation must decide.

**Nobody owns it.** When "everyone" is in charge, no one checks the results, no one updates the prompt, and no one notices when it goes wrong. The ChatGPT docs say that when you record how a workflow went, you "Note the use case, owner, and reporting period." Google's [Responsible Generative AI Toolkit](https://ai.google.dev/responsible/docs/design) asks teams to "Engage yourself and your team in regular critical reviews of your approach".

**3. The human check: one named owner and one data rule.**

**One named owner.** One person, by name, owns the AI workflow. Our list of what the owner does:

- chooses the tool and the account the team uses
- keeps the prompt and the data rule up to date
- checks a sample of results each month
- is the person others ask when they are unsure

**One data rule.** A short rule that everyone can follow without asking. Google's toolkit writes this about policies for AI products: "Defining clear policies with a highly granular level of detail, including exceptions to the policy with examples, is fundamental to building a responsible product." (Granular: detailed, in small parts.) The toolkit writes it for people who build AI apps, but the lesson fits a small team too: a rule like "be careful with data" helps nobody. Name the data, give an example, and name the exceptions.

A rule in that shape (ours, for a made-up charity):

```text
DATA RULE (owner: Dana)
Never put into any AI tool: a client's name,
address, phone, health or money details.
Example: "Mrs K. at 4 Elm Road needs insulin"
  → write "a client needs a cold place for medicine".
Exception: none without Dana's written OK.
```

A small comparison: the owner is like the person who holds the keys to the shared building. Everyone can use the rooms, but one person knows who has a key, and locks up at night.

The Tool Forge chapter on scoring a use case and the Guild Hall chapter on seats and spend go deeper.

## Worked example

Ama coordinates a community kitchen with 9 staff and about 50 volunteers. Every Friday, Ama writes a newsletter for the volunteers: next week's menu, the shift gaps, a thank-you. It takes about 3 hours. The team agreed to try AI on this one job for a month.

### Step 1: the setup

Ama uses the AI tool the kitchen already pays for. Ama writes a short prompt and saves it in a project:

```text
You draft our Friday volunteer newsletter.
Inputs: my notes for this week (pasted below).
- Friendly and short, under 300 words.
- Sections: Menu · Shifts we still need · Thank you.
- Use only my notes. If something is missing,
  write [ASK AMA].
```

### Step 2: measure the first month

At the start, Ama wrote down the baseline (the "before" number): 3 hours a week. Each week Ama noted the time for the draft and the time for checking and fixing.

```text
Week   Draft   Check + fix   Total
1      15 min  95 min        1 h 50
2      15 min  70 min        1 h 25
3      10 min  50 min        1 h 00
4      10 min  45 min        0 h 55
```

### Step 3: the hard case, the "saved" hours

**This is the hard case.** Week 4 looks great: about 2 hours saved. But week 1 saved only about 1 hour, because the check took longer than the draft. Ama counts the whole month, not the best week: almost 7 hours saved in four weeks.

Then the real question: where did those hours go? The kitchen pays nothing extra, because the account was already paid for. Nobody's pay changed, so no money was saved. Ama used the time to call three new volunteers. That is the real value, and Ama writes it down in those words, not as cash.

### Step 4: record it

```text
Use case:   Friday volunteer newsletter
Owner:      Ama
Period:     4 weeks in October
Before:     3 h a week
After:      1 h 50 → 0 h 55 a week (incl. checks)
Value:      ~7 h in the month, used for volunteer calls
Limits:     typos in names of dishes in week 1
Review on:  first Friday of December
```

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Honest numbers.** The check time counts, so the result is real.
- **Value in plain words.** The team knows what the hours became.
- **A date to look again.** The team will decide in December to keep it, change it, or stop.

## Common mistakes

1. **Judging the result against a guess.** Fix: write down the "before" number first. The ChatGPT docs say the record should "Include the baseline, results, credits, and limitations".
2. **Paying for a plan with no spending limit.** Fix: set a cap. The Claude Team plan lists spending caps for the organisation and for each user.
3. **Writing down the result once and never looking again.** Fix: the ChatGPT docs say to record changes "and a date to review them". Put the date in the calendar.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A 5-person theatre group writes thank-you letters to its donors. Today it takes 4 hours a month. With AI, drafting takes 30 minutes, and checking and fixing takes 90 minutes. A new paid plan would cost 25 a month. Answer two questions: how much time is saved each month, and is the plan worth it?

### Answer

- **Time saved:** 4 hours minus 2 hours (30 + 90 minutes) = **2 hours a month**.
- **Worth it?** You can't tell from the hours alone. Time saved isn't cash. Ask what the 2 hours will be used for. If they go to meeting donors, it may be worth far more than 25. If nobody uses them, the plan only adds a cost.

Write the answer down with a review date, and check again after three months.

## Recap

- A small team should start with one repeated job, where a person reads every result and no private details are needed.
- The two big risks are private data in personal accounts and a workflow that nobody owns.
- Name one owner, write one clear data rule with an example, and judge the result on honest numbers, including the time spent checking.
