## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Say where a pilot fits: after automated evals, before a full rollout.
- Design a fair A/B pilot: random groups, the same weeks, one change, and a size and length fixed in advance.
- Read a pilot result honestly: the size of the gap, luck, guard numbers and the "why".

## The problem

An energy supplier has 80 call-centre agents. A vendor offers an AI "live assist" tool. During a call, it listens and suggests answers to the agent on screen.

The head of customer service ran a pilot. The best team (10 agents) got the tool in January. Their results were compared with the whole call centre's results from last October. In the same month, the pilot team also started a new bonus for short calls. The pilot was stopped after nine days, "because the numbers already look great".

Calls were 25% shorter. The head wants the tool for all 80 agents. The call centre manager asks: "Was it the tool, the best team, the bonus, or January?"

This lesson shows how to run a pilot that can answer that question. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Evals first, then a pilot.** Evals test the AI on a fixed set of tasks. They don't show whether real people get better results at work. For that you need a **pilot**: a small, planned trial in real work. The fairest pilot compares two groups at the same time: one uses the AI, one works as before. That is an **A/B test**.

The Anthropic post [Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) lists several ways to see how an AI system performs: automated evals, production monitoring, A/B testing, user feedback, transcript review and human studies. It says "no single evaluation layer catches every issue", like the "Swiss Cheese Model from safety engineering": what slips through one layer is caught by another. It also says when each one fits:

- **Automated evals** help most before launch and in CI/CD (the automatic checks that run on every change). They run on each change and model upgrade as the "first line of defense".
- **Production monitoring** "kicks in post-launch". It catches drift (real input changing over time) and failures nobody expected.
- **A/B testing** is for checking big changes, and only once enough real users or cases flow through.
- **User feedback and transcript review** are "ongoing practices to fill the gaps".

So don't use real customers to find problems a cheap test would catch.

**What an A/B test is good for.** The post's table lists its strengths and limits:

- It "Measures actual user outcomes (retention, task completion)".
- It "Controls for confounds". A **confound** is another reason why two groups differ, like a better team or a busy season.
- It is "Slow; days or weeks to reach significance and requires sufficient traffic". **Significance** means the gap is too big to be luck.
- It "Only tests changes you deploy".
- It gives less signal on the "why" unless you also review the transcripts (the records of real conversations).

The [Claude docs on success criteria](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests) list the same method: "A/B testing: Compare performance against a baseline model or earlier version." The [OpenAI accuracy guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) describes a pilot that measured human and AI work side by side: customer satisfaction, decision accuracy on reviewed cases, and time to resolution. Where a wrong decision was very costly, a person stayed in charge.

**Six rules for a fair pilot.** Neither vendor page spells these out. They are standard practice, and they are ours:

1. **Pick the question and the numbers first.** Write down the main metric, the guard numbers, and what result means "go", "fix" or "stop", before the start.
2. **Assign by chance.** Don't let people volunteer, and don't let managers choose. Keen volunteers and hand-picked staff were better already. Draw names at random. Splitting by region or office is also risky, because places differ.
3. **Run both groups at the same time.** Seasons, launches and holidays change results. Comparing January with October measures the season, not only the tool. So does comparing a group with its own past.
4. **Change one thing.** If the tool group also gets a new bonus or script, you cannot tell which change caused the result. A random draw does not fix this: it makes the groups alike, but it can't tell apart two changes that hit the same group.
5. **Fix the size and length in advance.** A small group over a few days can show a big gap by luck. Don't stop on the first good week, and don't keep going until you like the result.
6. **Watch the guard numbers.** A main metric can go up while another number gets worse.

Our example: to test a fertiliser, you don't use only the sunniest field. You pick fields by coin toss, plant on one day, and change nothing else.

**Keep the pilot connected to your evals.** The [OpenAI evaluation guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices) says evaluation is continuous:

- "run evals on every change, monitor your app to identify new cases of nondeterminism, and grow the eval set over time."
- "Log everything", so you can "mine your logs for good eval cases".

Every real failure in the pilot should become a new test case.

**Read the result with four questions** (ours):

1. **How big is the gap?** Give numbers for both groups.
2. **Could luck explain it?** With few people or few days, maybe. Say so.
3. **Did a guard number get worse?** Then the gain may not be worth it.
4. **Why did it happen?** Read a sample of transcripts.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak pilot

```text
Group with tool:  best team, 10 agents, January
Comparison:       all agents, last October
Also changed:     new bonus for short calls (pilot team only)
Stop rule:        "when the numbers look good" (stopped day 9)
Main number:      call length
```

What goes wrong:

- The best team was better already, and January differs from October.
- The bonus alone could shorten calls.
- Nine days and 10 agents is thin, and the stop rule invites luck.
- Shorter calls are not the goal. A rushed call can mean a call back.

### Step 2: the fair plan

```text
Question:      Does live assist help agents solve the problem on the first call?
Groups:        80 agents, drawn at random: 40 with the tool, 40 without
Period:        the same 8 weeks, March to April, for both groups
One change:    the tool. No new bonus or script for either group.
Main metric:   problems solved on the first call (customer doesn't call
               back within 7 days)
Guard numbers: complaints, call length, wrong advice found in a weekly
               sample of 20 recorded calls per group
Go / fix / stop, written before the start:
  go    first-call solve up 5 points or more, no guard number worse
  fix   up, but a guard number worse
  stop  no gain after 8 weeks
Size and end: 40 + 40 agents, 8 weeks, no early stop
```

### Step 3: the hard case

**This is the hard case.** In week 4, IT wants to launch a new help-article search for all 80 agents. Must the pilot restart?

Our reasoning: the new search reaches **both** groups on the same day. So the gap between the groups can still be read fairly. If it went to the tool group only, it would be a second change, and the pilot could no longer show the tool's own effect. The team notes the date in the report and carries on.

### Step 4: reading the result

```text
                         With tool   Without
First-call solve         78%         72%
Complaints per 1,000     2.9         3.0
Wrong advice in sample   6 of 160    2 of 160
```

The main metric rose 6 points, which clears the 5-point part of the "go" bar. But wrong advice in the call sample went from 2 to 6, so a guard number got worse. Both numbers are small, so luck is possible. The team reads the 6 calls: the tool suggested an old tariff that ended last year. So the result is "fix": update the tool's tariff list, add the 6 calls to the eval set, and re-test for 4 weeks.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Random groups, same weeks, one change.** The gap points to the tool, not the team or the season.
- **Rules set in advance.** Nobody can stop on a lucky day or move the goalposts.
- **Guard numbers and transcripts.** They showed the result is a "fix", not a "go", and why.

## Common mistakes

1. **Volunteers or hand-picked groups.** Fix: draw people at random from everyone eligible.
2. **Two changes at once, or two different periods.** Fix: change only the tool, and keep both groups in the same weeks.
3. **Stopping when the numbers look good.** Fix: set the size, length and main metric before the start, and stick to them.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

An online furniture shop wants to test AI-written product pages. Here is the plan:

```text
Week of a big sale: show AI pages to all visitors
Normal week before: old pages
Main number: orders per 1,000 visitors
Stop rule: after day 1 if orders are up
```

Find three problems and fix them. Then name one guard number.

### Answer

1. **Different weeks, one with a sale.** The sale alone lifts orders. Fix: in the same weeks, show half of visitors the AI pages and half the old pages, chosen at random.
2. **A one-day stop rule.** One day can be luck. Fix: set the length and the number of visitors in advance, for example 4 weeks.
3. **No plan for the "why".** Fix: read a sample of AI pages and customer questions each week, and add failures to the eval set.

**Guard number:** returns, for example "item not as described". Pages that oversell can lift orders and returns together.

## Recap

- Run automated evals first. Use an A/B pilot to measure real outcomes, knowing it is slow and needs enough traffic.
- Draw groups at random, run them over the same weeks, and change one thing only.
- Fix the metric, guard numbers, size and length before the start. Read transcripts to learn why, and turn failures into tests.
