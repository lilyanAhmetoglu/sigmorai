## Why it matters

A **KPI** (key performance indicator) is a number that tells leaders if something is working. AI tools produce many numbers: chats, tokens, words written. Most of them show activity, not value. This chapter helps you pick the few numbers that answer "Is this worth it?"

## Three layers of numbers

This split is a tool for this course. Track one or two numbers from each layer:

1. **Quality:** is the output right? (from your evals, chapter 2)
2. **Operations:** speed, errors, cost per task.
3. **Business outcome:** what changed for customers or the company?

Anthropic's success-criteria guide lists metrics for all three. Its examples include generic quality metrics such as accuracy, operational metrics such as response time and uptime, and user feedback such as task completion rates.

## Activity is not value

Some numbers are easy to count but say little. Claude Code's analytics page is a useful example because it explains its own metrics carefully.

- **Lines of code accepted** counts lines that users accepted. The page says it excludes rejected suggestions and does not track later deletions. So a line that was accepted and then deleted still counts.
- **Suggestion accept rate** is the share of times users accept the tool's edits.

These are activity numbers. The same page points to outcome numbers for return on investment (ROI): track pull requests (finished pieces of work) per user over time, and compare work shipped with and without the tool. It says to use them alongside engineering KPIs the team already tracks. It also calls its contribution metrics "deliberately conservative": they count only work where the tool's part is clear.

Example: a sales team uses an AI email writer. "Emails drafted" is activity. "Replies per email sent" and "meetings booked per rep" are outcomes.

## Compare with a baseline

A number alone means little. You need something to compare it with. OpenAI's accuracy guide describes a customer service pilot. The team measured the process for humans and for AI side by side:

- the customer satisfaction (CSAT) score for human vs AI conversations,
- decision accuracy on cases reviewed afterwards, human vs AI,
- time to resolution, human vs AI.

Anthropic's guide also lists A/B testing against a baseline model or an earlier version as a way to measure.

Example: an IT service desk says "the AI resolved 3,000 tickets". The better KPI is "resolution rate and time to resolution, AI vs human, on the same ticket types".

## Count cost per finished task

Price lists are written per token. Anthropic's cost guide says you pay for completed tasks, so compare models on **cost per completed task**, not cost per token. A stronger model can do less work per task: fewer turns, less searching and less backtracking.

The guide also says to look at the hardest tasks, not only the typical one. A failed task still bills its tokens, then the retry, then whatever the failure costs later.

Anthropic's post on evals lists numbers you can track on a fixed set of test tasks: latency, token usage, cost per task and error rates.

Example: model X costs less per token than model Y. On 500 real tickets, X needs two tries on many of them. Cost per resolved ticket can then be higher for X.

## Be careful with feedback buttons

Thumbs-up rates are popular. Anthropic's post lists the limits of user feedback: it is sparse and self-selected, it skews toward severe issues, and users rarely explain why something failed. The post also lists strengths: it shows problems you didn't expect, with real examples.

OpenAI's guide uses positive ratings as one metric among several (its Q&A example sets "70+% positively rated answers" next to two other metrics). It also says to combine metrics with human judgment.

Example: if only 4% of users press a button, the rating describes those 4%, not everyone.

## Watch the guard numbers too

A KPI can rise while something important gets worse. OpenAI's guide shows this in the customer service pilot. The AI saved money even though it passed more cases to humans than wanted. Where a mistake was very costly, such as a fraud case, the team kept a human in charge.

So track a few **guard numbers** next to the main KPI. Examples: share of serious errors, complaints, cases sent back to a person.

## A KPI card

This card is a tool for this course. One card per KPI:

- **Name:** time to resolution
- **Layer:** operations
- **Baseline:** human team, same ticket types, last quarter
- **Target:** 30% faster with no rise in reopened tickets
- **Source:** ticket system, not the AI vendor's dashboard
- **Owner:** service desk manager

## Key points

- Pick a few numbers from quality, operations and business outcome.
- Activity numbers (lines accepted, chats) are not value on their own.
- Always compare with a baseline: human, old version or no tool.
- Use cost per completed task, not cost per token.
- Treat feedback buttons as one signal; add guard numbers for serious errors.
