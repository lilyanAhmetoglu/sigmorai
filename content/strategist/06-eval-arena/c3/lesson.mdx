## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Tell activity numbers (how much the tool is used) from outcome numbers (what changed for the business).
- Compare AI results with a baseline, and add guard numbers that catch hidden costs.
- Compare two models on cost per completed task, and read feedback buttons with care.

## The problem

A wholesale company gets 30,000 customer payments a month. A finance team matches each payment to the right invoice. Six months ago it added an AI tool that suggests the match.

The vendor's dashboard shows big numbers:

```text
Payments read by the AI          180,000
Match suggestions made           176,500
Suggestion accept rate           93%
Staff rating                     4.7 / 5  (11 of 40 staff rated it)
```

The CFO asks a simple question: "Is it worth the money?" None of these numbers answers it. They show that the tool is busy. They don't show if payments are matched faster or more correctly than before.

This lesson helps you pick the few numbers that answer "Is this worth it?". The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

A **KPI** (key performance indicator) is a number that tells leaders if something is working. AI tools produce many numbers. Most of them show activity, not value.

**Three layers of numbers.** This split is ours, a tool for this course. Track one or two numbers from each:

```text
quality     is the output right?            (from your evals)
operations  speed, errors, cost per task
outcome     what changed for customers or the company?
```

The [Claude docs on success criteria](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests) give example metrics for all three:

- generic ones like "Accuracy"
- operational ones like "Response time (ms), uptime (%)"
- user feedback through "Implicit measures like task completion rates."

**Activity is not value.** Some numbers are easy to count but say little. The [Claude Code analytics page](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/analytics) is a useful example, because it explains its own numbers carefully:

- **Lines of code accepted** counts lines that users accepted. The page says it "excludes rejected suggestions and does not track subsequent deletions". So a line that was accepted and then deleted still counts.
- **Suggestion accept rate** is how often users accept the tool's edits.

These show use. For return on investment (ROI), the same page points to outcomes (a PR, or pull request, is a finished piece of work):

- "Track changes in PRs per user over time".
- "Compare PRs and lines of code shipped with vs. without Claude Code".

It says to use these next to the other KPIs the team already tracks. It also says its own contribution numbers are "deliberately conservative". It includes a line or PR only when it has "high confidence" that the tool took part.

Our example: a phone call log shows how many calls you made. It doesn't show how many sales you closed.

**Compare with a baseline.** A number alone means little. You need something to compare it with: people doing the same work, an older version, or no tool. The [OpenAI accuracy guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) describes a customer service pilot that measured the process for people and for AI side by side:

- "The CSAT score for purely human interactions vs. AI ones" (CSAT is customer satisfaction)
- "The decision accuracy for retrospectively reviewed cases for human vs. AI"
- "The time to resolution for human vs. AI"

The Claude docs also list "A/B testing: Compare performance against a baseline model or earlier version."

**Add guard numbers.** A main KPI can rise while something important gets worse. In the OpenAI pilot, the AI saved money even though it passed more cases to people than wanted. But "where the cost of failure was very high, such as a fraud case being incorrectly resolved", a person stayed in charge. So next to each main KPI, track a few **guard numbers** (our term): serious errors, complaints, work sent back or redone.

**Count cost per finished task, not per token.** A **token** is a small piece of text; AI price lists charge per token. The [Claude docs on cost](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/optimizing-for-cost-and-intelligence) say: "You pay for completed tasks, though, so compare models on cost per completed task." A stronger model can need "fewer turns, less searching, less re-reading of its own context, and less backtracking".

Two more points from the same page:

- Look at the hard tasks: "compare models on the hardest tenth of your tasks, not the typical one."
- Failures cost more than they seem: "a failed task still bills its tokens, then the retry, then whatever the failure costs downstream."

It adds: "The ranking flips by workload, and no price list tells you which way." So test on your own work, and test again when prices change. The Anthropic post [Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) lists numbers you can track on a fixed set of test tasks: "latency, token usage, cost per task, and error rates".

**Read feedback buttons with care.** The Anthropic post lists the limits of user feedback:

- "Sparse and self-selected": few people vote, and they choose to.
- "Skews toward severe issues".
- "Users rarely explain why something failed".

It also lists a strength: it comes "with real examples from actual human users", and it brings up problems you did not expect.

The [OpenAI evaluation guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices) treats ratings as one number next to others:

- Its Q&A example sets "70+% positively rated answers" next to two other targets.
- It also says: "Combine metrics with human judgment".

Our advice: don't set a target for the rating itself. People then push for votes, not for better work.

## Worked example

The payment tool from the problem.

### Step 1: the weak one-pager

```text
AI matching: month 6
- 180,000 payments read
- 93% of suggestions accepted
- Staff rating 4.7 / 5
Conclusion: success, renew the contract
```

What goes wrong:

- Payments read and suggestions made are activity. They grow with volume, not value.
- The accept rate counts clicks. It doesn't show if the match was right.
- The rating comes from 11 of 40 staff, and has no baseline.

### Step 2: pick numbers from each layer, with a baseline

The team compares AI-assisted payments with payments matched by hand last year, for the same customer types:

```text
                                     AI-assisted   By hand (last year)
Quality  right first time            96%           97%
Ops      minutes per 100 payments    35            140
Ops      cost per right match        $0.11         $0.62
Outcome  days to close the month     4             7
Guard    matches reversed at         2.8%          0.9%
         month-end
```

Cost per right match uses the full cost of each method (tool fees plus staff time), divided by matches that were right.

### Step 3: the hard case

**This is the hard case.** The 93% accept rate looked like quality. But the guard number shows reversed matches tripled, from 0.9% to 2.8%. The team reads 30 reversed cases. Staff were accepting suggestions on payments with no invoice number, often without checking. Like "lines accepted", the accept rate counted the click and ignored the later fix.

So the one-pager shows the speed gain and the reversals side by side. The team adds a rule: payments with no invoice number need a second look. It tracks reversals every month.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **Outcomes, not activity.** Month-end close and cost per right match answer the CFO's question.
- **A baseline.** Every number has a "by hand" number next to it.
- **A guard number.** The reversal rate stops the speed number from telling half the story.
- **Feedback in its place.** The rating can go in a footnote, with "11 of 40 staff".

## Common mistakes

1. **Reporting activity as value.** Chats, messages and accepted suggestions show use, not results. Fix: report outcomes, such as time, cost per finished task or errors.
2. **Comparing with nothing.** "The AI did 5,000 tasks" means little alone. Fix: put people, an old version or "no tool" next to it, on the same type of work.
3. **Choosing a model by token price.** A cheap model that fails and retries can cost more per finished task. Fix: test candidates on your own tasks, including the hardest ones, and divide total spend by tasks completed.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A bank uses an AI tool to draft replies to customer emails. The support head shares this:

```text
Emails drafted by the tool        22,000
Drafts sent without edits         64%
Agent rating                      4.7 / 5  (5 of 30 agents rated it)
Minutes per reply                 with tool: 4    by hand, last year: 9
Replies that needed a correction  6.5%            by hand, last year: 2.1%
```

1. Which number is activity only?
2. What is the problem with "64% sent without edits"?
3. Which number is the guard number, and what does it say?

### Answer

1. **The number of drafts written.** It shows use, not results.
2. **It counts a click, not a right answer.** An agent may send a draft without checking it. Like "lines accepted", the number ignores what happens later. Read the correction rate next to it.
3. **Replies that needed a correction.** It went from 2.1% to 6.5%, about three times higher. The tool is faster (4 minutes vs 9), but the replies are less right than by hand.

## Recap

- Pick a few numbers from quality, operations and outcome. Activity numbers show use, not value.
- Compare with a baseline on the same work, and add guard numbers for serious errors and redone work.
- Compare models on cost per finished task, measured on your own work. Treat feedback buttons as one signal among several.
