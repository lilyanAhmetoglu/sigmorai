## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Build an honest ROI model: give each success and each mistake a value, and count every cost.
- Work out break-even accuracy, net benefit per month and payback time.
- Find the input that moves the result most, and compare cheaper options on cost per finished task.

## The problem

A car rental company gets 8,000 car returns a month. At each return, a staff member walks around the car, looks for damage and writes a report. A vendor sells an AI tool that checks photos of the car and drafts the damage report.

The vendor's slide says: "Saves 5 minutes per return. That is $30,000 a month." The licence and usage cost $6,500 a month. The sales rep says the tool pays for itself in a week.

The finance director has doubts. In the pilot, staff still checked each AI report. Sometimes the tool marked a scratch that wasn't there, and a customer got a wrong bill. At small branches, staff often wait between returns anyway. "Five minutes saved there doesn't save us a cent," says one manager.

This lesson shows how to build an ROI model that survives these questions. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**ROI** (return on investment) answers "What do we get for the money?". The formula is plain arithmetic, and it is ours, not a vendor's:

```text
ROI = (benefit − total cost) ÷ total cost
Payback in months = one-off cost ÷ net benefit per month
```

The hard part is not the formula. It is honest numbers for "benefit" and "total cost".

**Give each result a value.** The [OpenAI accuracy guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) gives a method: "First we identify the primary success and failure cases, and assign an estimated cost to them." Its customer service example uses these values:

- An AI success on a case a person used to solve "may save **$20**".
- A customer sent to a person when they shouldn't be "might cost **$40**".
- In the worst case, a customer leaves, "costing us **$1000**". The guide says "We assume this happens in 5% of cases"; in its table, that is 5% of the failures.

Its table for 1,000 cases:

```text
Event                    Value    Cases     Total
AI success               +20      815       $16,300
AI failure (escalation)  -40      175.75    $7,030
AI failure (churn)       -1000    9.25      $9,250
Result                                      +20
Break-even accuracy                         81.5%
```

**Break-even accuracy** is the accuracy where the tool neither gains nor loses money. Below it, the AI costs more than it saves. You can check the number yourself (our arithmetic): an average failure costs 0.95 × $40 + 0.05 × $1,000 = $88. Break-even is where 20 × accuracy = 88 × (1 − accuracy). That gives about 81.5%.

So what matters is what each kind of wrong costs. The guide's pilots led to two decisions:

1. The AI sent people to staff more than wanted, but still saved a lot. So "an accuracy of even 85% could be ok, if those 15% were primarily early escalations."
2. "Where the cost of failure was very high, such as a fraud case being incorrectly resolved", a person would drive and the AI would assist.

**Count the benefit carefully.** These rules are ours, not from a vendor page:

- **Time saved is not cash saved.** If staff save 30 minutes a day but nothing else changes, no money leaves the budget. Count saved time as money only when it goes to paid work (for example, work that now needs overtime or an outside firm) or avoids a hire.
- **Use the loaded hourly cost.** That is salary plus the other costs of employing someone. Divide by 60 for a cost per minute.
- **Count only the volume that really costs you.** If only part of the work would ever have reached paid staff, count only that part.
- **Label every input.** Write "measured" (from your pilot), "estimated" or "vendor claim" next to each number. Replace vendor claims with pilot numbers when you can.

**Count every cost.** Besides usage fees: set-up, review time (people who check or fix the AI's work), mistakes that slip through, training, and test upkeep.

**Compare models on cost per finished task.** The [Claude docs on cost](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/optimizing-for-cost-and-intelligence) say: "Price lists are written per token". But "You pay for completed tasks, though, so compare models on cost per completed task." A model with a higher price per token can finish with fewer turns and less backtracking. The docs add: "The ranking flips by workload, and no price list tells you which way."

The [OpenAI model selection page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-selection) says the same in its own words: experiment "using the same inputs" and keep "the lightest setting" that still meets your quality bar. It lists questions to ask, such as "How often does your workflow run?" and "How important is the quality of the result?". A cheaper option is fine if it still meets your bar. If its accuracy falls below break-even, it loses money, whatever its price.

**Cheap ways to cut the bill.** The [OpenAI cost guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/cost-optimization) lists "Reduce requests", "Minimize tokens" and "Select a smaller model", plus its Batch API and flex processing. The Claude docs say their Batch API "takes 50% off every token of a request" in exchange for "results arriving any time within 24 hours". Use it for jobs no one waits for, like overnight reports.

**Show a range, not one number.** Our method: build a cautious, a base and a good case. Then change one input at a time and see which one moves the result most. That input is where to look first.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the vendor's model

```text
8,000 returns × 5 minutes × $0.75 per minute = $30,000 a month
Minus licence and usage $6,500             = $23,500 a month net
```

What goes wrong:

- The 5 minutes is a vendor claim, not a pilot number.
- It counts every saved minute as cash, even where staff were waiting anyway.
- It has no line for mistakes or set-up.

### Step 2: the honest inputs

```text
Staff cost (loaded): $45 per hour = $0.75 per minute

Time saved per return, after staff check the AI report:
  3 minutes (measured in the pilot)
Large branches, 4,000 returns: saved time goes to cleaning cars,
  which now costs an outside firm. This counts.
Small branches, 4,000 returns: staff wait between returns. No cash.

Extra damage found that staff used to miss: 40 a month × $150
  (measured, small sample)
False damage bills that reach customers: 50 a month × $100 refund
  and goodwill (measured)
Licence and usage: $6,500 a month
One-off set-up: $33,000 (estimated)
```

### Step 3: the base case

```text
Time saved, large branches   4,000 × 3 × $0.75     +$9,000
Extra damage charged         40 × $150             +$6,000
False damage bills           50 × $100             -$5,000
Licence and usage                                  -$6,500
Net benefit per month                              +$3,500

ROI per month   (15,000 − 11,500) ÷ 11,500 ≈ 30%
Payback         33,000 ÷ 3,500 ≈ 9.4 months
```

### Step 4: the hard case

**This is the hard case.** A branch manager objects: "Small branches save 3 minutes per return too. Why is that zero?" The time is real, but no money leaves the budget, and no paid work absorbs it. So it stays out of the money lines. The report lists it as a non-cash benefit. If a small branch later avoids a hire because of it, count it then.

Next, change one input at a time:

```text
Change                              Net per month
Base case                           $3,500
Minutes saved 3 -> 2                $500
Extra damage found halves (20)      $500
False bills up 50% (75)             $1,000
Licence 10% cheaper                 $4,150
```

Time saved and damage found move the result most. Both rest on small samples, so the team re-measures them first. A cheaper licence would change little.

The range: cautious −$5,000 a month (2 minutes, 20 damages, 75 false bills), base +$3,500, good +$8,500 (3.5 minutes, 50 damages, 30 false bills).

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Every line is labelled**, and mistakes have a price.
- **Only real cash counts** as benefit; the rest is listed, not hidden.
- **A range and a ranked list of inputs** show the risk and what to check first.

## Common mistakes

1. **Using the vendor's time-saved number as cash.** Fix: use pilot numbers, and count saved time only where it goes to paid work or avoids a hire.
2. **Leaving out mistakes and review.** Fix: give each kind of mistake a cost, add review time, and find the break-even accuracy.
3. **Picking the cheapest plan by price alone.** Fix: compare cost per finished task on the same inputs, and check accuracy stays above break-even.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A city council uses AI to send incoming letters to the right department. 10,000 letters a month.

```text
Right department: saves a clerk $3 per letter
Wrong department: costs $9 on average (letter returns, delay)
Pilot accuracy: 85% (measured)
Running cost: $4,000 a month. One-off set-up: $24,000.
A "plus" plan costs $1,000 more a month and reached 90% in the same test.
```

1. Find the break-even accuracy.
2. Find the monthly net benefit and the payback time.
3. Is the plus plan worth it?

### Answer

1. **75%.** 3 × accuracy = 9 × (1 − accuracy), so 12 × accuracy = 9.
2. **$8,000 a month; payback 3 months.** Per letter: 0.85 × $3 − 0.15 × $9 = $2.55 − $1.35 = $1.20. Times 10,000 is $12,000. Minus $4,000 is $8,000. Then $24,000 ÷ $8,000 = 3 months.
3. **Yes.** Per letter: 0.90 × $3 − 0.10 × $9 = $1.80, so $18,000. Minus $5,000 is $13,000, which beats $8,000. Each mistake costs three times what a success saves, so accuracy is worth paying for.

## Recap

- ROI = (benefit − total cost) ÷ total cost. Count every cost, including mistakes and review time, and label each input.
- Give each success and failure a value, then find break-even accuracy. Saved time is cash only when it goes to paid work.
- Compare plans on cost per finished task. Show a range, and fix the input that moves the result most.
