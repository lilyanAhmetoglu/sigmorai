## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Turn a vague goal like "it should work well" into success criteria with a number and a test.
- Set several criteria for one tool, with a stricter target for the mistakes that cost the most.
- Build a test set from your own work, with the hard and messy cases included.

## The problem

A property company manages 4,000 flats. Tenants send repair requests by email: a broken boiler, a leaking tap, a lost key. A vendor sells a tool that reads each email, picks the right trade (plumber, electrician, locksmith) and sets the urgency.

Five staff tried it for two weeks. The team lead said: "It seems to work. People like it." The director now wants to sign a three-year contract.

Nobody wrote down what "work" means or counted mistakes. Nobody asked what happens when the tool marks a gas smell as "routine". Finance asks: "What exactly are we buying, and how will we know if it fails?"

This lesson shows how to write success down before you buy, build or launch. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

An **eval** (evaluation) is a test for an AI system. The Anthropic post [Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) puts it simply: "give an AI an input, then apply grading logic to its output to measure success." You cannot grade anything until you decide what success is. That decision is a **success criterion**: one thing the tool must do, with a target.

**"It seems to work" is not a test.** The [OpenAI evaluation guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices) lists "vibe-based evals" as an anti-pattern. It means trusting a feeling ("it seems like it's working"), or writing no evals until after launch.

**Four qualities of a good criterion.** The [Claude docs on success criteria](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests) list them:

- **Specific.** Say exactly what you want. "Good performance" is not specific. Name the task and what "right" means.
- **Measurable.** Use numbers, or a clear scale that people apply the same way each time.
- **Achievable.** "Base your targets on industry benchmarks, prior experiments, AI research, or expert knowledge."
- **Relevant.** Fit the purpose and the users. "Strong citation accuracy might be critical for medical apps but less so for casual chatbots."

Even fuzzy goals like safety can get a number. The docs show a bad safety criterion, "Safe outputs", and a good one: "Less than 0.1% of outputs out of 10,000 trials flagged for toxicity by the content filter." The good one names a test size, a check and a limit.

**One number is rarely enough.** The same docs list criteria that may matter: task fidelity (how well it does the job), consistency, relevance and coherence, tone and style, privacy, use of context, latency (speed) and price. They say: "Most use cases need multidimensional evaluation along several success criteria." Speed and price are real criteria, not only buying questions.

Their sentiment example sets four targets at once. One is about errors: "90% of errors would cause inconvenience, not egregious error". A footnote says that in reality you would define both of those words, too.

**Not every mistake costs the same.** The [OpenAI accuracy guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) says "there is a difference between producing a bad copy that a human needs to fix vs. refunding a customer $1000 rather than $100." It tells you to start with "a rough picture of how much a failure by the LLM costs you, and how much a success saves or earns you".

Our example: you can live with a doorbell that misses one ring in fifty. You cannot live with a smoke alarm that does. Give costly mistakes their own, stricter target. Keep cheap mistakes in the test too, with a looser target, because many small errors still add up.

**Test on your own work, not only a benchmark.** The OpenAI guide says "evals" can mean three things. Industry benchmarks are "for comparing models in isolation". Standard scores are tools you can use. The third kind is tests you build for your own application, and the guide is about that kind. A vendor's benchmark score is about someone else's tasks.

The Claude docs say: "Design evals that mirror your real-world task distribution." In plain words: your test should hold the same mix of jobs as your real work. They also say not to forget edge cases (rare or hard inputs), such as:

- "Irrelevant or nonexistent input data"
- "Overly long input data or user input"
- **Ambiguous** tasks, where "even humans would find it hard to reach an assessment consensus"

**Who should write the criteria.** The Anthropic post says evals "force product teams to specify what success means". It warns that two engineers may handle edge cases in different ways after reading one spec, and a written test removes that doubt. It adds that the people nearest to the users and the product needs are the best ones to define success. It names "product managers, customer success managers, or salespeople". So business people should write the criteria, not leave them to the vendor or the engineers.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak criteria

```text
Repair triage tool: acceptance criteria v1
1. The tool should understand tenant emails well.
2. Urgent jobs are handled safely.
3. Staff are happy with it after the trial.
4. It should be fast and not too expensive.
```

What goes wrong:

- "Well" and "safely" have no number and no test. Nobody can pass or fail them.
- "Staff are happy" is a feeling, not a scale people apply the same way.
- "Fast" and "not too expensive" name the right topics, but give no limit.
- Privacy is missing, and the costly mistakes have no target of their own.

### Step 2: price the mistakes

The repairs manager estimates the cost of each mistake:

```text
Mistake                                  Rough cost
Wrong trade on a routine job             a second visit, about $90
Routine job marked urgent                an out-of-hours fee, about $150
Gas smell or flood marked routine        danger to tenants, possible legal case
```

The last row needs its own, much stricter target.

### Step 3: write criteria with numbers

```text
Test set: 400 past repair emails from last year, in the real mix
(about 60% plumbing, 25% electrical, 15% other), including:
  - 40 emails with no address or no flat number
  - 30 very long emails with photos and old threads pasted in
  - 30 emails where two repair managers disagreed on urgency

Criterion            Target                                   How we check
Danger jobs          100% of the 25 gas/flood/fire emails      code check on
                     marked urgent                            the label
Trade correct        at least 92% of the 400                  compare with the
                                                              job that was done
Urgency correct      at least 90% of routine and urgent jobs  manager's label
Missing details      asks for the address, never guesses one  reviewer reads all 40
Personal data        tenant phone numbers never sent to the   reviewer check
                     trade outside the job card
Speed                95% of labels within 60 seconds          tool log
Price                under $0.05 per email                    vendor bill ÷ emails
```

### Step 4: the hard case

**This is the hard case.** Look at the 30 emails where two managers disagreed on urgency. There is no single "right" label to compare with. If you grade them like the rest, the tool fails or passes by luck.

So these 30 get their own rule: the tool must flag each one for a person, or pick the more urgent of the two labels. A tool that marks one of them "routine" fails that row, even if one manager agreed.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Every row can pass or fail.** Each has a number, a test set and a way to check.
- **The costly mistake has its own target.** Danger jobs are not hidden inside an average.
- **The test looks like real work.** It uses the real mix of trades and the messy emails.
- **Written before the trial.** Nobody can move the goalposts after seeing a result.

## Common mistakes

1. **A goal with no number.** "Fast", "accurate" or "safe" alone cannot fail. Fix: add a number, a test set and how you will check it.
2. **One average for all errors.** A rare, costly mistake disappears inside a good overall score. Fix: give costly mistakes their own stricter target, and keep cheap ones with a looser target.
3. **Testing only clean, typical cases.** The tool looks great until real, messy input arrives. Fix: build the test from your own past work in its real mix, and add missing, very long and unclear inputs.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A dental clinic group wants an AI chat tool that books, moves and cancels appointments on its website. The manager wrote these criteria. Find three problems and fix them.

```text
1. Patients find the bot friendly.
2. The bot books appointments correctly.
3. We will measure results after three months live.
```

### Answer

1. **A feeling, not a measure.** Fix: "On 200 past chats, at least 90% of replies score 4 or 5 on our 1–5 tone guide, rated by two receptionists."
2. **No number, no test, no edge cases.** Fix: "On 300 past requests in the real mix, at least 97% of bookings have the right date, time and dentist." Add requests with no date, very long ones, and unclear ones. Add a stricter row for the costly mistake: cancelling a visit the patient wanted to keep.
3. **Testing only after launch.** Fix: run the test before the bot goes live. Add speed and price rows. Let the front-desk team write the criteria, because they know the patients best.

## Recap

- Write success criteria before you buy, build or launch. Each one should be exact, have a number, be realistic and fit the purpose.
- Most tools need several criteria, including speed and price. Put a rough cost on each kind of mistake, and set stricter targets for the costly ones.
- Test on your own work, in its real mix, with edge cases. A vendor's benchmark is not your test.
