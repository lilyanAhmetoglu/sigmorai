## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Write a one-page report that starts with the decision you ask for.
- Show results against the targets set before the pilot, on the real mix of work, with sample sizes and labels.
- Recommend a scope that fits the cost of a mistake, instead of all or nothing.

## The problem

A city transport company runs buses and trams. It publishes about 600 customer notices a month: timetable changes, service updates and safety notices. Each notice must go out in five languages. For three months it piloted an AI tool that translates them. Human translators checked a sample.

The project lead drafted the summary for the executive team:

> "AI translation: 97% accurate, 100% on safety phrases, saves $120,000 a year. Recommend full rollout."

The data behind it is less simple. The test used only Spanish and French. Polish and Arabic notices were tested on a small set, with weaker results. The $120,000 is the vendor's estimate. The executive team will read the page for three minutes, then decide: spend more, fix or stop.

This lesson shows how to report AI results honestly and clearly. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

A report can fail in two ways. It can hide problems to look good. Or it can bury the decision under forty numbers.

**Lead with the decision.** This structure is ours, a tool for this course:

```text
1. The ask             "Approve X" or "Stop the pilot"
2. Evidence, one line  the key results against their targets
3. Risks               what could go wrong, and its cost
4. Unknowns            what we haven't tested, and how we'll find out
5. Next step, date
Everything else goes in a short appendix.
```

Background ("the pilot ran for three months at...") is not an opening line. It goes later.

**Compare with the targets you set before.** Report against the targets written before the pilot. Don't pick new goals after you see results. The [Claude docs on success criteria](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests) say: "Most use cases need multidimensional evaluation along several success criteria." So show a few rows, each with a target, a result and "pass" or "miss". A mix of passes and misses is a normal result. Say so.

**Test the real mix, not the easy part.** The [OpenAI evaluation guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices) lists "Biased design" as an anti-pattern: "Creating eval datasets that don't faithfully reproduce production traffic patterns." If the test holds only easy cases, the headline number is too high.

To get the real figure, weight each group's result by its share of real work. This arithmetic is ours. Our example: a tool scores 99% on short emails and 75% on long ones. If 40% of real emails are long, the real figure is about 0.6 × 99% + 0.4 × 75% ≈ 89%.

**Put a sample size next to every number.** A result from 20 cases is a hint, not a fact. Write "80% (16 of 20)", not "80%". Say when a figure needs a bigger test. Don't drop a weak small result; that hides the problem. Don't present it as proof either.

**Read the examples before you trust the score.** The Anthropic post [Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) says: "we do not take eval scores at face value until someone digs into the details of the eval and reads some transcripts." If grading is unfair, tasks are unclear or good answers are marked wrong, it says the eval must change.

It also warns about **saturation**: "An eval at 100% tracks regressions but provides no signal for improvement." A perfect score can mean the test is too easy.

**Use more than one kind of evidence.** The post says "no single evaluation layer catches every issue". It names the "Swiss Cheese Model from safety engineering": what slips through one layer is caught by another. The OpenAI guide says: "Combine metrics with human judgment to ensure you're answering the right questions." In a report, show the score, a few real examples, and what staff and customers said.

**Be honest about money and limits.** Three habits (ours):

- **Label every number:** "measured", "estimated" or "vendor claim".
- **Show a range:** "$40,000 to $90,000 a year" is more honest than "$65,000".
- **Put every cost in.** If review time is missing from the slide, add it to total cost before you work out ROI.

The [Claude Code analytics page](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/analytics) is a good model. It calls its contribution numbers "deliberately conservative" and "an underestimate" of the tool's real impact: "Only lines and PRs where there is high confidence in Claude Code's involvement are counted." It suggests using them next to the engineering KPIs the team already tracks. A modest number you can defend beats a big one you can't.

**Recommend a scope that fits the risk.** You don't have to say yes or no to everything. The [OpenAI accuracy guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) shows a middle path. "Where the cost of failure was very high, such as a fraud case being incorrectly resolved, we decided the human would drive and the AI would function as an assistant." It used "the decision accuracy stat" to make that call. Where most failures were early hand-offs to a person, "an accuracy of even 85% could be ok".

Our example: a new driver may drive in town but not yet on the motorway. You widen the scope after a test.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak summary

```text
AI translation: 97% accurate, 100% on safety phrases,
saves $120,000 a year. Recommend full rollout.
```

What goes wrong:

- 97% comes from Spanish and French only.
- "100% on safety phrases" has no sample size and nobody checked what was tested.
- $120,000 is a vendor estimate, with no range and no review cost.
- "Full rollout" ignores that a wrong safety notice costs far more than a wrong timetable note.

### Step 2: the data, sorted

```text
Target agreed in advance: 95% correct on the real mix of languages

Main test, Spanish + French:  291 of 300 correct (97%)   measured
Polish, small test:            34 of 40 correct (85%)    measured
Arabic, small test:            21 of 30 correct (70%)    measured
Real mix last year: Spanish + French 70%, Polish 20%, Arabic 10%
Real-mix estimate: 0.7 × 97% + 0.2 × 85% + 0.1 × 70% ≈ 92%   (miss)

Translator fees saved: $120,000 a year   vendor claim
Pilot fees saved: $5,000 to $7,000 a month (average $6,000)
                                         measured, 3 months
Extra review of Polish and Arabic: $1,500 a month   measured
```

### Step 3: the hard case

**This is the hard case.** The "100% on safety phrases" row. The team reads the 50 test items. All are short fixed phrases like "Do not lean on the doors". Real safety notices are long: a closed station, a diversion, what to do in a fire. The 100% says the tool can translate short phrases. It says nothing about real safety notices. So the row becomes "50 of 50 on short phrases; full safety notices not tested yet".

### Step 4: the one-page report

```text
ASK: Approve AI translation for timetable and service notices in
Spanish and French. Keep safety notices, Polish and Arabic with
human translators. Re-test Polish and Arabic on 200 notices by June.

EVIDENCE: Spanish + French 97% (291 of 300), measured. Real-mix
estimate 92% against a 95% target: miss. Polish 85% (34 of 40),
Arabic 70% (21 of 30), both small tests.

MONEY: $6,000 a month saved, minus $1,500 review = $4,500 net,
measured over 3 months. Vendor estimate ($10,000 a month) not
yet confirmed. Range: $3,500 to $5,500 net a month.

RISKS: a wrong safety notice could put riders in danger.
UNKNOWNS: full safety notices; peak-season volume.
NEXT STEP: bigger language test, results by 30 June.
```

### Step 5: why it is better

- **The ask comes first**, and it is a scope, not all or nothing.
- **Results sit against the target set before**, on the real mix.
- **Every number has a sample size and a label.**
- **The risky part stays with people** until it passes a test.

## Common mistakes

1. **Reporting the easy test as the headline.** Fix: weight each group by its share of real work, and show the groups as separate rows.
2. **Dropping a weak small result, or calling it proof.** Fix: show it with its sample size, and say a bigger test is needed.
3. **All or nothing.** Fix: approve the low-risk part, keep the costly part with people, and set a date to re-test.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

An airline uses AI to draft replies to lost-baggage claims. The draft summary says: "The tool is 94% accurate and saves 3,100 staff hours. We recommend going ahead." The data:

```text
Target agreed in advance: 95% correct on all claims
Claims under $500:  480 of 500 drafts correct (96%)   measured
Claims over $2,000:  17 of 21 drafts correct (81%)   measured
3,100 hours saved: vendor's calculation
```

Write a better first line (the ask) and the evidence line.

### Answer

A strong version:

- **Ask:** "Approve AI drafts for claims under $500. Keep claims over $2,000 with staff, and re-test them on a bigger set by a set date."
- **Evidence:** "Small claims 96% (480 of 500, measured), above the 95% target. Large claims 81% (17 of 21, measured), below target and a small test. Hours saved: vendor claim, not yet measured."

Why: it leads with a scoped ask, shows sample sizes and labels, and keeps the costly claims with people.

## Recap

- Lead with the ask, then evidence, risks, unknowns and the next step.
- Report against targets set before the pilot, on the real mix of work, with sample sizes and labels. Read real examples before you trust a score.
- Match the scope to what a mistake costs: AI where errors are cheap, people in charge where they are costly.
