## Why it matters

You have success criteria, evals, KPIs, an ROI model and a pilot. Now you must tell leaders what it all means. A board or an executive team has a few minutes. It wants to know one thing: should we spend more, fix it or stop?

A report can fail in two ways. It can hide problems to look good. Or it can bury the decision under forty numbers. This chapter is about a short report that is honest and clear.

## Lead with the decision

Start the page with what you ask for and why. This structure is a tool for this course:

1. **The ask.** "Approve a rollout to the clean-document team" or "Stop the pilot."
2. **The evidence in one line.** The best number against its target.
3. **The risks.** What could go wrong, and the cost of it.
4. **What we don't know yet.** And how we will find out.
5. **The next step and its date.**

Everything else goes in a short appendix.

## Compare with the target you set

In chapter 1 you wrote targets before the pilot. Report against them. Do not choose new goals after you see the results.

Anthropic's success-criteria guide says most use cases need evaluation along several criteria. So the report shows a few rows, each with a target, a result and a pass or miss.

| Criterion      | Target   | Result | Status |
| -------------- | -------- | ------ | ------ |
| Accuracy       | 95%      | 91%    | Miss   |
| Time per task  | -30%     | -41%   | Pass   |
| Serious errors | Under 1% | 2%     | Miss   |
| Cost per task  | $1.20    | $0.95  | Pass   |

Example (made up): two passes and two misses is a mixed result. Say so. A mixed result is a normal outcome of a good pilot.

## Show the real mix, not the easy test

A strong number on an easy test set misleads. OpenAI's evaluation guide lists "biased design" as an anti-pattern: eval datasets that don't faithfully reproduce production traffic patterns. If your test set holds only clean examples but real work is half messy, the headline accuracy is too high.

Example: an AI reads forms. It scores 99% on typed forms and 60% on handwritten ones. If half of real forms are handwritten, the real figure is about 80%. Report both rows and the mix. This arithmetic is ours.

## Put sample sizes next to every number

A result from 20 cases is a hint, not a fact. Show how many cases stand behind each number: "70% (14 of 20 scans)". Say when a figure needs a bigger test.

Anthropic's post says they don't take eval scores at face value until someone digs into the details and reads some transcripts. If grading is unfair or tasks are ambiguous, the eval should be revised. Do the same before you put a score on a slide. Read the examples that failed, and make sure they fail for fair reasons.

The post also warns about **saturation**: an eval at 100% tracks regressions but gives no signal for improvement. A perfect score may mean the test is too easy.

## Use more than one kind of evidence

Anthropic's post says no single evaluation layer catches every issue. It compares this to the Swiss cheese model from safety engineering: a failure that slips through one layer is caught by another. The most effective teams combine automated evals, production monitoring and periodic human review.

OpenAI's evaluation guide says the same in a business way: don't rely on scores alone. Combine metrics with human judgment, and use human feedback to calibrate automated scoring.

In a report this means: show the score, a few real examples, and what staff and customers said.

## Be honest about uncertainty and limits

Three habits make a report trusted:

- **Label every number.** Measured in the pilot, estimated, or taken from a vendor? Use three words: "measured", "estimated", "vendor claim".
- **Show a range.** "Net benefit $2,000 to $9,000 a month" is more honest than "$5,000".
- **State what you did not test.** Other languages, peak season, new document types.

Claude Code's analytics page gives an example of honest reporting. It says its contribution metrics are deliberately conservative. They count only the lines and pull requests where the tool's part is clear, so they are an underestimate of the real impact. It also says to use these numbers to demonstrate ROI next to the engineering KPIs the team already tracks. A conservative number that you can defend is worth more than a big one you can't.

## Recommend a scope that fits the risk

You do not have to say yes to everything or no to everything. OpenAI's accuracy guide shows the middle path. Where the cost of failure was very high, such as a fraud case resolved wrongly, the team decided that a human would drive and the AI would assist. Where most failures were early hand-offs to a person, even 85% accuracy could be fine.

So a good recommendation often reads: "Go live for the cases where errors are cheap. Keep a person in charge where they are costly. Re-test the weak group by a set date."

## A one-page scorecard

This checklist is a tool for this course:

1. Is the ask in the first line?
2. Are results shown against targets set before the pilot?
3. Is the test mix like real work?
4. Does each number have a sample size and a label?
5. Is there a range for the money figure?
6. Are guard numbers and failures shown?
7. Does the scope match the cost of a mistake?
8. Did someone read real examples before the numbers went on the slide?

## Key points

- Lead with the decision, then the evidence, risks and next step.
- Report against targets written before the pilot, with pass or miss.
- Test on the real mix and show sample sizes; read examples before trusting a score.
- Label measured, estimated and vendor numbers; show ranges.
- Match the scope to the cost of a mistake: AI alone where it is cheap, a person in charge where it is not.
