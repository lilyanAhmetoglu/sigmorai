## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Sort a wrong answer into one of two groups: missing facts (a context problem) or wrong tone or format (a behaviour problem).
- Explain why a newer model with a later knowledge cutoff does not fix missing company facts.
- Plan the context for an assistant: only the current, useful pages, a rule that allows "I don't know", and spot checks.

## The problem

A car leasing company has 40,000 customers. Its customer team tests a chat assistant. The assistant uses a general model and a one-line role prompt. No company documents are connected.

The test goes badly:

- A customer asks about the early-return fee. The assistant gives a typical market figure. The company's own fee is different, and it changed in August.
- A customer asks what the damage code on their return report means. The assistant invents a meaning.
- The replies sound casual, with jokes. The company wants a calm, formal style.

The team leader asks: "Do we need a smarter model?" This lesson shows how to read these failures and what to fix first. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Context** is everything you send the model with a request: instructions, documents and data. A model learns from a large amount of public text before you use it. It never saw your fee table, your codes or last month's policy change. The [Anthropic Contextual Retrieval post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval) gives a plain example: customer support chatbots "need knowledge about the specific business they're being used for."

**Three gaps that context fills.** The [OpenAI accuracy guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy) says you work on context when:

1. the knowledge was not in the model's training set,
2. the knowledge is out of date, or
3. the task needs proprietary (private company) knowledge.

**Behaviour is a different problem.** The same guide puts other failures under the model's behaviour: the format is wrong, the tone or style is wrong, or the model does not follow the reasoning steps consistently. You fix behaviour with better instructions and examples. **Fine-tuning** (extra training on your own examples) is also on this side. It improves consistency. It is not the first tool for missing facts.

```text
wrong fact, missing fact  -> context problem   -> give the right document
wrong tone, wrong format  -> behaviour problem -> change the instructions
```

**Two kinds of memory.** The OpenAI guide uses an exam picture. Months of classes are **learned memory**: the model learns from examples. Bringing the textbook into the exam is **in-context memory**: you put the facts in the prompt, or at larger scale you use **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation: the system finds the right pages and adds them to the prompt). The guide calls the two "additive, not exclusive". Its method: evaluate, find out what went wrong, then take the step that fixes that problem.

**A newer model does not bring your facts.** Vendors publish a **knowledge cutoff** for each model: the date up to which its public knowledge is good. The [Claude models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/overview) defines it as "The date through which the model's knowledge is most extensive and reliable", and lists Jun 2026 for Claude Opus 5.5. The [OpenAI GPT-5.5 page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-5.5) lists "Dec 01, 2025 knowledge cutoff". A later date covers more public news. Your private fee table was never public, so no model learns it in training.

**More context is not always better.** The [Anthropic context engineering post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) describes **context rot**: "as the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases." (The **context window** is the most text one request can hold. **Tokens** are the small pieces of text a model reads.) The post calls context "a critical but finite resource". Its advice is to find "the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens". The OpenAI guide agrees: models "can struggle to maintain attention across very large prompts", so test long prompts "at different context sizes".

**Let the model say "I don't know".** A **hallucination** is an answer the model made up and states as fact. The [Claude docs on reducing hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations) list simple steps:

- Clearly allow the model to admit it is not sure.
- Tell it to use only the documents you provide, not its general knowledge.
- For long documents, ask it to pull out word-for-word quotes first and work from them.

The same page warns that these steps reduce hallucinations but "don't eliminate them entirely". It says: "Always validate critical information, especially for high-stakes decisions."

Our example: a general model is like a smart temp worker on day one. The worker writes well and knows the industry. But nobody has given the worker your price list yet. You don't fix that by hiring a smarter temp. You hand over the right binder.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the weak plan

The team leader writes this plan:

```text
Plan A
1. Switch to the newest, largest model.
2. Add to the prompt: "Be accurate. Double-check your facts."
3. Paste every policy and fee table since 2019 into each request,
   so the model always has enough information.
```

What goes wrong:

- The newest model still has never seen the August fee or the damage codes.
- "Double-check your facts" gives the model nothing to check against.
- Old fee tables sit next to the new one. The model may quote either, and the long prompt makes recall worse.
- Nothing fixes the casual tone.

### Step 2: sort each failure

```text
Failure                         Group       First fix
Early-return fee is wrong       context     add the current fee table
Damage code is invented         context     add the code list
Replies are casual, with jokes  behaviour   change the style instructions
```

### Step 3: the good plan

```text
Plan B
Context:
- Add the current fee table (August version) and the damage-code list.
- Leave out old versions. Send only the pages that fit the question.
Instructions:
- Write in a calm, formal style. No jokes.
- Answer only from the documents provided.
- If the documents don't answer the question, say you don't know
  and offer to pass the question to the customer team.
Checks:
- Re-run the same test questions.
- Each week, a person checks a sample of fee answers.
```

### Step 4: the hard case

A customer asks: "What is the fee if I return the car three months early **and** it has a scratch?" The answer needs two documents: the fee table and the damage-code list. **This is the hard case.** If the system sends only one of them, the model has half the facts and may guess the rest. The team adds this question to the test set. It checks that both documents arrive, and that the model says what it cannot answer instead of filling the gap.

### Step 5: why it is better

- **Each failure gets the right fix.** Facts come from documents; tone comes from instructions.
- **Small, current context.** No old fee tables compete with the new one.
- **A safe way out.** "I don't know" is allowed, so the model has less reason to invent a code.
- **People still check.** The vendor docs say these steps reduce errors but do not remove them.

## Common mistakes

1. **Waiting for a better model to fix missing facts.** A later cutoff adds public knowledge, not your private rules. Fix: put the current document in the context.
2. **Fixing a tone problem with more documents, or a fact problem with style rules.** Fix: sort the failure first, then choose the fix for that group.
3. **Treating "I don't know" as the end of checking.** It lowers invented answers but does not stop them. Fix: keep spot checks on important answers.

## Try it

Not graded. Write your answer down before you read the answer.

A dental practice tests an appointment assistant. Sort each failure into context or behaviour, and give a first fix.

```text
1. It says a check-up costs 60 EUR. The practice charges 75 EUR since May.
2. Its replies are one long paragraph. Patients want a short list.
3. It names a dentist who left the practice last year.
4. Asked about parking, it invents a car park that does not exist.
```

### Answer

1. **Context.** The price is out of date. Add the current price list.
2. **Behaviour.** The format is wrong. Change the instructions: "Answer in a short bulleted list."
3. **Context.** The staff list is private and recent. Add the current staff page, and remove old ones.
4. **Context**, plus a safe way out. Add the directions page. Also allow "I don't know" when the documents have no answer, and keep checking a sample.

## Recap

- Missing, old or private facts are a context problem. Wrong tone, format or reasoning is a behaviour problem. Sort first, then fix.
- A newer model with a later knowledge cutoff does not add your private documents.
- Send the smallest set of current, useful pages, allow "I don't know", and keep checking important answers.
