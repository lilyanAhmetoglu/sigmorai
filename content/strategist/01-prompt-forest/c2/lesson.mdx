## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Add 3–5 tagged examples to a prompt, so the model copies your format and tone.
- Choose examples that are relevant, varied and in one format, including one hard case.
- Find a pattern in your examples that you did not mean to teach.

## The problem

Mara, the operations lead at the training company, gets about 300 feedback comments after each month of courses. She wants each comment sorted, so the right person sees it. Her brief is clear now:

```text
Sort each feedback comment into one topic: Trainer, Content, Venue or
Booking. Then say if someone must act: yes or no.
```

The results are uneven. One answer says `Venue, yes`. The next says `Topic: venue. Action needed.` A third writes two sentences about the room. Comments that mention two things get a different topic each time. A good brief tells the model what to do. It does not always show what a good answer looks like.

This lesson adds a second tool: show the model a few solved examples. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

A prompt with no examples is **zero-shot**. A prompt with a few examples is **few-shot**. The [Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/prompting-strategies) use these names. The model looks for the pattern in the examples and uses it on the new input. The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering) say the model "picks up" the pattern, with no need to retrain (fine-tune) it.

Our example: examples are like a finished form you give a new colleague. They copy the layout, the wording and the length, sometimes better than they follow a page of rules.

The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices) call examples "one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output format, tone, and structure". They say good examples are:

- **Relevant.** They "mirror your actual use case closely". Use real work, not made-up toy cases.
- **Diverse.** They "cover edge cases and vary enough" so the model doesn't learn **unintended patterns**: patterns you did not mean to teach. The OpenAI docs also ask for "a diverse range of possible inputs".
- **Structured.** Each one sits in `example` tags, inside `examples` tags, so the model can tell examples apart from instructions.

**How many?** The Claude docs say: "Include 3–5 examples for best results." The Gemini docs say the model can often learn from a few, but warn that with too many examples it "may start to overfit". **Overfit** means it sticks to the examples too tightly and misses the general pattern. The Gemini docs also say: "We recommend to always include few-shot examples in your prompts."

**One format.** The Gemini docs say the structure and formatting of all examples should be the same, "to avoid responses with undesired formats".

**Hidden patterns.** The model copies everything your examples share. In one Gemini example, two short sample answers made the model choose the shorter answer. That is useful when you want short answers, and a problem when you don't. The same goes for labels and tone: if every example has the same label or the same mood, that is a pattern too.

**Keep examples easy to update.** The [OpenAI prompting page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompting) suggests a "concise YAML-style or bulleted block" of examples, so your team can scan and update them. The Claude docs add that you can ask the model to check your examples for relevance and diversity, or to draft more from your first set.

Our advice: build examples from real past work your team already approved. Remove names, emails and order numbers first, because a shared prompt sends them with every request.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the zero-shot prompt

```text
Sort each feedback comment into one topic: Trainer, Content, Venue or
Booking. Then say if someone must act: yes or no.

Comment: [the live comment]
```

What goes wrong: nothing shows the exact answer shape, and nothing shows what to do when a comment mentions two topics.

### Step 2: add a rule for mixed comments

```text
If a comment has praise and a problem, choose the topic of the problem.
Answer in this form: Topic | Act
```

Better, but "this form" still leaves room. Does `Venue | Yes` count? What about `Venue | yes, the heating`?

### Step 3: add examples

```text
Sort each feedback comment into one topic: Trainer, Content, Venue or
Booking. Then say if someone must act: yes or no.
If a comment has praise and a problem, choose the topic of the problem.

<examples>
<example>
Comment: The trainer explained contract law in a way I finally understood.
Answer: Trainer | no
</example>
<example>
Comment: The handouts still show last year's tax rates.
Answer: Content | yes
</example>
<example>
Comment: My booking confirmation had the wrong date, so I almost missed day one.
Answer: Booking | yes
</example>
<example>
Comment: Brilliant session and a very patient trainer, but the room was so cold that half of us kept our coats on all afternoon.
Answer: Venue | yes
</example>
<example>
Comment: Good coffee.
Answer: Venue | no
</example>
</examples>

Comment: [the live comment]
Answer:
```

**The hard case is the fourth example.** It praises the trainer but reports a problem with the room. It is also the longest comment. It shows the mixed-comment rule in action.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **One format.** Every answer is `Topic | yes/no`, in the same case and order. The prompt ends with `Answer:`, so the model knows where to start.
- **Varied.** All four topics appear. Both `yes` and `no` appear. Comments are short and long, positive and negative.
- **Relevant.** All five are real (anonymized) comments from past courses.
- **Five examples.** Inside the 3–5 range, so the prompt stays short.
- **Tagged.** The model can tell the examples from the rules and from the live comment.

Then test it. Run the old and the new prompt on the same 30 past comments and compare.

## Common mistakes

1. **Mixed formats.** Three examples say `Venue | yes` and one says `Topic: Venue (action needed)`. The model now has two shapes to copy. Fix: give every example exactly the same structure.
2. **Toy examples.** "Comment: The course was good." teaches little, because real comments are longer and messier. Fix: use real past work, anonymized.
3. **No line between examples and the task.** The live input looks like one more example, so the model may continue the list. Fix: wrap examples in tags and put the live input after them.

## Try it

Not graded. A recruiter wants short replies to job applicants. Find two problems in these examples before you read the answer.

```text
<examples>
<example>
Applicant: Senior accountant, 12 years of experience.
Reply: Thanks, Daniel! We'd love to invite you to an interview.
</example>
<example>
Applicant: Finance director, 20 years of experience.
Reply: Thanks, Aisha! We'd love to invite you to an interview.
</example>
<example>
Applicant: Junior analyst, 2 years of experience.
Response - Dear applicant, we'd love to invite you to an interview.
</example>
</examples>
```

### Answer

1. **An unintended pattern.** Every example invites the person to an interview. The model may invite everyone, even people the team will reject. Fix: add an example of a polite "no", written in the same style.
2. **Mixed formats.** Two examples use `Reply:` and a first name. The third uses `Response -` and "Dear applicant". Fix: use one label and one greeting style in every example.

## Recap

- Few-shot means a few input and output examples in the prompt. The model follows their format, tone and structure.
- Good examples are relevant, varied and in one format. Use about 3–5, in tags, with one hard case.
- The model copies everything the examples share, including patterns you did not mean. Check what your examples have in common.
