## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Turn a one-line request into a brief with five parts: task, context and reason, audience, constraints and format.
- Lay out a prompt with a long document: document first, question last.
- Spot the words in your brief that only your team understands, and define them.

## The problem

Mara is the operations lead at a company that runs business training courses. From 1 March, clients pay a fee when they cancel a course late. She has a two-page policy document. She asks an AI assistant:

```text
Write an email about the new cancellation rules.
```

The draft is polite and fluent. It is also useless. It talks about "recent updates to our policies", gives no dates, and sounds like a bank. It does not say what clients must do. Mara rewrites it by hand, and the assistant saved her nothing.

The model did what it could. It got a one-line request and guessed the rest. This lesson shows how to write a **brief**, so the model has less to guess. The guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Brief the model like a new colleague.** The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices) say: "Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows." It is smart, but it does not know your clients, your products or your goals. If something is missing, it may guess instead of asking.

The same docs give a **golden rule**: show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context and ask them to follow it. "If they'd be confused, Claude will be too."

Our example: you would not hand a new temp a sticky note that says "email the clients". You would tell them who, why, what to say and how long. A prompt needs the same.

**The five parts of a brief.** The vendors use different words, but they cover the same ground:

1. **Task.** What you want, exactly. The [Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/prompting-strategies) call clear and specific instructions "an effective and efficient way to customize model behavior".
2. **Context and reason.** The facts the model needs, and why the task matters. The Claude docs say that explaining why an instruction matters "can help Claude better understand your goals". The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering) list context such as private data that is outside the model's training data.
3. **Audience and role.** Who reads the result. You can also give the model a role. The Claude docs say that for a role, "Even a single sentence makes a difference".
4. **Constraints.** Length, tone, what to include and what to leave out.
5. **Format.** The shape of the result: three bullets, a table, a short email. Chapter 3 goes deeper.

**Number the steps when order matters.** The Claude docs suggest numbered lists or bullet points "when the order or completeness of steps matters".

**Long document first, question last.** The Claude docs say to put long documents "near the top of your prompt, above your query". They add that a question at the end can lift quality (the docs measured "up to 30 percent"). The Gemini docs agree: "supply all the context first" and put the question "at the very end".

**Define your own words.** The Gemini docs say: "Explicitly explain any ambiguous terms or parameters." Words like "late", "active client" or "urgent" mean something exact inside your company. The model does not know that meaning.

**Ask for the extra mile.** The Claude docs say that if you want "above and beyond" behavior, ask for it. Don't hope the model infers it.

**How much detail?** The OpenAI docs compare two kinds of model. "A reasoning model is like a senior co-worker" (a model built to think through a problem before it answers): you give a goal and trust it with the details. "A GPT model is like a junior coworker" (a model that answers right away): it does best with explicit instructions. When you are not sure which one you have, a clear brief helps both.

## Worked example

### Step 1: the first prompt

```text
Write an email about the new cancellation rules.
```

What goes wrong:

- **No audience.** Is it for clients, trainers or the finance team?
- **No facts.** The model has not seen the policy, so it invents vague wording.
- **No constraints or format.** Length, tone and the call to action are left to chance.

### Step 2: the brief

Mara pastes the policy first and puts her request after it.

```text
<policy>
[two-page cancellation policy pasted here]
</policy>

Based on the policy above, write an email to our client contacts.

Who reads it: HR and training managers at client companies. They
book courses for their staff. They are busy and not legal experts.

Why: from 1 March a late cancellation costs a fee. We want clients
to know before they book their spring courses, so nobody is
surprised by an invoice.

Definition: "late cancellation" means less than 10 working days
before the course starts. Moving a place to a colleague is not a
cancellation.

Steps:
1. Say what changes and from which date.
2. Give the fee and the 10-day rule in one short paragraph.
3. Say what clients can do instead of cancelling.
4. End with one link to the FAQ page.

Tone: friendly and direct, no legal language.
Length: under 150 words. Subject line plus four short paragraphs.
If you think an important question is not answered by the policy,
list it after the email so I can check it.
```

### Step 3: the hard case

**The hard case is the word "late".** Inside the company, everyone knows it means "less than 10 working days". The policy uses the word 14 times but defines it only in a footnote. Without the definition line, the first draft said "a few days before", which is wrong and would cause disputes. With it, the email states the rule exactly.

### Step 4: why it is better

- **All five parts are there.** Task, context and reason, audience, constraints and format.
- **Document first.** The two pages come before the question, as both vendors suggest.
- **Steps are numbered**, because the order of the email matters.
- **The company word is defined**, so the model doesn't guess.
- **The extra mile is asked for.** The list of open questions is extra work Mara wants, so she says so.

Our advice: keep the brief. Next quarter, when the policy changes again, she edits two lines instead of starting over.

## Common mistakes

1. **The question above a long document.** The model reads pages of text after your request. Fix: paste the document first, then ask, starting with "Based on the document above…".
2. **Fixing the answer instead of the brief.** You edit the draft by hand and send the same prompt next week. Fix: ask what the brief left out, add it, and try again. The Gemini docs call prompting "iterative".
3. **Hoping for extra effort.** You wanted a checklist at the end, but you never asked. Fix: write down every extra you expect.

## Try it

Not graded. A sales manager sends this prompt. Name three parts of a brief that are missing, then rewrite it.

```text
Write a LinkedIn post about our new course.
```

### Answer

Missing parts: **context** (which course, what is new, why now), **audience** (who should read the post), **constraints** (length, tone, what not to promise) and **format** (one paragraph, hashtags or not, a link). A better brief:

```text
Write a LinkedIn post for our company page.
Course: "Negotiation for buyers", a new two-day course, first date
in April. Why now: many clients asked for a buyer-side version.
Audience: procurement managers at mid-size companies.
Tone: confident and plain. No claims about results we can't prove.
Format: 3 short paragraphs, under 120 words, one link at the end,
no more than 2 hashtags.
```

## Recap

- Brief the model the way you would brief a new hire who knows nothing about your team. If a colleague would be confused, so will the model.
- A brief has five parts: task, context and reason, audience, constraints and format. Number the steps when order matters.
- Put long documents first and the question last. Define the words only your company uses, and ask for any extra you want.
