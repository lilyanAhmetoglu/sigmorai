## Goal

After this lesson you can:

- Weigh capability, speed and cost for one task, and pick a starting model.
- Run a small test with your own examples and choose the lightest option that passes.
- Keep the choice stable when vendors release or retire models.

## The problem

A finance team receives about 400 supplier invoices a day as PDFs. They want AI to pull out the supplier, the invoice number and the amount. Results are needed by the end of the day, not in seconds.

Every vendor offers several models. Some are stronger and slower. Some are faster and cheaper. There is no single best model. There is a best model **for one task**, at your quality bar and your budget. This lesson gives a way to choose that still works when the model names change. Model names and settings change often; the guidance below is from each vendor's docs (October 2026).

## The idea

**Weigh three things first.** The [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/choosing-a-model) list the factors to evaluate first:

- **Capabilities.** What must the model be able to do? For example, read scanned documents.
- **Speed.** How fast must it answer? A live chat needs replies in seconds. A nightly report can wait.
- **Cost.** What's the budget, both for testing and for daily use?

The same list adds a fourth, **effort**, covered below. The [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-selection) ask similar questions: "How often does your workflow run?" and "How quickly do you need the result?"

**Two ways to start.** The Claude guide describes both:

- **Efficiency-first.** Begin with a quick, cheaper model. Test it, and upgrade "only if necessary". It suits prototypes, tight speed needs, cost-sensitive work and "high-volume, straightforward tasks".
- **Capability-first.** Start with a strong model, get the quality right, then try lighter options "down the line". It suits complex reasoning, nuanced understanding, and work "where accuracy outweighs cost considerations".

The OpenAI guide lands in the same place: compare options on the same inputs, then keep the lightest setting that is good enough for your quality bar.

**Settings matter, not only the model.** Many models have a setting for how hard they think. Anthropic calls it **effort**; OpenAI calls it **reasoning effort**. The Claude guide says: "Tuning effort is often a better lever than switching models." It trades quality for speed and cost inside one model.

**Test with your own work.** A benchmark on a vendor's page is not your task. The Claude guide says "having a good evaluation set is the most important step". An **evaluation set** is a fixed list of real examples with known right answers.

**Keep it stable.** Vendors update and retire models. The [OpenAI text guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/text) recommends "Pinning your production applications to specific model snapshots" (fixed versions) "to ensure consistent behavior", plus tests to re-run when you upgrade. The [Claude model table](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/overview) lists a retirement date for each model.

Our example: choosing a model is like hiring for one role. You write the job needs, try a few candidates on real work, and hire the one who meets the bar at the right salary, not the most famous name.

## Worked example

### Step 1: write the needs

```text
Task:          pull supplier, invoice number and amount from PDFs
Capabilities:  read scanned invoices; no long reasoning
Speed:         same day is fine
Cost:          400 a day, every day, so the price adds up
```

High volume and a straightforward task point to **efficiency-first**.

### Step 2: build the test set

The team takes 25 past invoices where they already know the right values. They set the bar: at least 24 of 25 fully correct.

### Step 3: run the candidates

```text
Option                       Correct   Cost
Light model, low effort      21 / 25   lowest
Light model, high effort     24 / 25   low
Strong model, default        25 / 25   about 5x the light model
```

The prices and scores are made up for this example.

### Step 4: the hard case

The light model at low effort failed on the handwritten invoices. **This is the hard case.** Before switching to the strong model, the team raised the effort setting on the same light model. That fixed three of the four failures. The one left is a smudged scan that people also struggle to read, so it goes to a person.

### Step 5: the decision memo

```text
Choice: light model, high effort, pinned to version [x].
Why: 24/25 meets our bar at a low cost. The strong model
scored 25/25 at about 5x the cost.
Hard cases: invoices the model can't read go to the finance desk.
Upkeep: keep the 25-invoice test set. When the vendor releases
or retires a model, re-run the test before we switch.
```

### Step 6: why it is better

- **Fits the task.** The needs came first, so the team didn't pay for power it doesn't use.
- **Tested on their own invoices**, not on a public leaderboard.
- **Effort before switching.** A cheaper fix was tried first.
- **Stable.** A pinned version and a saved test set make the next change safe.

## Common mistakes

1. **Picking the newest, biggest model by default.** It may cost far more for no gain on your task. Fix: write the needs first and test a lighter option.
2. **Judging by a few nice-looking outputs.** A handful of tries hides the hard cases. Fix: score a fixed set of real examples with known answers.
3. **Letting the model change under you.** An automatic upgrade can change results overnight. Fix: pin a version and re-run your tests before you switch.

## Try it

Not graded. For each task, choose efficiency-first or capability-first, and say why.

```text
A. Tag 10,000 product reviews a day as positive, negative or mixed.
B. Draft the four-page commentary for the quarterly board pack.
   It runs four times a year and must be accurate.
```

Then: the vendor releases a new model next month. What should the team do before using it for task A?

### Answer

- **A: efficiency-first.** High volume, a simple task, and cost adds up every day. Start light and move up only if the tests fail.
- **B: capability-first.** It runs rarely, so cost barely matters, and accuracy matters most. Start strong, then try lighter options later.
- **New model:** run the saved test set on it and compare with the current, pinned version. Switch only if it meets the bar.

## Recap

- Choose for the task: capability, speed and cost. Start cheap and move up, or start strong and move down.
- Try a higher or lower effort setting before you change models.
- Decide with your own test examples, keep the lightest option that passes, and pin the version.
